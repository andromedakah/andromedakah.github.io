# 🗓️ AI Tech Radar — The Millstream

**Thursday, 5 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering**

## 1 · Executive Summary (90-second read)

For a long year the radar worked on the *servant* — name it (the livery), keep one book (the steward), put your name on the bond (the surety), check its wage (the second wage), send it to reap (the two harvests), fund it (the purse). Then the frame dropped a level: the model commoditised in public and the lesson became *own the ground — don't let the stove-seller pour your foundation* (the foundation). **This morning the ground itself is laid bare, and it turns out the foundation is not code. It is power, capital and twenty-year supply contracts.** Two acts in a fortnight make the shift impossible to miss, and the model layer's continued shrug is the third.

**The act — the frontier labs stopped selling a model and started mortgaging a millstream.** On **4 November 2026 TeraWulf disclosed that Google will provide credit support for Anthropic's data-centre project** at Hawesville, Kentucky: a greater house co-signing the water-rent. The underlying lease (signed July 2026, via TeraWulf's Raylan Data subsidiary) is **20 years, ~401 MW of critical IT load across three buildings, ~$19B of contracted revenue, with first capacity expected only in the second half of 2027** (Data Center Knowledge; Securities.io; Yahoo Finance, as reported). Days earlier, on **26 October 2026, Anthropic filed its S-1** to go public on Nasdaq as **ANT**, disclosing **~$16.29B of H1-2026 revenue (up from ~$1.16B a year earlier, ~+1,304%), an $80.7B net loss of which ~$72.4B is a non-cash mark on convertible notes, ~85% gross margin, and an 80/20 split between consumption and subscription revenue** (CNBC; Yahoo Finance; The VC Corner; the S-1 on EDGAR, as reported). The broader market is being **"pre-sold at gigawatt scale"** (Data Center Knowledge headline): J.P. Morgan puts 2026 hyperscaler capex near **$697B**, and Morgan Stanley judges that **hyperscalers can no longer self-finance the data centres they need** (as reported).

**The shrug — the model, meanwhile, keeps commoditising, and nobody gasps.** In the same window Google shipped **Gemini 4 Carbon** (28 Oct, limited to select users), OpenAI countered with a **cheaper, immediately-available GPT-6.1 Terra**, Tencent opened its **Hy4 API at a reported $0.42 in / $1.25 out per million tokens with a ~1.05M-token context**, and Flower launched **Endeavor 1.1** pitched on "owning your outputs" with UK-only inference (as reported; aggregator sources disagree on some names and dates, so treat the specifics as unverified). A fleet of new frontier and near-frontier models in a fortnight — and the reaction was a shrug, exactly as it was for the 40%-bigger Grok 4.7 in September. **When intelligence this capable arrives this often at these prices and barely moves the needle, the miller's skill is a commodity. The scarce, expensive, financed thing is the millstream that turns the wheel.**

**The stakes — your supplier is now a capital-and-power counterparty, so diligence it like one.** The lock-in has moved one more floor down: past the model (a commodity) and past the proprietary software floor (the "foundation" of the last edition) to the **physical and financial substrate** — gigawatts of contracted power, twenty-year leases, convertible-note structures, hyperscaler credit backstops, and IPO balance sheets. This is good news and bad news at once. **Good:** the thing you actually buy (inference) is getting cheaper as models commoditise. **Bad:** the supplier you buy it from is now an $80-billion-paper-loss, capital-hungry, power-constrained enterprise whose price and availability are set by electricity markets and capital markets, not software economics — and whose first new capacity for some of these deals does not arrive until **H2 2027**. The board move: **read the balance sheet, not just the benchmark; map the water under the model; and keep a second stream, not merely a second model.**

## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Millstream"

*Topic: This morning the frame drops one floor beneath the foundation, to the water and the capital that turn every wheel. On 4 November 2026 TeraWulf disclosed that Google will credit-backstop Anthropic's ~$19B, ~401 MW, 20-year data-centre lease at Hawesville, Kentucky (first capacity H2 2027), days after Anthropic's 26 October S-1 revealed ~$16.29B of H1-2026 revenue (up ~1,304%), an $80.7B net loss (~$72.4B non-cash) and a plan to list on Nasdaq as ANT (Data Center Knowledge; CNBC; Yahoo Finance, as reported). In the same fortnight a fleet of new models — Gemini 4 Carbon, GPT-6.1 Terra, Tencent Hy4 at ~$0.42/$1.25, Flower Endeavor 1.1 — arrived to a shrug (as reported), the surest sign that the miller's craft has commoditised while the millstream has not. The millstream allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There was a valley full of mills, and for a season every house judged a mill by its miller. A clever miller ground finer flour and faster, and the houses bid for the cleverest, and paid dear. The radar's own house had learned, hard, two earlier truths about this: that a miller could be hired cheaply and swapped in an afternoon, so you should never fall in love with one; and that the real danger was letting the man who rented you the cheap miller quietly pour the foundation of your mill-house, so you could never leave. The house had done its homework. It owned its floor. It kept its own larder. It thought it understood the ground.

Then, this fortnight, the great mills did something the house had not reckoned on. They stopped boasting about their millers at all. One great mill went down to the money-changers in the city square and offered to **sell shares in the mill itself** — opened its books to the whole town, and the books showed a torrent of custom (revenue grown thirteen-fold in a year) sitting beside a loss so vast it took the breath away, most of it an accountant's mark rather than coin truly lost, but a number that announced, plainly: *this is no longer a workshop; this is a great enterprise that lives and dies by capital.* And in the same days another great house signed a **twenty-year lease on the millstream** — the river, the weir, the head of water that turns every wheel in the valley — and, because even a great house could no longer pay for so much water alone, a *greater* house put its name to the water-rent as surety. The new wheels this water would turn were not even built yet; the first of them would not run until the summer after next.

And the valley's millers? They had never been better, nor cheaper, nor more plentiful. That very fortnight four new millers arrived, each as fine as the last season's best, one grinding at a fraction of the old toll — and the houses barely looked up. A miller half again as skilled drew a shrug, because milling skill had become a commodity, as common and cheap as it had always secretly been dear. **Here was the whole lesson in one glance:** the thing everyone had competed over — the miller — had become a thing you could get anywhere for pennies; and the thing nobody had thought to compete over — **the water** — had quietly become the only thing that mattered, and it was being bought up, twenty years at a time, with capital the ordinary house could not dream of raising.

So the house asked the colder question it had never asked before. *Not "which miller is best?" and not even "do we own our floor?" but: "whose water do we actually grind on — who paid for the weir, who co-signed the rent, what do their books look like, and what becomes of our flour if that stream runs low, or doubles its price, or the wheels we were promised are not built until the summer after next?"* And the house saw, with a start, that it had been performing diligence on the wrong thing entirely. It had been reading the miller's testimonials — the benchmarks, the token prices — when it should have been reading the mill-owner's ledger and the deed to the river. Its supplier was not a workshop it could out-hire. It was a capital-and-water enterprise, and a house that depends on such a supplier is exposed to **capital markets and electricity markets**, not to software.

Two roads, as always. The easy road was to keep shopping millers — there were four new ones this fortnight, after all, and one was gloriously cheap — and to feel modern and thrifty doing it. The wiser road, which the careful houses took, had three plain parts. **First, read the ledger, not just the testimonials:** before you lean your whole harvest on a mill, read its books the way you would a bank's — its losses, its debts, its backers, the leases it has signed and whose name co-signs them — because a supplier that must raise capital to keep the water flowing can change your price, or your terms, or its very existence, by an act in a market you do not watch. **Second, trace the water:** know, for each mill you depend on, not just the miller but the stream beneath — which river, whose weir, which greater house holds the rent, how much water is contracted and for how long, and when the new wheels actually turn. A model is swappable in an afternoon; a twenty-year lease on four hundred megawatts, first running in 2027, is the real shape of your supply. **And third, keep a second stream, not merely a second miller:** a fallback that draws from the *same* river — the same power market, the same data-centre campus, the same capital backer — is no fallback at all. True redundancy now means a second supply with a different source of water and a different balance sheet behind it.

Here is the part that should keep a master awake. The miller market was loud, cheap, and wonderful — four new millers in a fortnight, each finer than the last — and all that glorious noise was, again, a kindly distraction. **While every house happily compared millers, the great mills were quietly buying the rivers**, twenty years at a time, and raising the capital to do it from money-changers and greater houses — for whoever holds the water holds the valley, and whoever can finance twenty years of water holds the water. One day the house that merely shopped millers would go to grind its harvest and find the stream already spoken for, the price set by a market it never watched, the new wheels still a year from turning. The lesson was not that the great mills were wicked. The lesson was that **the competition had moved from a thing you can judge by tasting the flour to a thing you can only judge by reading a ledger and a water-deed — and most houses were still tasting flour.**

**The moral, stated plainly:** the model is a miller, now so cheap and plentiful that a finer one draws a shrug; the millstream is the power and the capital that turn every wheel; your knowledge is the larder; the floor is the ground you own; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising servant eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; the foundation is why you own the ground the house stands on — and **the millstream is why, having owned your floor, you must now look *beneath* it, at the water and the capital that actually turn the wheel, and diligence your supplier as the capital-and-power enterprise it has become: read its ledger, trace its water, and keep a second stream fed by a different river, so that when the price of water moves — and it will, in a market you do not control — your harvest still gets milled.** A house that leans its whole crop on a mill whose books it has never read, whose river it cannot name, and whose only fallback draws from the same stream has not been thrifty. It has bet the harvest on another man's balance sheet and another man's weir, and called it a supply chain.

**The question it forces:** *We can swap the miller in an afternoon — four new ones arrived this fortnight to a shrug. But do we know whose water we grind on? Have we read our AI supplier's ledger the way we would a bank's — its losses, its debts, its backers, the twenty-year leases it has signed and whose name co-signs the rent? Have we traced each critical capability down to the river beneath it — which grid, whose data centre, which capital backer, and when the promised capacity actually turns on? And is our fallback a genuinely different stream, or the same river wearing a second name?*

## 3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

### 🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
- **Have we been doing diligence on the miller when we should be reading the mill-owner's ledger?** Our AI supplier just became a capital-markets entity — an S-1 showing ~$16.29B of revenue against an $80.7B net loss, and 20-year, gigawatt-scale power leases co-signed by hyperscalers. **For each AI capability we depend on, have we assessed the supplier's funding, capital structure and power contracts as rigorously as we assess its benchmarks — and named our exposure if that supplier must raise capital or ration power to keep running?**
- **Whose water are we grinding on, and when does it actually turn on?** Some of the capacity now being contracted — e.g. the Hawesville lease — does not come online until H2 2027. **Do we know, for each critical AI workload, which region, grid, data-centre campus and capital backer sits beneath it, how much capacity is contracted and for how long, and whether our growth assumptions depend on supply that has not been built yet?**
- **Is our fallback a second stream or the same river renamed?** A multi-model strategy that runs every model inside the same hyperscaler region, on the same power market, behind the same capital backer is not redundancy. **Can we point to a genuinely independent second supply — different infrastructure, different balance sheet — and have we costed what a price or availability shock in the dominant stream would do to us?**

### 🏦 Financial Services
- You diligence counterparties for a living, and your AI vendor is now one. An $80.7B net loss (mostly non-cash, but headline-defining), convertible-note structures, and hyperscaler credit backstops are exactly the artefacts your credit team reads. **Can we produce a counterparty-risk assessment of our principal AI suppliers — funding runway, capital structure, concentration of their own backers — alongside the usual model-performance review, and does our board see both?**
- Concentration risk now runs down into power and capital, not just compute. **Have we mapped whether our AI stack, our cloud, and our critical SaaS all ultimately draw on the same handful of hyperscalers and the same constrained power markets — and can we evidence, for a regulator, a tested exit that does not simply move us to another tap on the same pipe?**

### 🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
- Clinical and regulatory workloads need continuity measured in years, and some AI capacity is being pre-sold years out (first Hawesville capacity: H2 2027). **Are our long-horizon AI commitments matched to supply that actually exists today, or are we depending on capacity and funding that a vendor still has to build and finance — and what is our plan if that build slips?**
- Patient data plus a capital-hungry supplier is a specific risk. **If our principal AI vendor hit a funding or power constraint, would our validated clinical workflows degrade gracefully on a genuinely separate supply, or are our data gravity and validation effectively a bet on one capital-and-power enterprise staying solvent and powered?**

### 🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
- You already run AI the way you run energy and capex — long contracts, dual-sourcing, supply-chain mapping. AI *is* now an energy-and-capex business: the inference you buy sits downstream of gigawatts and 20-year leases. **Have we applied our existing energy-procurement and supplier-qualification discipline to AI — tracing it to the grid and the campus, and second-sourcing across different power markets, not just different models?**
- Capacity that turns on in 2027 is a scheduling input you understand. **Do our AI-dependent production and automation roadmaps account for when contracted AI capacity actually comes online, and do we hold a buffer or alternate for the gap?**

### 🛒 Retail / Consumer
- Your margins ride on cheap inference — and the good news is that models keep commoditising (Tencent Hy4 at a reported $0.42/$1.25; a cheaper GPT-6.1 Terra). **Are we actively capturing that commoditisation dividend by routing per task across cheap, swappable models — while making sure the capacity behind them is genuinely diversified, not all pre-sold to the same campus?**
- Peak-season capacity is being "pre-sold at gigawatt scale." **If demand spikes on our biggest day, is our AI capacity contracted and reserved across more than one stream, or are we assuming spot availability from a supply curve that is already committed years out?**

### 🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
- Sovereignty now means sovereignty of *power and capital*, not just data. Some of these deals are tied to specific jurisdictions and grids (Hawesville, Kentucky; Flower's UK-only inference, as reported). **Are we procuring AI with explicit attention to where the compute, the power and the capital backer physically sit, and whether that aligns with our jurisdictional and continuity obligations?**
- Continuity of public service over a decade cannot rest on one capital-hungry supplier. **Can we show that no critical public service depends on a single AI vendor's solvency, power supply and build schedule — and that we hold a genuinely independent alternate stream, procured for the long horizon?**

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Foundation to the Millstream

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking *is this agent named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping and funded?* and even *do we own the floor it stands on?* and started asking the one question beneath all of those: *what is the floor made of, who financed it, and when does the supply actually turn on?* The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the census counts it; the keyring is which key it holds; the provenance traces the work; the errand bounds it; the bell discloses when it slips; the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests and purse govern the servant; the foundation says own the ground. **Every one of those stops at software. The millstream is about the physical and financial substrate under the software — and this fortnight the market made the distinction concrete: the frontier labs raised public capital (Anthropic's S-1) and signed gigawatt-scale, decade-plus power leases backstopped by hyperscalers (Google–Anthropic–TeraWulf), in the same window that a fleet of new models commoditised to a shrug.** The point is blunt: **the thing that has commoditised (the model) is not the thing that now constrains you (power and capital), and a house that diligences the model while ignoring the ledger and the grid is reading the wrong document entirely.**

- **The act — the moat moved from model to millstream (power + capital + long-term supply).** **Google will credit-backstop Anthropic's lease at Hawesville, KY (disclosed 4 Nov): 20 years, ~401 MW across three buildings, ~$19B contracted, first capacity H2 2027, via TeraWulf's Raylan Data; TeraWulf's separate Muskie campus expanded to ~1 GW. Anthropic's 26 Oct S-1: ~$16.29B H1-2026 revenue (+~1,304% YoY), $80.7B net loss (~$72.4B non-cash mark on convertible notes), ~85% gross margin, 80/20 consumption/subscription, Nasdaq ticker ANT (Data Center Knowledge; Securities.io; CNBC; Yahoo Finance; the S-1, as reported).** Three engineering-economic consequences follow. First, **your supplier's unit economics are now power- and capital-bound, not software-bound** — a workload's price and availability track electricity and financing markets, which move on timescales and for reasons unrelated to your usage. Second, **supply is being committed years out** — "pre-sold at gigawatt scale," with some capacity not live until 2027 — so capacity planning must look at contracted build schedules, not spot availability. Third, **vendor due diligence must now include the balance sheet** — a supplier carrying a vast paper loss and raising public capital is a different counterparty from a profitable SaaS, and should be assessed as one.
- **The shrug — the model layer keeps commoditising, confirming where the moat is not.** **A fortnight of frontier/near-frontier releases — Gemini 4 Carbon (28 Oct), a cheaper/faster GPT-6.1 Terra, Tencent Hy4 (~$0.42 in / $1.25 out per M tokens, ~1.05M context), Flower Endeavor 1.1 ("own your outputs," UK-only inference) — met with a shrug (as reported; names/dates vary across aggregators, treat as unverified).** Two realities for architecture. **First, capability parity keeps arriving at ever-lower prices**, so the rational posture remains: treat models as interchangeable components routed per task behind an interface you own. **Second — and this is the new part — cheap, abundant models make the *physical* substrate the binding constraint.** When the model is free-ish and plentiful, the question that decides whether you can actually run your workload at scale, next year, at a predictable price, is whether the *power and capacity* exist and whom they belong to. The commoditisation of the miller is precisely what makes the millstream decisive.
- **The answer — diligence the counterparty; map the substrate; diversify the stream.** **Read the supplier's filings and power contracts as part of vendor selection (funding, losses, backers, leases); trace each critical capability down to its physical substrate (region, grid, data-centre campus, capital backer, contracted capacity, on-line date); and hold a genuinely independent second supply — different infrastructure and different balance sheet, not the same hyperscaler region wearing a second model's name.** This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — *be able to account for, and act on, what you run* — now driven down to the substrate: not what the agent is, does, serves, spends, or even what floor it stands on, but **what that floor is made of, who financed it, and whether the water will still be flowing, at a price you can bear, when you need to grind.**

The strategic core: **the model commoditising is the very thing that promotes power and capital to the role of moat — and of risk.** Cheap, abundant intelligence is good news you collect by routing ruthlessly; but the supplier you route to is now a capital-and-power enterprise whose solvency, financing and electricity contracts determine your supply. The board translation: **shop millers ruthlessly; but before you lean your harvest on a mill, read its ledger and trace its water.** The failure mode is exact: **a house that governs, funds and perfects its agents, and even owns its software floor, but leans its whole AI operation on one capital-hungry, power-constrained supplier whose books it has never read and whose river it cannot name — will pass every governance review and discover, the first time capital or power tightens, that its fallback drew from the same stream and there is no flour today.**

### Why "the millstream" is the discipline that matters now

For a long year the worry was whether the servant was governed; last edition it was whether you owned the floor. **This morning the market dropped the question beneath the floor: what is it made of, and who financed it?** Anthropic's S-1 and the Google-backstopped Hawesville lease prove the labs now compete — and are exposed — on capital and power, not model craft; the fortnight's fleet of shrugged-at models proves the craft has commoditised. Governance that stops at software, and even vendor-strategy that stops at "own the floor," will not tell you whether your supplier can stay solvent and powered, or whether the capacity you are counting on exists yet. The estates that keep grinding will diligence the substrate: the ledger, the grid, the lease, the backer, the on-line date.

### How it lands on legacy estates
