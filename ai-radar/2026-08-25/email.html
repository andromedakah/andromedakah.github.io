The Recipe

Tuesday, 25 August 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

Yesterday the radar named the gate every agent must pass. Today it asks what behind it is worth defending — and this week the market answered bluntly: not the model. Forbes declared on 24 August that "enterprise AI vendor loyalty is fading fast"; Ramp shows Anthropic at 43.5% of US-business AI adoption vs OpenAI's 39.7%, with more than half of firms paying both and swapping each release. A brain you rent at the same price as your rival is not a moat. So where did it go? To the one asset a competitor cannot rent: your governed proprietary knowledge. The proof: Pinecone's Nexus knowledge engine reached GA, and an agent using it as its knowledge layer topped Sierra's τ-Knowledge benchmark at 47.4%, beating the best frontier model (GPT-5.5, 46.4%) at >90% lower token cost. A cheaper agent that knows your business beat a pricier genius that doesn't.

1 · Executive Summary

For a week this radar walked the value up the stack; today it names what is worth putting behind the gate, because this week the market said out loud what the radar has argued all along: the model is a commodity you rent and swap, and the durable advantage is your governed proprietary knowledge. The shift is no longer "can an agent act?" or "through what door does it pass?" but "when the brain is rented and interchangeable, what do we own that makes our agent worth more than a competitor's running the identical model?"

1. The stove is rented — and everyone rents the same one. On 24 August, Forbes (Ron Schmelzer) declared "Enterprise AI Vendor Loyalty Is Fading Fast." Ramp's data shows Anthropic at 43.5% of US-business AI adoption vs OpenAI's 39.7% in July (Anthropic first passed OpenAI in April), and more than half of firms paying either lab now pay both, re-weighting spend within months of each release. A model you rent at commodity price and swap at will is not a defensible asset.

2. The larder and the recipe win — governed knowledge beat the frontier. Pinecone brought its Nexus "knowledge engine" to GA — it turns proprietary data and workflows into governed, agent-ready knowledge served in one call. On Sierra's τ-Knowledge benchmark, an agent using Nexus as its knowledge layer scored 47.4% — top of the board, beating the best frontier model, GPT-5.5, at 46.4% — while cutting token cost >90% vs agentic RAG, answering up to 30× faster, at >90% accuracy. Knowledge, not model horsepower, drove the outcome.

3. But only if it is governed — the pantry and the tab are still open. VentureBeat finds 21% of enterprises cannot halt a runaway agent's spend in real time, 49% name shadow AI — agentic pipelines on corporate cards outside oversight — their worst control failure, 25% already hit by an "infinite-loop" agent bill, and only 38% have a central governance team. The EU AI Act's Article 12/50 duties (live since 2 Aug, fines up to €15M/3%) make the ledger a legal floor.

Bottom line: the engine is a commodity you rent, and this week the market proved it by swapping vendors like stoves — so the moat is the one thing a competitor cannot rent: your governed proprietary knowledge, curated and served so your agent beats a frontier model, and guarded so no one raids the pantry or runs up the tab. Stop chasing the hotter stove and start building the larder and the recipe: turn your proprietary data into governed, agent-ready knowledge, feed it to the agent instead of a bigger model, and put identity, cost-metering and audit around it.

2 · Allegory — The Recipe

A town famous for cooking competed for a century on one thing: the stove. Then the foundries learned to cast a superb stove cheaply, a better one every season, and to rent it by the month — and within a year every kitchen cooked on the same rented range, swapping the moment a hotter one arrived. The thing they had competed on became a commodity everyone rented at the same price. A dish cooked on a rented stove tastes like every other dish on the same rented stove; you cannot build a name on what your rival rents too. The advantage had moved to the two things the foundries could never cast: the larder (ingredients grown on the house's own land — its data) and the recipe (its way of choosing, preparing and serving them — its governed knowledge).

A cook-off settled it: a celebrated chef with the finest rented stove and a bare pantry lost to a modest cook with a plain stove and a well-kept larder and recipe — on the plate, and at a fraction of the fuel, because her larder was already prepared and her recipe already knew the way. But some kitchens heard "the larder is the advantage" and lost it anyway — pantry door open, shelves unlabeled, line cooks charging supplies to the house with no one totting the bill, a runaway apprentice burning a season's coal overnight. An unkept larder is not a moat; it is a liability with a lock no one turns.

The question it forces: we are still paying to rent a stove a half-degree hotter than the kitchen across the square — which rents the same stove next month. Have we built the larder and the recipe only we own — our proprietary data, curated and governed so our agent beats a frontier model — and are we keeping the pantry (door watched, shelves inventoried, tab metered, every dish logged)? Or have we spent the season polishing a rented stove?

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Reclassify the model as "rent," and stop over-paying. Make your model layer swappable behind an internal interface, benchmark two or three vendors on your own tasks, and cap what you pay for a marginal frontier upgrade you can't show moves your business. Change the engine without changing your strategy.

2. Build the larder — governed proprietary knowledge. Pick your highest-value agent use case and stand up a governed, agent-ready knowledge layer (Nexus-class or built) instead of grinding raw data every query — the move that cut token cost >90% and beat a frontier model. Then measure: does your agent, on your knowledge, beat a bare frontier model on your task?

3. Keep the pantry — govern it and meter the tab. Put identity on who may reach each knowledge source, provenance on what's in it, a real-time cost cap on agent spend (21% still can't stop a runaway bill), and Article-12-grade logs. Hunt shadow larders (49% call them their worst failure). Report two numbers to the board: share of agents on governed knowledge, and share of agent spend under a real-time cap.
