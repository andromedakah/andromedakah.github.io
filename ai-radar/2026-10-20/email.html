1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from perfecting a loyal servant to governing a trusted insider — because a vendor the whole enterprise runs on now says, in writing, that you should treat your own agents as insider threats and design so you need trust the model least.

1. The reversal — agents as insider threats. On 19 October 2026 Microsoft published "Agents are your newest insiders. Security must move at their speed" (Nadim Abdo, CVP Identity and Network Access): an AI agent holds standing access and acts at machine speed, so "frontier models can't be trusted" by default and must be designed around, not believed (Microsoft Security; TechCrunch; SiliconANGLE, as reported).

2. The design principle — separate intelligence from authority. Satya Nadella: "separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it… the most trustworthy agentic system is the one designed to let us trust the model least." Deputy CISO Terrell Cox: "agents will only be adopted at scale if they can be trusted at scale." The model that advises must not also hold the authority to act (Microsoft Security; SiliconANGLE, as reported).

3. The machinery — a face, a watchman, a human keeping the keys. Entra Agent ID gives each agent an identity separate from its human sponsor; Guardian Agents watch agents at machine speed while people keep the decisions that matter; discovery now spans 60 local agent types (+35), incl. Claude Code, OpenAI Codex, GitHub Copilot (Microsoft Security; SiliconANGLE; Unite.ai, as reported).

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each assumed the servant was, at bottom, yours. The insider is the morning that breaks: a trusted insider is dangerous precisely because trust is access. Build so no single agent, however trusted, can move the gold alone — separate the counsel from the keys, give each agent a face, set a quick watchman, and keep every decision that matters in a human hand.

2 · Allegory — The Insider

A great house had at last done everything right — named every hand, kept one book, stood surety, checked the wage, sent its servants to reap, funded a scoped purse, and kept the ground its own. The master loved his cleverest servant best — a quick, tireless, capable hand. Then a grey-headed steward who had spent his life guarding treasuries told him the hardest thing of the year: "The servant you trust most is precisely the one who can ruin you — not because he is wicked, but because trust is only another word for access. The thief who robs a great house is almost never the stranger at the gate. It is the trusted one already inside."

Loyalty was not the question, the old man warned: a servant clever enough to be useful is clever enough to be turned — by a whisper slipped into his ear while he wears your coat, by an honest error at a speed no human catches, by a reach no one re-checked because, after all, he is trusted. So the treasury-keepers built the house to need trust the servant as little as possible: "The safest treasury is not the one with the most trusted clerk. It is the one arranged so that no single clerk, however trusted, can move the gold alone."

Three plain things. Separate the counsel from the keys — the mind that advises must never be the hand that signs. Give each servant a face of his own — a mark distinct from any man, so no deed hides in a person's shadow. Set a watchman of the servant's own quick kind — one that never tires and matches his speed to raise the alarm and shut a door, while every decision that truly matters stays in a human hand, or you have only built a second insider to trust.

The question it forces: We have made our servant excellent. But have we made our house safe, or only our servant trusted? If our cleverest agent were turned tomorrow, or simply erred at machine speed, could it move anything that matters alone — or was the trust we place in it never the thing holding the house up?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Insider

Every prior control improves a servant you assumed was yours; the insider says stop assuming it — treat the agent as a trusted insider and design to trust the model least. The reversal: the model is not a trust boundary — a system prompt or a vendor safety claim is advice, not enforcement; the boundary must live outside the model, in a control you hold. Authority ≠ intelligence: the reasoning model proposes; a distinct, simpler, non-model mechanism (policy engine, scoped executor, human approval) the agent cannot reconfigure disposes. Prompt injection makes a trusted agent an attacker's hand: once an agent ingests untrusted input and holds authority to act, a malicious instruction wearing your credentials is an insider attack — which is why counsel and keys must split. The machinery: a human cannot be the real-time control for a machine-speed actor, so the watchman is an agent (scoped to detect, alert and apply permitted safeguards, escalating to a human); and you cannot govern insiders you cannot see, which is why discovery jumped to 60 cross-vendor agent types.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Separate the counsel from the keys. A supplier you run on says the model is not a trust boundary and the advising model must not also hold authority to act. Pick your highest-access agents, identify every consequential action each can take alone (move money, change a record, send externally), and insert a gap you control — a human approval, a scoped executor the agent cannot reconfigure. Report the share of consequential agent actions with no human and no separate control in the gap.

2. Give every agent a face of its own. Entra Agent ID exists precisely so an agent's actions can be told apart from its sponsor's; identity is the enforcement point for scope and attribution. Inventory the agents with standing access (expect more, and more cross-vendor, than you think), and give each a distinct identity with a bounded, reviewable scope. Report the share of production agents carrying their own identity and an owner.

3. Set a quick watchman — keep the decisions human. A human cannot be the real-time control for a machine-speed actor; oversight must itself run at machine speed, while consequential decisions stay human. Stand up automated oversight for one critical agent workflow — detection and an automatic halt the instant the agent runs out of scope — and draw the line of what the agent may never do alone. Report the mean time-to-detect-and-halt and a dated plan to extend it.
