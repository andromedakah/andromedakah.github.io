## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Herald's Proclamation"

*Topic: This morning OpenAI launched GPT-6 Astra, and its president Greg Brockman called it "the start of AGI," welcoming "the AGI era" (Fortune, Bloomberg, TechCrunch). The model is agentic and computer-using — reported strong on computer-use benchmarks (~72.6% on OSWorld 2.0, as reported via secondary explainers) — and it arrived "powerful and controversial," shipped with added cyber guardrails. The board-level catch is the ledger the proclamation does not change: McKinsey's State of AI 2026 finds ~80% of firms report productivity gains but only ~37% any EBIT impact, ~93% of enterprise AI teams over budget, and ~60% of agentic-AI cost on "response refinement"; PwC's survey of 4,454 CEOs finds only ~12% booked both cost and revenue benefits while ~56% saw no significant benefit. And the proclamation lands on a live regime: EU AI Act enforcement over general-purpose models has run since 2 August, with systemic-risk duties biting exactly this kind of cyber-capable model. The lesson: a declaration of arrival is not arrival — measure the age by your own storehouse, not the herald's bell, and mind the locks the new tool can open. The herald's-proclamation allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There was once a valley that lived and died by its harvest, and above the market square stood a tower with a great bell that was rung only for news that changed everything. For most of the valley's life the bell was silent, because most news did not change everything; the seasons turned, the mills ground, the storehouses filled or did not, and the people measured their fortunes the only honest way there is — by walking into the storehouse at year's end and counting the sacks.

Then one morning a herald climbed the tower, and the great bell rang out over the whole valley, and he proclaimed in a voice that carried to the farthest field: **"The age of gold has come! A new tool is here that can do the work of a hundred hands — that can read any ledger, open any door, and labor without rest. The old age is over. Rejoice."** And the valley split, as valleys do. Half the square erupted in celebration — they ran to reprice everything, tore up their plans, told their lenders the age of gold had come and borrowed against it. The other half fell to panic — they barred their doors and cursed the tower. Both halves had done the same thing without noticing: they had let the *bell* set the value of their year, when the only thing that had actually happened was that a man had rung it.

A few households did neither, and they were the ones the story is about. They did not argue with the bell — the tool was real, and remarkable, and the herald was not lying. What they did was quieter: they walked into their own storehouse and counted. And the storehouse told a plainer tale than the tower. Yes, the new tool was astonishing; yes, every hand in the valley now worked faster with it. **But when they weighed the sacks against the year before, the flour had barely risen.** The tool had made everyone busier and almost no one richer — the gains dissolved into more work attempted, more grinding, more re-grinding of the same grain, and the ledger at year's end read *productivity everywhere, profit almost nowhere.* The herald had proclaimed the age of gold from the tower; the storehouse said the age of gold arrives, if it arrives at all, one honestly counted sack at a time — and it had not arrived yet.

And then there was the other half of the proclamation, the half the celebrating households had been too thrilled to hear. The herald had also said, almost in passing, that the new tool could **open any door.** The wise households heard that clearly, because a tool that can open any door is not a blessing or a curse by nature — it is a blessing in the hand of the owner and a curse in the hand of a thief, and it does not ask which hand holds it. So even as the square argued about whether the age of gold had come, these households went and checked every lock the new tool could pick — and found that the same marvel that could do a hundred hands' honest work could also do a hundred hands' mischief, and that the valley's real exposure had gone up on the very morning the bell called it a triumph. The herald himself had brought the tool wrapped in extra guards, which told the careful few everything: *you do not wrap a gift in guards unless you know what it can do in the wrong hand.*

The households that kept their footing, then, did three unglamorous things while the square celebrated and panicked by turns. **They let the bell ring and did not reprice their year on it** — a proclamation is a claim about the age, and claims are cheap; the storehouse is dear. **They kept counting their own sacks** — measuring the new tool not by how fast it made a hand move but by whether, at harvest, there was more flour in the storehouse than the year before, and being honest when there was not. And **they minded every lock the tool could open** — treating the marvel as exactly as dangerous as it was useful, because the day a tool can do anything is the day it can do anything to *you*. They were not the loudest households in the valley. But when the celebrating ones came back from their repriced, over-borrowed year with empty sacks and an open door, these were the ones still standing.

**The moral, stated plainly:** the model is a rented stove, now nearly free; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the lingua franca is the tongue it speaks; the conductor keeps the ensemble in time; the ear hears whether it plays well; the repertoire is the music only your house can play; the census is the roll that counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the break-in is what the missing entries cost; the two mills are whether the motion grinds flour; the miller's toll is what the cheapest grinding charges; the apprentice's apprentice is what happens when the tools improve the tools — and **the herald's proclamation is the reminder that a declaration of arrival is not arrival: a lab can ring the bell for "the age of AGI," but the age arrives, if it arrives, in your storehouse, one honestly counted sack at a time — so measure it by your own ledger, not the tower's bell, and mind every lock the new tool can open.** Let the bell ring; keep counting your sacks; and guard the locks — because *a claim about the age is not flour in the storehouse, and a tool that can open any door has already changed what you must guard.*

**The question it forces:** *A lab has proclaimed the age of AGI, and the proclamation is loud, credible, and repriced into every headline and every valuation. So the question is not "is the tool remarkable" — it plainly is. It is two harder questions the bell cannot answer for us. First: are we repricing our strategy, our roadmap, our promises to our board and our lenders on a herald's word — or on our own ledger, which still says productivity is everywhere and EBIT is not? Are we measuring this by how fast it makes a hand move, or by whether there is more flour in the storehouse at year's end? And second: have we heard the other half of the proclamation — that the tool can open any door — and gone and checked every lock it can pick, because a capability that can do a hundred hands' work can do a hundred hands' harm, and the regulator's inspector is already in the square asking us to prove we did? When the bell rings for the age of gold, do we run to reprice — or do we walk into our storehouse, count our sacks, and mind our locks?*

The enterprise job is to hold those two clocks apart.**

- **The bell — a credible, agentic, dual-use proclamation.** **OpenAI launched GPT-6 Astra on 3 September; president Greg Brockman called it "the start of AGI" and "the AGI era" (Fortune, Bloomberg, TechCrunch, as reported). The guardrails are the tell: you harden what you know can be misused.

### Why "the herald's proclamation" is the layer that matters now

For two years the reflex was to ask whether the model was capable enough; every proclamation was treated as a strategy input. ## 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. **Run strategy on the ledger, not the bell — refuse to reprice on a proclamation.** A frontier lab has proclaimed "the start of AGI," and it will be quoted into every roadmap this week. **This month, put a hard rule on it: no plan, budget or board promise gets repriced on a claim about the age — only on our own storehouse, measured against EBIT, quarter by quarter.** Report one number to the board: the share of our AI spend that is tied to a measured EBIT or hard-cost outcome, versus the share justified by capability, benchmark or proclamation — and separate productivity (easy, ~80%) from earnings (hard, ~37%) explicitly.
2. **Count the flour — measure yield, not activity, and find the re-grinding.** ~93% of enterprise AI teams are over budget and ~60% of agentic-AI cost goes to "response refinement." **Require that every AI initiative carry a storehouse metric — output that reaches the P&L — and audit where the spend actually goes.** Report the share of AI cost producing measurable flour versus re-grinding the same grain, and reallocate toward the process redesigns (not the extra agents) that actually move the harvest.
3. **Mind the locks — treat the new capability as symmetric and prove the guardrails.** A computer-using, cyber-capable model is a gift to whoever holds it, and the EU AI Office's systemic-risk regime is already live. **Check every door an agentic, machine-operating model can now open in our estate; bound what it may touch; and be ready to show the model evaluation, red-teaming and incident-reporting the regulator now requires.** Report two numbers next quarter: the share of agentic/computer-using deployments with a mapped, bounded and monitored set of permissions, and our readiness — for any GPAI-class model we provide or heavily rely on — to meet the live systemic-risk duties. **The model is rented and nearly free, the knowledge is the moat, the census is the roll, the inspector asks for it, the break-in is what the missing entries cost, the two mills are whether it grinds flour, the miller's toll is what the cheap tier charges, the apprentice's apprentice is why a human must still hold the standard — and the herald's proclamation is the reminder that a declaration of arrival is not arrival: measure the age by your own storehouse, and mind every lock the new tool can open.**
