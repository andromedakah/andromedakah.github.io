The Keyring

Monday, 7 September 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

Yesterday a lab rang the bell for the age of AGI and the task was to mind the locks the new tool can open. This morning the story turns to the keys. Docusign will open its agreement layer to every AI agent via the Model Context Protocol — its MCP Server (Iris AI engine), callable from Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, Slack and any MCP client from 30 September (PRNewswire, The New Stack, as reported). 1 · Executive Summary

The enterprise reality moving fastest right now is not a new model but a new distribution of access: your systems are being opened to any agent that can speak the protocol, and the keys are handed out faster than anyone writes down who holds them.

1. The house is opening every room — via the open protocol. Docusign will open its agreement layer to every AI agent through its MCP Server (Iris engine), GA and callable from Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, Slack, Salesforce and any MCP client from 30 September, with account-level admin controls and multi-region infrastructure (PRNewswire, The New Stack, Yahoo Finance). Governed access to a system-of-record is now a key any agent can hold — which is why who holds the key stops being a detail.

2. The good pattern — the customer keeps the keys and the logs. Anthropic's Enterprise Frontier Safeguards (1 Sep): zero data retention plus automated cross-session misuse detection, with logs in the customer's own cloud (S3, Azure Blob, GCS) under the customer's own keys, no Anthropic human review; built with 100+ customers incl. a quarter of the Fortune 100 and every U.S. systemically important bank (Unite.AI, MarkTechPost). Hold the ring yourself; let the watcher watch without holding your data.

3. The gap is real regardless — no one keeps the ring. Non-human identities outnumber humans ~100:1 (144:1 cloud); >16% of orgs don't track AI-identity creation; ~91% use agents but only ~10% govern their identities; ~51% report no clear owner; Okta's AI Agents at Work 2026 finds ~90% can't govern what production agents do and ~54% already had an agent-related incident (CSA, GitGuardian, WEF, Okta, as reported).

Bottom line: every agent you admit is handed a key, and in most houses no one keeps the ring. The market is moving on both sides at once — Docusign cutting keys for every agent to a system-of-record, Anthropic handing the customer the keyring and the logbook. Treat every agent's access as a numbered key on a ring you keep — named to a holder, scoped to the rooms it needs, revocable the day the work ends, with the crown jewels behind a key no single agent holds alone. You minded the locks yesterday; today, keep the keyring — because a lock is only as good as your knowledge of who holds its key.

2 · Allegory — The Keyring

A great house ran on keys. Every door had a lock, every lock a key, and the whole security of the house came down to one humble object: the steward's ring, on which every key hung, numbered and written in a book against the name of the person allowed to carry it. A new maid got the three keys her work required and no more; when she left, they came back to the ring before she was paid. It was boring. It was also why the house had never been robbed from the inside.

Then came a season of such plenty that the house hired helpers by the hundred, and — because scoping was slow — handed every new helper a master key that opened every door. The work flew; the helpers outnumbered the family a hundred to one; and it became easier to cut another master key than to write it in the book. One evening the careful few went looking for the ring and could not find it — not stolen, but quietly ceased to exist: hundreds of loose master keys in unknown pockets, and no book saying whose. The doors were all still locked and the locks all still good — but a lock is only as good as your knowledge of who holds its key, and the house had lost it. A single lost coat now opened every room at once, and no one would know to change the locks until the grain was gone.

The question it forces: We minded the locks yesterday; this morning the harder question is the keys. Every agent we admit — every MCP client we let reach a system-of-record — is handed a key. Do we keep a ring? Can we say, this afternoon, how many non-human keys are out, who holds each, which rooms it opens, and which were never returned when the work ended? Are we still cutting master keys because it's faster than deciding which three doors a helper needs — and if one agent's key were lost tomorrow, would we know within the hour which doors it opened? When the whole market is cutting keys for every agent, the oldest question in the house is the only one that matters: who is keeping the ring?

3 · Technical — From the Locks to the Keys

The industry made granting access trivial and barely started making inventory, scoping and revocation trivial — and that gap is the enterprise's largest ungoverned surface. The door: Docusign opens its agreement layer to every agent via its MCP Server (Iris engine), GA and callable from Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, Slack and any MCP client from 30 September. The unit of access is no longer a human login but an agent identity — and there are far more of those than people. The MCP 2026-07-28 spec makes the keyring standard concrete: Enterprise-Managed Authorization (EMA) stable and Client ID Metadata Documents (CIMD) replacing Dynamic Client Registration — the plumbing to name and centrally govern which client holds which key. The keyring: Anthropic's EFS (1 Sep) keeps logs in the customer's own cloud under the customer's own keys, with automated misuse monitoring but no Anthropic human review — custody of keys and logs stays on your side of the API. The missing ring: NHIs outnumber humans ~100:1 (144:1 cloud); >16% of orgs don't track AI-identity creation; only ~10% govern them; ~51% have no owner; Okta — ~90% can't govern production agents, ~54% already had an agent incident. A compromised agent credential is an enterprise-scale access path, not a service-account leak.

On legacy: identity was built for a few thousand humans reviewed at joining and leaving; the agentic estate breaks every assumption — machine identities, a hundred to one, created at runtime, spawning more. Enumerate: one inventory of every non-human identity. Own: a named human owner for each. Scope: least-privilege, crown jewels behind a shared key. Revoke: kill one key in an hour without halting the estate, and hold your own keys and logs. The census is the roll, the inspector asks you to produce it, the herald's proclamation is why you measure the age by your storehouse — and the keyring is why you must know which key each agent holds and how to take it back.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Keep the ring — one authoritative inventory of every non-human key. Stand up a single roll of every agent, service and machine identity, with a named human owner for each (~51% have none; >16% don't even track creation). Report the share of non-human identities that are inventoried, owned and mapped to what they can reach.

2. Stop cutting master keys — scope every agent. Least-privilege for every agent, crown jewels behind a key no single agent holds alone (human-in-the-loop or dual control). Adopt the keyring standards the plumbing already offers (MCP EMA and CIMD) rather than per-server consent sprawl. Report the share of agents on least-privilege scopes vs broad or standing access.

3. Be able to take a key back — and hold your own keys and logs. Make it possible to revoke a specific agent's key within the hour without halting the estate, and buy the EFS pattern — our data, our keys, our logs, misuse watched in place. Report mean-time-to-revoke a compromised agent credential and the share of AI workloads where keys and logs stay in our control.
