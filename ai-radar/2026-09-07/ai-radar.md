## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Keyring"

*Topic: This morning the enterprise-AI story turns from the locks to the keys. Docusign said it will open its agreement layer to every AI agent via the Model Context Protocol — its MCP Server, powered by the Iris AI engine, callable natively from Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, Slack and any MCP client from 30 September, giving agents governed access to a system-of-record (PRNewswire, The New Stack). Anthropic introduced Enterprise Frontier Safeguards on 1 September — zero data retention plus automated cross-session misuse detection, with logs held in the customer's own cloud under the customer's own keys and no human review by Anthropic, built with 100+ customers including a quarter of the Fortune 100 and every U.S. systemically important bank (Unite.AI, MarkTechPost). The board-level catch is the identity ledger beneath: non-human identities outnumber humans ~100:1 (144:1 cloud-native); >16% of organizations don't track the creation of AI-related identities; ~91% use agents but only ~10% govern their identities; ~51% report no clear ownership; Okta's AI Agents at Work 2026 finds ~90% cannot govern what production agents do and ~54% have already had an agent-related incident. The lesson: every agent you admit is handed a key — keep the ring, so you can name who holds each one, scope it, and take it back. The keyring allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There was once a great house that ran on keys. Every door in it — the wine cellar, the counting-room, the muniment room where the deeds were kept, the little cupboard where the seed for next year's planting was locked away — had a lock, and every lock a key, and for generations the whole security of the house came down to one humble object: the steward's ring, on which every key hung, each one numbered, each one written in a book against the name of the person allowed to carry it. When a new maid was hired she was handed the three keys her work required and no more; when she left, the three keys came back to the ring before she was paid her last wage. It was a boring system. It was also the reason the house had never, in living memory, been robbed from the inside.

Then came a season of such plenty that the old ways could not keep up. The house took on helpers faster than it ever had — not by threes but by hundreds — and because there was no time to cut each helper a careful set of keys, someone had the bright idea of handing every new helper a *master key*, the kind that opened every door in the house, so that no helper would ever be slowed by a door. It worked wonderfully, for a while. The work flew. The helpers multiplied until they outnumbered the family a hundred to one, and every one of them could open every room, and the house had never been so productive. Nobody meant any harm. It simply became easier to cut another master key than to decide which three doors a helper actually needed — and easier still to skip writing the key down in the book, because the book had a hundred new lines a day and no one had been given the job of keeping it.

The careful few — there are always a careful few — went looking for the ring one evening, and could not find it. Not because it was stolen, but because it had quietly ceased to exist. There was no ring any more; there were hundreds of loose master keys, in hundreds of pockets, and no book that said whose. When they asked the plainest questions a steward is supposed to be able to answer — *how many keys are out; who holds them; which doors does each open; which of these hundred helpers left last month with a master key still in his coat* — the house could not answer a single one. It was not that the answers were bad. It was that there were no answers, because no one had kept the ring. The doors were all still locked, and the locks were all still good; but a lock is only as good as your knowledge of who holds its key, and the house had lost that knowledge the day it started cutting master keys faster than it wrote them down.

And the danger was not theoretical, because a house with a hundred master keys in unknown pockets is a house where a single lost coat is a catastrophe. One helper's key, dropped in the road or lifted from a pocket, now opened not one door but every door — the cellar, the counting-room, the muniment room, the seed cupboard all at once — and because no one knew that helper had held a master key in the first place, no one would know to change the locks until long after the grain was gone. The house had spent everything on hiring hands and nothing on keeping the ring, and so it had, without ever deciding to, made every helper a potential thief and every lost key a total loss. The productivity was real. So was the exposure, and the exposure had grown with every key cut and every line left unwritten.

The households that kept their footing did the boring thing, at scale. **They kept the ring** — one place where every key, human or not, was hung and counted, so the plainest questions had answers again. **They stopped cutting master keys** — every helper got only the doors the work required, and the precious rooms, the muniment room and the seed cupboard, sat behind a key no single helper held alone. **They wrote every key in the book at the moment it was cut, not after** — and, most important, **they took the key back the day the helper's work ended,** because an un-returned key is the whole risk in one sentence. One wise house went further and did what the best vendors were beginning to offer: it kept its own logbook, in its own strong-room, under its own lock, and let a trusted watchman read the log for signs of mischief without ever taking the book out of the house — so it got the watching without giving away the keys. They were not the fastest-hiring houses that season. But they were the ones that, when a coat went missing, could say in an hour exactly which three doors were exposed and change exactly those three locks — while the master-key houses were still trying to work out how many keys they had cut.

**The moral, stated plainly:** the model is a rented stove, now nearly free; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the lingua franca is the tongue it speaks; the conductor keeps the ensemble in time; the ear hears whether it plays well; the repertoire is the music only your house can play; the census is the roll that counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the break-in is what the missing entries cost; the two mills are whether the motion grinds flour; the miller's toll is what the cheapest grinding charges; the apprentice's apprentice is what happens when the tools improve the tools; the herald's proclamation is the reminder that a declaration of arrival is not arrival — and **the keyring is the discipline of knowing, for every agent you admit, exactly which key it holds: named to a holder, scoped to the rooms it needs, written down the moment it is cut, taken back the day the work ends, and never a master key when three doors would do.** Keep the ring — because a lock is only as good as your knowledge of who holds its key, and a house that cuts keys faster than it writes them down has already been robbed; it just doesn't know by whom yet.

**The question it forces:** *We spent yesterday minding the locks a powerful new tool can open. This morning the harder question is the keys. Every agent we admit — every MCP client we let reach a system-of-record, every helper we hire into the estate — is handed a key, and the market is now handing out keys to every agent that can speak the protocol. So: do we keep a ring? Can we answer, this afternoon, how many non-human keys are out in our house, who holds each one, which rooms it opens, and which were never returned when the work ended? Are we still cutting master keys because it is faster than deciding which three doors a helper needs — and if a single agent's key were lost tomorrow, would we know within the hour which doors it opened, or would we find out the way robbed houses always do, after the grain is gone? When the whole market is cutting keys for every agent, the only question that matters is the oldest one in the house: who is keeping the ring?*

## 3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

### 🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
- **Do we keep a ring — one roll of every non-human key?** NHIs outnumber humans ~100:1 (144:1 in cloud), yet ~51% of organizations report no clear owner for AI identities and >16% don't even track when an AI identity is created. **Can we answer this afternoon how many agent identities are live in our estate, who owns each, and what each can reach — or have we been cutting keys faster than we write them down?**
- **Have we stopped cutting master keys?** The fast path is to give every agent broad access because scoping is slower — and Okta finds only ~34% of firms apply the same controls to digital labor as to human labor. **Is every agent scoped to the rooms its work needs, with the muniment room and seed cupboard — our crown-jewel systems — behind a key no single agent holds alone?**
- **Can we take a key back — instantly — the day the work ends?** ~90% of organizations say they have no way to govern what production agents are actually doing, and ~54% have already had an incident from an agent acting unexpectedly. **If one agent's credential were compromised tomorrow, would we know within the hour which doors it opens and be able to revoke exactly those — or would we find out after the grain is gone?**

### 🏦 Financial Services
- Agents are being handed keys to systems-of-record (Docusign is opening its agreement layer to every MCP client from 30 September). **For every agent reaching a book of record — payments, positions, customer data — is the access a scoped, named, revocable key on a ring we keep, or a standing master key no one wrote down?**
- Anthropic's EFS is being built with every U.S. systemically important bank precisely so logs and keys stay in the customer's cloud. **For the models we run, do we hold our own keys and our own logs — and can we prove misuse is monitored without our data ever leaving our control, as our regulators will now ask?**

### 🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
- A single agent identity chaining across EHR, lab and research systems is a blast radius, not a convenience. **Have we scoped every clinical or research agent to the minimum rooms, and can we name the owner and revoke the key for each — before an agent with a master key touches patient data?**
- The identity, not the prompt, is now the control surface. **Are we hardening who the agent *is* and what its key opens — not only what we tell it — given that ~90% of organizations cannot govern what their production agents actually do?**

### 🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
- Non-human identities on the plant floor — agents touching MES, PLM, supplier and OT systems — are the fastest-growing keys in the estate. **Do we keep a ring for machine and agent identities the way we keep one for people, and is any agent holding a master key to systems that can move a physical process?**
- A lost or stale credential here mis-operates a line, not just a document. **Can we revoke a specific agent's key without halting the whole floor — or is access so coarse that the only "off switch" is pulling the plant?**

### 🛒 Retail / Consumer
- Agents are being pointed at pricing, checkout, inventory and customer data through open protocols. **Is each commerce agent a scoped, logged, revocable key — or have we widened the attack surface by handing master keys to every helper in the rush to "agentic everything"?**
- Third-party agents (via MCP clients like Docusign's) now reach into your stack from outside. **Do we know which external agents hold keys to our rooms, who approved each, and whether that access was ever scoped or reviewed?**

### 🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
- The EU AI Act's live regime turns "who accessed what, and who approved it" from hygiene into evidence. **For every agent touching a citizen record or a regulated workflow, can we produce the key, the holder, the scope and the approval on demand — the inspector is already asking?**
- Custody of keys and logs is now a procurement requirement, not a feature. **Are we buying the pattern Anthropic's EFS represents — our data, our keys, our logs, monitored in place — rather than surrendering custody of either the keys or the logbook to a vendor?**

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Locks to the Keys

Read the whole stack one more time, but from the seat of the person who has to answer, this afternoon, how many keys are out in the house. The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the lingua franca lets it speak; the conductor coordinates it; the ear hears its errors; the repertoire is the data it performs; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the break-in is what the missing entries cost; the two mills are whether the motion grinds flour; the miller's toll is what the cheapest grinding charges; the apprentice's apprentice is what happens when the tools improve the tools; the herald's proclamation is a claim about the age, not a delivery. **The keyring sits underneath all of it as the operational floor — because every layer above assumes something most houses do not have: a single, authoritative answer to "which agent holds which key, and can we take it back."** The engineering point is blunt: **the industry has spent two years making it trivial to grant an agent access and has barely started making it trivial to inventory, scope and revoke that access — and the gap between those two is now the enterprise's largest ungoverned surface.**

- **The door — access is being opened to every agent, over the open protocol.** **Docusign will open its agreement layer to *every* AI agent via its MCP Server, powered by the Iris AI engine and generally available globally, callable natively from Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, Slack, Salesforce and any MCP client from 30 September, with account-level admin controls, global multi-region infrastructure and multilingual support; agents reach past negotiations, accepted terms, clauses and company policy (PRNewswire, The New Stack, Yahoo Finance, as reported).** Read architecturally: this is a system-of-record exposing a governed, protocol-native door that any conformant agent can walk through. The capability is real and valuable — and it means the unit of access is no longer a human login but an agent identity, and there will be far more of those than there are people. The standards beneath make this concrete: the MCP 2026-07-28 spec promoted **Enterprise-Managed Authorization (EMA)** to stable — centralized, IdP-based access to MCP servers replacing per-server consent prompts — and adopted **Client ID Metadata Documents (CIMD)** in place of Dynamic Client Registration (verified at the MCP primary source). EMA and CIMD *are* the keyring standard: the plumbing to name and centrally govern which client holds which key. The point of the deep-dive is that the plumbing now exists — and most houses have not adopted it.
- **The keyring — hold your own keys and your own logs.** **Anthropic's Enterprise Frontier Safeguards (1 Sep) combines zero data retention with automated cross-session misuse detection by keeping activity logs in the customer's own cloud (Amazon S3, Azure Blob Storage, Google Cloud Storage) under the customer's own encryption keys and access policies, with automated safety monitoring but no human review by Anthropic; developed with 100+ customers including a quarter of the Fortune 100 and every U.S. systemically important bank, plus Comcast, KPMG, Mastercard, Salesforce and Visa, rolling out in phases this fall (Unite.AI, MarkTechPost, Qz, Neowin, as reported).** In keyring terms: the customer keeps the ring and the logbook in its own strong-room, and the vendor's watchman reads the log for misuse without ever removing the book. This is the shape enterprise governance is converging on — custody of keys and custody of logs stay on your side of the API — and it is exactly what regulated buyers were withholding budget for until it existed.
- **The missing ring — the identity governance vacuum.** The refrain from the census and the break-in returns as an operational floor: you cannot govern what you cannot enumerate. **Non-human identities already outnumber human users roughly 100-to-1, reaching ~144:1 in cloud-native environments; a 2026 CSA analysis found more than 16% of organizations do not track the creation of AI-related identities at all; ~91% of organizations use AI agents but only ~10% have a well-developed non-human-identity strategy, and a WEF analysis found ~51% report no clear ownership of AI identities. Okta's *AI Agents at Work 2026* (Okta Platform data plus 200+ executives) finds only ~34% always apply the same security controls to their digital labor force as to their human one, ~90% have no way to govern what agents in production are actually doing, and ~54% have already suffered a security incident from an agent acting unexpectedly (CSA, GitGuardian, WEF, Okta, as reported).** The engineering failure mode is precise: agents acquire permissions dynamically at runtime, spawn sub-agents, invoke external APIs and chain actions across dozens of systems — so a single compromised agent credential is not a service-account leak but an enterprise-scale access path, and the blast radius is exactly as large as the un-scoped, un-inventoried, un-revocable keys you handed out.

The strategic core: **the industry optimized granting and neglected governing, and the agentic enterprise makes that asymmetry the main event.** For weeks the frame climbed the stack — own the layer, build the gate, name the agent, speak the open tongue, conduct the ensemble, keep the ear, own the repertoire, take the census, face the inspector, shut the break-in, ask whether it grinds flour, price the toll, keep a human holding the standard, measure the age by your storehouse. This morning it lands on the plainest floor beneath all of them: an agent is an identity, an identity holds keys, and the enterprise that cannot name, scope and revoke its non-human keys has no security story at all — only a productivity story with an unmeasured liability underneath. "We opened it to every agent" is not the whole sentence; the whole sentence is **"we opened it to every agent — and here is the ring on which every one of those keys hangs, named, scoped, and revocable the day the work ends."**

### Why "the keyring" is the layer that matters now

For two years the reflex was to ask whether an agent *could* reach a system; the win was making access easy. This morning reframes it: **the market has made granting access to every agent trivial — over an open protocol, into systems-of-record — and has left inventorying, scoping and revoking that access almost entirely to the buyer.** A house can adopt every agent in the valley and still be one lost coat away from total loss, because the security of the estate was never the locks; it was the ring — the authoritative knowledge of who holds which key. What separates a firm that banks the agentic productivity from one that is quietly robbed is not a better model or a bolder rollout; it is whether it kept the ring while it hired the hundred hands.

### How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — **be able to account for what you run** — now aimed at the identity floor. On a legacy estate, identity was built for people: a few thousand humans, each with a login, each reviewed at joining and leaving. The agentic estate breaks every assumption in that sentence — the identities are machines, they outnumber people a hundred to one, they are created at runtime, they spawn more of themselves, and nobody was given the job of keeping their ring. The retrofit is **enumerate, own, scope, revoke. Enumerate:** build one authoritative inventory of every non-human identity, because >16% of firms don't even track creation. **Own:** assign a named human owner to every agent identity, because ~51% have none. **Scope:** replace master keys with least-privilege access to the specific rooms the work needs, and put crown-jewel systems behind a key no single agent holds alone. **Revoke:** make it possible to kill one agent's key in an hour without halting the estate — and hold your own keys and logs (the EFS pattern) so misuse is watched in place. ## 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. **Keep the ring — build one authoritative inventory of every non-human key.** The market is opening systems-of-record to every agent over MCP, and >16% of firms don't even track when an AI identity is created. **This month, stand up a single roll of every agent, service and machine identity in the estate — with a named human owner for each, because ~51% of organizations have none.** Report one number to the board: the share of non-human identities in our estate that are inventoried, owned and mapped to what they can reach — and be honest that if the number is near zero, we have been cutting keys faster than we write them down.
2. **Stop cutting master keys — scope every agent, and lock the crown jewels behind a shared key.** ~90% of organizations cannot govern what their production agents actually do, and a single compromised agent credential is an enterprise-scale access path, not a service-account leak. **Require least-privilege for every agent — access to the specific rooms the work needs, nothing more — and put crown-jewel systems behind a key no single agent holds alone (human-in-the-loop or dual control).** Report the share of agent identities on least-privilege scopes versus broad or standing access, and adopt the keyring standards the plumbing already offers (MCP Enterprise-Managed Authorization and Client ID Metadata Documents) rather than per-server consent sprawl.
3. **Be able to take a key back — and hold your own keys and logs.** ~54% of organizations have already had an incident from an agent acting unexpectedly, and the EU AI Office's live regime turns "who accessed what, who approved it" into evidence. **Make it possible to revoke a specific agent's key within the hour — without halting the estate — and buy the pattern Anthropic's EFS represents: our data, our keys, our logs, misuse watched in place rather than in a vendor's cloud.** Report two numbers next quarter: our mean-time-to-revoke a compromised agent credential, and the share of our AI workloads where keys and logs stay in our own control. **The model is rented and nearly free, the knowledge is the moat, the census is the roll, the inspector asks for it, the break-in is what the missing entries cost, the two mills are whether it grinds flour, the miller's toll is what the cheap tier charges, the apprentice's apprentice is why a human must still hold the standard, the herald's proclamation is why you measure the age by your storehouse — and the keyring is why you must know, for every agent you admit, exactly which key it holds and how to take it back.**
