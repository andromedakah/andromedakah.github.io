The Keyring

Yesterday's edition ("The Airlock") fitted a control point at the door your data leaves through. But a prompt going out is only half the crossing — the other half is what the agent may reach and act on once it is working. 1 · Executive Summary

For a month this radar has argued the model is a commodity, so the durable advantage is the layer you own around it. Yesterday that thesis got its first bolt — Inference hooks, the airlock that decides what leaves for the model. Today it gets the matching fitting on the other side of the crossing: what your agents may reach and do once they are working. On MCP's Enterprise-Managed Authorization (EMA) — stable 18 June 2026, 7-28 spec final — Okta's Cross App Access (XAA) hits general availability for Workforce customers via the Okta Integration Network in August 2026, and Anthropic names Okta a featured identity provider for Claude, Claude Code and Cowork. XAA extends OAuth so agent access is governed by the identity provider you already run: an admin authorizes a connector once for the whole org; each request exchanges an identity assertion (ID-JAG) for a token scoped to the user's groups and roles; the server exposes only pre-approved tools and data; no per-app consent; and offboarding revokes every grant centrally.

1. The keyring is yours, held at the identity provider you already own. Which tools, for which identity, at what scope — decided by your IdP and its group/role model, not a thousand app-by-app consents. Authorize once; provision by identity; revoke everywhere in one turn of the ring.

2. It is a standard, not a point integration. XAA is an OAuth extension; EMA is a stable MCP extension. The same keyring pattern can govern any tool and any model vendor that implements it — the asset is the standard, not the first product.

3. Real, and early — know the gaps. Okta is the first IdP (others to wire in); only tools with an MCP/EMA door are governed; and it controls access, not payload or behaviour (that is yesterday's airlock and your monitoring).

Bottom line: yesterday you fitted the airlock at the door your data leaves through; today you take back the keyring to everything your agents can reach — held through the identity provider you already trust, on a standard any vendor can implement. Wire your IdP to XAA, authorize connectors at the org level, scope by group and role, and rehearse "revoke everywhere in one motion" — because a hundred workers with un-tracked keys is not a workforce, it is an unlocked house.

2 · Allegory — The Keyring

Picture a great house at the hour its staff triples overnight. Ten thousand new workers — tireless, quick, literal — each needing to get into rooms to do a job. This is not ambition; it is arithmetic: in the modern estate the new workers, the agents, already outnumber the people who hired them roughly a hundred to one. Until now a worker got into a room badly — each door had its own lock, and the worker collected keys by standing at each door and being asked "do you allow this?" and saying yes because the work waited. The keys piled up on a hundred belts; nobody kept the roster; a key cut for an afternoon opened the room for a year; a worker who left took live keys into the night — and because the locks were all different, no single turn of the hand could call them back. A house with more keys than doors, and no one holding the ring.

What comes of age this month is the oldest fix, made new: one keyring, held by the house's own steward. The doors are re-fitted to a common lock the whole trade agreed on (MCP's enterprise-managed authorization; Cross App Access lets your steward turn it). A worker who needs a room presents itself once, at the steward's office — the steward you already employ, who keeps the roster of who is on which crew — and the steward cuts a key to exactly that room, stamped with its expiry, written in the daybook. When the worker leaves, the steward turns the ring once and every key goes cold, at every door. Two truths: the vendor supplied the fitting, but the keyring came home to the steward you already trust; and it is new, not yet whole — not every door is re-fitted (Okta is the first steward, not the only one), and a keyring governs which rooms a worker may enter, not what it does inside (that is yesterday's airlock).

The question it forces: Our agents outnumber our people a hundred to one — so who holds the keyring to everything they can touch? If the answer is "each app hands out its own keys and the user clicks allow," we have described the sprawl, not the control. Is there one steward — the identity provider we already run — that issues every agent's access, scoped, logged, and revocable everywhere in a single motion? If a person left tonight, how many live keys would walk out with them?

The engine is not where your risk lives; the keys those workers hold are. 5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Bring the keyring home — wire your identity provider to Cross App Access this month. XAA is GA for Okta Workforce via the Integration Network; EMA is a stable MCP extension and the 7-28 spec is final. Start with the tools that already expose an MCP/EMA door (Asana, Atlassian, Figma, Linear, Supabase, and the growing list), authorize connectors once at the org level, and let your IdP — not a hundred per-app consent screens — decide which agents reach which tools.

2. Scope by identity and rehearse the one motion that revokes everywhere. Cut each agent's access to the groups and roles the person actually holds, time-bound the grant, and — the acid test — prove that a single revocation at the identity provider kills every key that person's agents hold, at every tool. Report one metric to the board: the share of agent access issued and revocable through our own IdP.

3. Make it standard, not point — and map the doors still on ad-hoc locks. The asset is the pattern (XAA over OAuth, EMA in MCP): demand the same identity-scoped access from every tool and every model vendor, so swapping Opus 5 for Gemini or GPT-5.6 never scatters the keys. Then list the doors this fitting doesn't yet close — other IdPs, homegrown/legacy systems, and the payload/behaviour questions — assign each an owner, and treat them as the next fittings.
