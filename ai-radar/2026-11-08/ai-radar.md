## 1 · Executive Summary (90-second read)

For a long year the radar worked on the *servant* — name it (the livery), keep one book (the steward), put your name on the bond (the surety), check its wage (the second wage), send it to reap (the two harvests), hand it a scoped purse (the purse) — and then looked down at the *ground* (the foundation). **This morning the axis turns to the words themselves, and to an invisible maker's mark that the supplier is now pressing into every one of them.** The shift is not a metaphor this week; it shipped.

**The event — the mark is now in the ink, on by default.** On **7 November 2026 xAI switched on invisible text watermarking by default across its Grok API** — covering **seven models (Grok 4.7, 4.6, 4.5, 4.3 and three Grok 4.20 variants)** on both the Responses and Chat Completions endpoints. The mark **biases the model's word-and-token selection to encode a signal, adds no visible characters, and survives copy-paste**; it is **on by default**, and **only a team admin who confirms a legal exception may turn it off**. Developers can check a response through a header, but **detection access is granted per application — ordinary readers cannot verify text on their own yet** (KuCoin; SuperGrok; social coverage, as reported). xAI says watermarking does not change response quality or speed — its own claim, untested by outsiders. The stated basis is **Article 50 of the EU AI Act**: providers of systems that generate synthetic text, image, audio or video must mark the output in a machine-readable form, to the extent technically feasible. xAI **did not sign the EU's voluntary transparency Code of Practice**; it is meeting the legal minimum (Cooley; European Commission, as reported).

**The precedent — Anthropic already did it, by choice.** Since **2 August 2026**, **Claude models launched in the EU carry machine-readable marking at launch — embedded invisible watermarks in generated text and Content Credentials (C2PA) in generated files where supported — under the Article 50(2) Code of Practice Anthropic signed** as both model and system provider; models placed on the market before 2 August have until **2 December 2026** to comply; detection tools for third parties are "coming," not yet shipped; reported penalties reach **€15M or 3% of global turnover** (Anthropic help centre; The Decoder; Cooley, as reported). Two labs, two routes to the same place: **the maker's mark is now standard, and the enterprise did not put it there.**

**The stakes — whose signature is in your words, and who can read it?** The mark is first a *gift*: at last you can prove which of your outgoing words a machine wrote, and meet Article 50 without building the plumbing yourself. But it is the *vendor's* mark, not yours — it names **his** workshop inside **your** letters, it is on by default, you cannot remove it, and the lamp that reads it is, for now, in his hand. Set that against concentration: **three labs (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google) now take 69% of enterprise-AI spend, up from 50%** (Menlo 2026), while IBM's sovereignty study finds **71% say switching their primary AI vendor would be difficult and only 9% say they understand their AI dependencies well** (IBM Institute for Business Value, as reported). The maker's mark turns that dependence from an abstraction into a *signature*: most of what the whole market now writes is secretly stamped by one of three houses. The board move re-uses the year's discipline and points it at the words: **own a mark of your own** (sign and disclose your AI output on provenance you control — C2PA, your own labels — not only the vendor's hidden watermark); **keep a lamp of your own** (detection and transparency you don't have to beg a supplier for); and **count the workshops** (treat the maker's mark as a live map of whose hand is in your voice, and diversify before three signatures become one).

## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Maker's Mark"

*Topic: the frame turns from the servant, and from the ground it stands on, to the words the servant writes and the invisible mark now pressed into them. On 7 November 2026 xAI switched on invisible text watermarking by default across seven Grok models, citing EU AI Act Article 50 (live since 2 August 2026; machine-readable-marking grace period to 2 December 2026), having declined the voluntary transparency code; Anthropic has embedded invisible watermarks in Claude text and C2PA Content Credentials in its files since 2 August, under the code it chose to sign (KuCoin; SuperGrok; Cooley; Anthropic; The Decoder, as reported). Read beside Menlo's finding that three labs now take 69% of enterprise-AI spend (up from 50%) and IBM's that 71% say switching is hard while only 9% understand their AI dependencies (as reported). The maker's-mark allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There was a great house that had spent a long and careful year making its servants excellent, and then a harder season making sure it owned the ground they stood on. It had named every hand and cut it a scoped key, kept one honest book, put the master's name on the bond, asked whose second coin each advising servant took, sent the servants to reap, handed each a scoped purse, and refused to let the company that rented it the cheap, swappable stove pour the foundation beneath the floor. By any fair reckoning it was a well-run house, and a wise one.

But the house no longer wrote its own letters. That had been the quiet revolution of the age: the scribes still sat at their desks, but they wrote with **ink bought ready-mixed from the great ink-houses**, and the words came out faster and finer than any hand could manage alone. The master thought of the ink the way he thought of the stove — a commodity, bought by the barrel, swapped at will. He was wrong about one thing, and this week he learned it.

For a new rule had come down from the magistrate, and this very week two of the greatest ink-houses obeyed it. From now on, **every barrel of ink would carry an invisible maker's mark** — not a blot on the page, not a watermark you could hold to a window, but a mark woven into *the very choice of words*, into the turn of each phrase, so that any letter written with that ink could be traced back to the workshop that made it. One great ink-house switched the mark on across all seven of its inks, by default, overnight; another had already begun months before, of its own accord, under a charter it had signed; the newer houses did it only because the law now left them no choice. The master was, at first, delighted. *"At last,"* he said, *"I can prove which letters my machines wrote and which my people did. The magistrate wanted honesty about the machine's hand; now the honesty is baked into the ink. This costs me nothing and spares me the plumbing."* And he was right — that much was a genuine gift.

Then the steward, who read everything twice, pointed at the small print, and the master's pleasure cooled. **The mark was the ink-maker's, not the house's.** It did not say *this letter came from this house*; it said *this letter was written with this workshop's ink.* It was on every page, whether the master asked for it or not. He could not wash it out — it lived in the words themselves, not in a seal he could break. And here was the part that made the master set down his cup: **only the ink-maker held the lamp that could read the mark.** The workshop would reveal, to those it chose, which of its inks had written a given page — but the house itself had no lamp of its own, no way to look at its own outgoing letters and know for certain what signature they carried. *"So,"* said the steward slowly, *"every letter we send into the world is signed, invisibly, by a workshop that is not us — and we must ask that same workshop, cap in hand, if we ever wish to know what it wrote in our name."*

Then the master asked the colder question, the one the foundation had taught him to ask. *"How many ink-houses are there, really?"* And the clerk counted, and the answer was the same frightening arithmetic as the floor beneath them. **Three workshops now mixed more than two barrels in every three sold in the whole valley** — their share had climbed from a half to better than two-thirds in a single year — and seven houses in ten admitted they could not easily change their ink-maker, while only nine in a hundred could honestly say they understood whose inks they truly depended on. So the maker's mark was not merely a signature. **It was a map.** Lay all the valley's letters side by side, read the hidden marks, and you would see at a glance that most of what the whole valley wrote each day was secretly signed by one of three hands. *"Once you are locked to a supplier,"* a neighbouring master had said that season, *"you lose your leverage"* — and the mark made the lock legible, letter by letter.

So the wise houses learned three plain things, and they cost less to do early than late. **The first: keep a mark of your own, and set it before the letter leaves.** Welcome the ink-maker's mark as proof of the machine's hand — but do not let it be the *only* mark on the page. Put the **house's own seal** on every letter before it goes out: your own honest label of what was machine-written, your own provenance stamped in a form you control and can show to anyone, so that the world learns the letter came from *your* house and not merely from *some* workshop. A house known only by its supplier's signature has no signature of its own. **The second: keep a lamp of your own.** Do not be the house that must beg the ink-maker to read its own correspondence. Demand — in the contract, in the design — a way to detect and verify the marks in your own outgoing words without the supplier's permission; a provenance you can audit yourself. The honesty is worth little if the only candle that reveals it belongs to the man who lit it. **And the third: count the workshops, and read the mark as the map it is.** Treat the maker's mark not as a compliance chore but as a live census of whose hand is in your voice. If two barrels in three across the whole valley carry one of three signatures, then diversity of ink is now a matter of sovereignty, not thrift — because the day may come when three hidden signatures are the only three there are.

Here is the part that should keep a master awake. The rule that pressed the mark into the ink was a good rule, meant to protect the reader from being deceived by a machine's hand — and it does. But a good rule, obeyed by a market of three, quietly hands those three a new kind of hold: **they now sign, invisibly, almost everything the valley says, and they alone can read the signatures.** The lesson is not that the ink-makers are wicked, nor that the mark is wrong — the mark is right, and the house should be glad of it. The lesson is that **when you do not own a mark of your own, do not keep a lamp of your own, and do not count the workshops, you have let three strangers become the invisible co-signatories of everything your house says — and called it transparency.**

**The moral, stated plainly:** the model is a rented stove, now so cheap a bigger one draws a shrug; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising servant eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap and weigh what it brings in; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; the foundation is why you own the ground the house stands on — and **the maker's mark is why, having perfected the servant, funded it, and secured its ground, you must now look at the words it writes and refuse to let the ink-maker be the only one who signs them and the only one who can read the signature: keep a mark of your own and set it before the letter leaves, keep a lamp of your own to read the marks in your own words, and count the workshops whose signatures are in your voice — because the mark the magistrate meant as honesty becomes, in a market of three hands, the quietest map ever drawn of who owns what you say.** A house that lets its supplier be the sole signatory and the sole reader of everything it writes has not become transparent; it has handed its voice to three strangers and called the arrangement honesty.

**The question it forces:** *Every word our enterprise now sends into the world may carry an invisible maker's mark — a signature woven into the very choice of words, pressed there by a supplier, on by default, that we cannot remove and, for now, cannot even read without his lamp. Do we also set a mark of our own on our outgoing words, keep a lamp of our own to read what is in them, and count how few hands now sign most of what the whole market says — or have we let three ink-houses become the invisible co-signatories of everything we write, and mistaken their honesty for ours?*

## 3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

### 🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
- **Whose signature is in the words we send out — and can we read it ourselves?** Two major labs now press an invisible, machine-readable maker's mark into generated text by default (xAI across seven Grok models from 7 Nov; Anthropic in Claude since 2 Aug), and for now detection is largely gated to the vendor. **For our AI-generated external content, do we know which outputs carry a vendor watermark, can we verify it without the vendor's permission, and do we also attach a provenance mark of our own (C2PA / our own disclosure) that says the letter came from *us*?**
- **Have we turned Article 50 from a liability into a control we own?** The EU AI Act's transparency duties are live (since 2 Aug 2026) and the machine-readable-marking grace period for pre-existing systems closes **2 December 2026**. **Can we show, per system, that AI-generated or AI-manipulated content we publish is marked and disclosed as the Act requires — using provenance we control, not merely whatever hidden mark our supplier happens to add?**
- **Is the maker's mark telling us something our vendor map isn't?** Three labs now take 69% of enterprise-AI spend (up from 50%), 71% say switching is hard, and only 9% understand their AI dependencies (Menlo 2026; IBM, as reported). **If we read the hidden signatures across our own AI output, how few suppliers are actually writing most of our words — and is that concentration a deliberate choice or an accident we've never measured?**

### 🏦 Financial Services
- Communications, disclosures and advice are regulated for provenance and suitability. **For every AI-assisted client communication, can we prove — on provenance we control — what was machine-generated, and are we relying on a vendor's gated watermark that we cannot independently verify for records we must retain and produce to a supervisor?**
- Vendor concentration is a supervised risk, and the maker's mark makes it concrete. **Can we produce, for our regulator and board, a map of which AI suppliers' hidden marks are in our outputs, with a tested ability to detect and re-sign content ourselves if a single provider changes its watermarking, pricing or detection access?**

### 🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
- Clinical, regulatory and patient-facing text carries legal and safety weight. **Where AI drafts clinical, safety or patient material, do we attach our own validated provenance and disclosure — rather than trusting an invisible vendor watermark we cannot read — so that what we signed, and what a machine drafted, is auditable by us and by regulators, not only by the supplier?**
- Watermarks can persist through some editing but are not guaranteed to survive heavy revision. **Have we tested whether vendor marks survive our actual editing workflows, and do we have our own content-credential trail so provenance does not silently disappear the moment a clinician rewrites a paragraph?**

### 🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
- You run supplier-qualification and traceability discipline for physical inputs; AI-written content is now a traceable input too. **Have we applied the same traceability rule to AI output — a known, auditable provenance mark on technical documentation, manuals and specs — rather than depending on whichever supplier's hidden signature happens to be in the text?**
- Single-sourcing is a known risk you already manage. **If 69% of the market's AI words now come from three labs, is our documentation and IP pipeline single-sourced to one vendor's model — and would we even know, since the only record of authorship is a mark we cannot read without that vendor's lamp?**

### 🛒 Retail / Consumer
- Trust and authenticity drive conversion; disclosure rules are tightening. **For AI-generated product copy, reviews-adjacent content and customer messaging, do we disclose and sign with provenance we control, so a customer (and a regulator) can trust the mark came from us — rather than relying on an invisible vendor watermark no shopper can see or check?**
- Scale makes vendor dependence invisible until it bites. **If our highest-volume content is written by one model whose maker's mark we cannot independently read, have we measured that dependence and kept a tested alternate, so a change to that vendor's watermarking or detection terms doesn't silently rewrite the provenance of everything we ship?**

### 🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
- Public trust depends on knowing what the state wrote versus what a machine drafted. **Under the transparency duties you already owe, can you show that AI-generated public communications carry provenance the public can verify independently — using open, owned content credentials — rather than a proprietary vendor mark only the vendor can read?**
- Sovereignty is the sharpest here. **If most AI-written public text in the economy carries one of three private labs' hidden signatures, and only those labs can read them, is that a transparency win or a sovereignty risk — and are we procuring AI with a requirement that provenance and its detection be ours, portable, and open?**

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Foundation to the Maker's Mark

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking *is this agent named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and standing on ground I own?* and started asking the one question none of those controls touches: *whose signature is in the words my house sends out, and who is allowed to read it?* The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the keyring is which key it holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what it can reach; the bell discloses when it slips; the looking-glass measures by an honest outside light; the livery gives it a scoped identity; the steward keeps one office; the surety puts your name on the bond; the second wage asks whose interest it serves; the two harvests sends it to reap; the purse funds it; the foundation owns the ground. **Every one of those governs the *servant* or the *ground*. The maker's mark is about the *output* — and this week two labs made an invisible signature in that output standard: xAI by default across seven Grok models (7 Nov), Anthropic by choice since 2 August.** The point is blunt: **provenance just stopped being something you add and became something the supplier bakes in — which is a gift and a dependency at once, because the mark names the vendor's workshop, rides in your outgoing words, and (for now) can be read only with the vendor's lamp.**

- **The event — a maker's mark woven into the words, on by default.** **On 7 November 2026 xAI enabled invisible text watermarking by default across its Grok API — seven models (Grok 4.7, 4.6, 4.5, 4.3 and three Grok 4.20 variants), Responses and Chat Completions endpoints — a signal encoded in the model's word-and-token selection that adds no visible characters and survives copy-paste; opt-out requires a team admin to confirm a legal exception, and detection is granted per application rather than to the public (KuCoin; SuperGrok; social coverage, as reported).** Three engineering realities follow. First, **a token-biasing watermark is statistical, not a seal**: it persists through copy-paste and light edits but can degrade under heavy rewriting, translation or paraphrase, so it is evidence, not proof, and your retention and audit design must treat it that way. Second, **detection asymmetry is the real story**: a mark you cannot read without the vendor's cooperation is provenance you do not own — design for your own verifiable trail. Third, **"on by default, admin-only off" changes your data posture overnight**: outputs you already ship may now carry a vendor signature you never chose, which matters for confidentiality, competitive intelligence and records.
- **The precedent and the law — standardization by two routes.** **Anthropic has, since 2 August 2026, embedded invisible watermarks in Claude's generated text and Content Credentials (C2PA) in generated files, under the EU AI Act Article 50(2) Code of Practice it signed; systems placed on the market before 2 August have until 2 December 2026 to meet machine-readable marking; third-party detection tools are promised but not yet shipped; reported penalties reach €15M or 3% of global turnover (Anthropic help centre; The Decoder; Cooley; European Commission, as reported).** The engineering lesson is that **two different compliance philosophies — sign the voluntary code (Anthropic) vs. meet the legal minimum (xAI) — converge on the same fact for you: the mark is now in the output by default, and C2PA/content-credential provenance is the emerging open standard you can actually own.** Build to C2PA and open provenance now; it is the one layer where you can hold your own signature and your own lamp.
- **The answer — own a mark, own a lamp, count the hands.** **Attach provenance you control to AI output before it leaves (open content credentials / your own disclosure, not only the vendor's hidden watermark); build or contract for detection you can run without the vendor's permission; and treat the maker's mark as a live dependency map — because three labs already write 69% of the market's AI words (Menlo 2026) and only 9% of leaders understand their AI dependencies (IBM, as reported).** This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — *be able to account for, and act on, what you run* — now aimed at the words themselves: not what the agent is, serves, harvests, spends or stands on, but whose signature it leaves in what it writes, and whether you can read that signature yourself.

The strategic core: **the maker's mark is a gift you should accept and a dependency you must offset in the same breath — accept the provenance (you owed Article 50 anyway), and offset the lock-in by owning a mark and a lamp of your own.** The market makes the trap concrete: it feels modern and compliant to let the vendor's default watermark carry your provenance, and it feels like friction to stand up your own content-credential pipeline and detection — so most houses take the free mark and never notice they can neither read it nor remove it, in a market where three suppliers already sign two words in three. The board translation: **welcome the mark; never let the ink-maker be your only signatory and your only reader.** The failure mode is exact: **a house that names, governs, funds, perfects and grounds its servants — and then ships every AI-written word carrying a hidden signature it did not choose, cannot read, and cannot remove — will pass every transparency review and discover, the first time a vendor changes its watermarking or gates its detector, that it never owned the provenance of its own voice.**

### Why "the maker's mark" is the discipline that matters now

For a long year the worry was whether the servant was named, governed, funded and grounded. **This week the question moved into the words themselves: provenance stopped being optional and became a signature the supplier bakes in by default.** xAI's 7-November switch and Anthropic's since-August marking prove the maker's mark is now standard; Article 50 made it law; Menlo's 69% and IBM's 9%-understand-their-dependencies prove how few hands hold the pen. Transparency that stops at accepting the vendor's hidden mark will not protect your voice. The estates that keep their standing will take the gift (built-in provenance) and immediately offset the dependency — a mark of their own, a lamp of their own, and a clear-eyed count of whose signatures are in what they say.

### How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — **be able to account for, and act on, what you run** — now sharpened to *be able to read, and own, the provenance of what you send.* On a legacy estate the instinct is to treat the vendor's free watermark as "transparency, handled" and move on — it is easier, it is compliant-looking, and the plumbing is someone else's.
