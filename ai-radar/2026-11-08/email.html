The Maker's Mark

Sunday, 8 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

Provenance just stopped being something you add and became something the supplier bakes in. Two labs now press an invisible maker's mark into every word your AI writes — xAI on by default across seven Grok models, Anthropic by choice since August. It's a gift (built-in Article 50 provenance) and a dependency (a signature that names the vendor's workshop, rides in your outgoing words, can't be removed, and — for now — only the vendor can read). Own a mark of your own, keep a lamp of your own, count the workshops.

1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from the servant and the ground it stands on to the words it writes and the invisible maker's mark now pressed into them — and this week it shipped.

1. The event — a maker's mark, on by default. On 7 November 2026 xAI switched on invisible text watermarking by default across its Grok API — seven models (Grok 4.7, 4.6, 4.5, 4.3, three Grok 4.20 variants), both endpoints. The mark biases the model's word-and-token selection, adds no visible characters, survives copy-paste, is on by default, and only a team admin confirming a legal exception can turn it off; detection is granted per application (KuCoin; SuperGrok, as reported). Basis: EU AI Act Article 50; xAI did not sign the voluntary transparency code.

2. The precedent — Anthropic by choice, since 2 August. Claude's EU text has carried invisible watermarks and its files C2PA Content Credentials since 2 Aug 2026, under the Article 50(2) Code of Practice Anthropic signed; pre-existing systems must comply by 2 Dec 2026; third-party detection is promised but not yet shipped; penalties up to €15M or 3% of turnover (Anthropic; The Decoder; Cooley, as reported). Two labs, one standard — and the enterprise did not put it there.

3. The stakes — a signature you don't own, in a market of three hands. The mark is a gift (built-in provenance) but it names the vendor's workshop, rides in your words, can't be removed, and — for now — only the vendor can read it. Three labs now take 69% of enterprise-AI spend (up from 50%; Menlo 2026), 71% say switching is hard and only 9% understand their AI dependencies (IBM, as reported). Own a mark of your own, keep a lamp of your own, count the workshops.

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each governed the servant or its ground — the maker's mark is about the words it writes and the hidden signature now pressed into them. It is honesty about the machine's hand, and you owed it anyway. Just never let the ink-maker be the only one who signs your words, and the only one who can read the signature: own a mark of your own (C2PA), keep a lamp of your own (detection you don't rent), and count the workshops before three hidden signatures are the only three there are.

2 · Allegory — The Maker's Mark

A great house had made its servants excellent and secured the ground they stood on — but it no longer wrote its own letters. The scribes wrote with ink bought ready-mixed from the great ink-houses, and the master thought of it as a commodity, swapped at will. This week a new rule came down, and two of the greatest ink-houses obeyed: every barrel would carry an invisible maker's mark — not a blot, but a mark woven into the very choice of words, tracing any letter to the workshop that made it. One switched it on across all seven of its inks, by default, overnight; another had begun months before, by a charter it signed. The master was delighted: at last he could prove which letters a machine wrote. That much was a genuine gift.

Then the steward read the small print. The mark was the ink-maker's, not the house's. It named the workshop, not the house. It was on every page, asked for or not. The master could not wash it out — it lived in the words. And only the ink-maker held the lamp that could read it. "So," said the steward, "every letter we send is signed, invisibly, by a workshop that is not us — and we must beg that same workshop to learn what it wrote in our name." Then the master counted the ink-houses, and found the same arithmetic as the floor beneath him: three workshops now mixed more than two barrels in three, up from half in a single year; seven houses in ten could not easily change; only nine in a hundred understood their dependence. The mark was not just a signature. It was a map of who owned the valley's voice.

So the wise houses learned three things: keep a mark of your own (your house's seal and disclosure on the page before it leaves, not only the ink-maker's); keep a lamp of your own (a way to read the marks in your own words without begging the supplier); and count the workshops (read the mark as a dependency map, and diversify the ink before three hidden signatures are the only three there are). The rule was good — honesty about the machine's hand. But obeyed by a market of three, it handed those three the signatures of nearly everything the valley says, and the only lamps to read them.

The question it forces: Every word our enterprise now sends may carry an invisible maker's mark — a signature pressed there by a supplier, on by default, that we cannot remove or, for now, even read without his lamp. Do we also set a mark of our own, keep a lamp of our own, and count how few hands now sign most of what the whole market says — or have we let three ink-houses become the invisible co-signatories of everything we write, and mistaken their honesty for ours?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Maker's Mark

Every prior control governs the servant or the ground; the maker's mark is about the output. Provenance just stopped being something you add and became something the supplier bakes in by default — a gift and a dependency at once. The event: a token-biasing watermark is statistical, not a seal — it survives copy-paste but degrades under heavy rewriting, so it is evidence, not proof; the real story is detection asymmetry (a mark you can't read without the vendor isn't provenance you own); and "on by default, admin-only off" means outputs you already ship now carry a signature you never chose. The precedent & law: Anthropic's since-August marking (text watermarks + C2PA on files) and xAI's legal-minimum default converge on one fact — the mark is in the output, and C2PA/content credentials are the open standard you can actually own. The answer: own a mark (C2PA + your disclosure, set before send), own a lamp (detection you can run without the vendor), and count the hands (three labs write 69% of the market's AI words; only 9% understand their dependencies).

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Own a mark of your own. The vendor's watermark names his workshop and you can't read or remove it. Stand up an owned provenance step on your highest-volume AI-generated external content — attach open Content Credentials (C2PA) and a clear AI-use disclosure before anything ships, so every letter carries your house's signature, not only the ink-maker's. Report the share of AI content that leaves with provenance you own.

2. Keep a lamp of your own. A mark you can only read with the supplier's permission isn't provenance you own; xAI's detection is gated and Anthropic's third-party detector hasn't shipped. Require in contracts and build into your pipeline a way to detect and verify your own outgoing AI content without the vendor's gate — at minimum a C2PA trail you can audit end-to-end. Report whether you can independently verify a sample today — yes or no.

3. Count the workshops. Three labs write 69% of the market's AI words, 71% say switching is hard, and only 9% understand their dependencies — the hidden signatures in your output are a sovereignty map you've never read. Inventory which models write your highest-volume content, record whose mark each carries, and qualify a tested alternate for one load-bearing workflow. Report your AI-output concentration by vendor and the time-to-switch for one critical workflow.
