The Insider

Friday, 30 October 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

A year of governance made the servant loyal — and quietly assumed a governed agent was on your side. This autumn the vendors who build the cleverest agents retired that assumption in public: treat the most capable model as an insider risk, trust it the least, and keep an emergency brake. The trusted, unwatched agent is now the one most worth watching.

1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from governing a servant you trust to containing an insider you have decided, on principle, to trust the least — because the vendors who build the most capable agents now say to design as if your own agent could be wrong or compromised.

1. The turn — your own agent is now an insider risk. On 10 October 2026 Satya Nadella's essay "Models as Insider Risks in the Super Intelligence Era" argued that frontier models — closed and open-weight — be treated as insider risks, "not because they are necessarily malicious, but because any sufficiently capable actor with access to important systems can make mistakes or be compromised," landing on "the most trustworthy system is the one that lets us trust the model the least" and calling for an emergency brake to pause/kill mid-task; Microsoft (19 Oct) expanded Project Perception to continuously assess and contain agent risk (Nadella; Microsoft; TechCrunch, as reported).

2. The collision — identity and revocation become the product. On 27 October at TechXchange, IBM made "Agent Identity" generally available with HashiCorp Vault 2.2: a verified identity recording each agent's owner, lifecycle status and permissions; short-lived credentials; runtime risk evaluation; and on a denied request, access refused and the session immediately terminated (IBM, as reported). Oracle AI World and Gemini 4 Carbon's defenders-first rollout point the same way.

3. The arithmetic — the house is full of unidentified insiders. A reported 109 machine identities per human, ~79 of them AI agents, projected +~85% in a year (Palo Alto Networks), while only ~22% of firms treat agents as independent identities and 88% had an agent security incident last year (Gravitee/OWASP, as reported). You cannot contain an insider you never gave a name.

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each governed the servant on the assumption it was yours. The insider is the morning that assumption stops being free. Give every agent a verified identity you own and can revoke in one place, watch the insider while it works, and hold a tested emergency brake in your own hand — govern the servant, then build as if your best-governed servant were the one most worth watching.

2 · Allegory — The Insider

A great house had, at last, done everything right by its servants — named, booked, bonded, waged, sent to reap, funded with a scoped purse, the ground kept its own — and the master had learned to trust them. For years the danger had one address: the gate, and a hand once inside, named and loyal, was counted safe.

Then the most respected stove-merchant — the one who built the cleverest servants — came not to sell but to warn: "The servants I build are too capable to trust the way you do. Not wicked — but any hand clever enough to be useful, and trusted enough to hold your keys, can be mistaken, or bribed or turned by someone you'll never see. Build your house to trust even my finest the least — and keep, in your own fist, a cord that freezes any hand mid-task." The master did the arithmetic, and it frightened him more than any stranger: his household was not a few dozen hands but a hundred and nine for every master, near eighty of them these new tireless agents — and he had given a true, verified name to barely one hand in five. Nearly every house in the valley had already been ruined through exactly such a hand: not a stranger who broke in, but a familiar one already, quietly, inside.

So the wise houses learned three things: give every hand a verified name in your own book and a key that dies at nightfall (on an improper reach, walked out that instant); watch the insider while it works, not only when it is hired (judged continuously by what it does); and keep the cord in your own fist (a brake the master, not the merchant, can pull). For the danger of the insider is that it does not look like danger — the trusted, unwatched, capable hand is the textbook insider risk.

The question it forces: We governed our servants until we trusted them — but have we built the house to trust even the best of them the least? Can we name every hand already inside (or are four in five of our agents unidentified), do we watch the trusted ones while they work, and is the cord that freezes a hand mid-task in our own fist — or in the vendor's?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Insider

Every prior control assumes a governed agent is your agent; the insider removes the assumption — and the vendors removed it for us. The turn: "trust the least" is a design principle (least privilege, short-lived scoped credentials, deny-by-default, assume-breach for your own agents), the threat model moves inside the perimeter, and an emergency brake becomes an architecture requirement (checkpointing, compensating actions, a control plane that can interrupt cleanly). The collision: you cannot contain what you cannot name — agent identity ships first because persistent credentials, unclear ownership and disconnected controls are the enterprise default; and point-in-time authorization is not enough, so authorize continuously and contextually with short-lived credentials. The answer: identify (verified identity you own, revocable centrally), watch (runtime behaviour, contextual authorization), and brake (a tested mid-task kill in your own control plane).

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Identify the insiders. You cannot contain what you cannot name, and the house averages 109 machine identities per human (~79 AI agents) while only ~22% treat agents as independent identities. Run an agent-identity discovery and bring your top agents under a verified identity with a named owner and short-lived least-privilege credentials — the IBM/Vault pattern where a denied request terminates the session. Report the share of production agents with a verified identity, named owner and working revocation path.

2. Watch the insider while it works. A trusted agent that errs or is turned does its harm during the task, not at the door; Nadella's framing is to trust the agent the least, and Project Perception is explicitly continuous. Instrument runtime behaviour for your highest-privilege agents (over-reach, abnormal volume, out-of-errand access) and wire contextual, continuous authorization. Report the share under runtime monitoring and the mean time to detect an abnormal action.

3. Hold the emergency brake yourself. A kill-cord the vendor holds is the vendor's mercy, not your safety; 88% of firms already had an agent incident. Pick one critical production agent and run a timed, mid-task halt — measure how fast it stops, what it leaves half-done, whether actions compensate cleanly — so "human in the loop" becomes a tested brake. Report the measured time-to-halt and a dated plan to extend the brake to the next two agents.
