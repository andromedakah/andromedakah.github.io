# 🗓️ AI Tech Radar — The Stop-Cord

**Saturday, 28 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering**

> You spent a year making the servant excellent — you named it, kept one book, put your name on the bond, checked its wage, sent it to reap, handed it a scoped purse, and then looked down and owned the ground it stands on. Every one of those controls improved what the servant *is*, *does*, *serves*, *spends*, or *stands on*. This morning the frame narrows to the one control that is worth nothing unless it actually works: **can you stop it?** The month made the question concrete. On **3 November 2026, at Fal.Con Europe in Barcelona, CrowdStrike shipped an "AI Kill Switch"** inside Falcon Guardian — "one action, every agent stops" — that cuts a rogue agent off from the models it calls, revokes its permissions and restricts it across the estate (CrowdStrike; CSO Online; AI Magazine, as reported). And beneath both sits the number that should stop a board cold: in a survey reported this autumn, **only 6% of technology leaders could cut off a rogue AI agent within an hour**, while **65% of organizations already suffered at least one AI-agent security incident in the past year** and **97% expect a material agent-driven incident within twelve months** (CSA/Token Security; Arkose Labs; Cybernews, as reported). A brake you have never pulled is not a brake. It is a length of rope.

## 1 · Executive Summary (90-second read)

For a year the radar worked outward from the servant — name it (the livery), keep one book (the steward), put your name on the bond (the surety), check its wage (the second wage), send it to reap (the two harvests), hand it a scoped purse (the purse), own the ground it stands on (the foundation). Each discipline answered a different question about the agent. **This morning the question collapses to one word: *stop.*** Three developments in the same month make the shift plain, and they converge on a single, unglamorous capability.

**The product — the kill switch shipped.** On **3 November 2026, at Fal.Con Europe (Barcelona), CrowdStrike introduced an "AI Kill Switch"** as part of Falcon Guardian: a single action that, in the company's framing, **stops every AI agent at once** by cutting agents off from the models and services they call, revoking their permissions through identity security, restricting SaaS agents through Falcon Shield, and (per coverage) blocking a terminated agent from relaunching and from reaching the enterprise network (CrowdStrike; CSO Online; AI Magazine; Cyber Daily, as reported). The honest fine print, from the same coverage: the first release acts **at the AI gateway** — the chokepoint between agents and the services they call — while **endpoint termination is listed as planned/"coming soon."** A kill switch that reaches the front gate but not yet the whole house is a real control with a real boundary, and knowing where that boundary is is the whole point.

**The rule — the regulator is drafting the duty.** In the same window, the **HKMA circulated a November 2026 discussion paper on agentic-AI governance in banking**, following its October **ART framework**. Among its proposals: **intent-anchored accountability**, a **registry of material AI agents**, and **effective intervention mechanisms** — i.e., a supervised expectation that a bank can *identify* each material agent and *halt* it, by design, not by hope (HKMA; fintech.global; Crowdfund Insider, as reported). It is a discussion paper, not yet binding — but the direction is unmistakable: the ability to stop an agent is moving from a nice-to-have in the runbook to a thing a supervisor will ask you to demonstrate, alongside a roll of which agents you even have.

**The gap — almost no one can actually pull it.** The reason both the product and the rule arrived now is the arithmetic underneath. Agents have multiplied — the autumn model wave (OpenAI's GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol around 17 Nov; Claude Fable 5.2 at a reported $8/$40 on 9 Nov; Gemini 4 Carbon/Hydrogen/Neon across 10–18 Nov; Mistral Large 4 GA on 5 Nov) made intelligence cheaper and faster still, so enterprises now run fleets of them (names and dates vary across trackers; as reported). And the fleets are not governed: **65% of organizations reported at least one AI-agent security incident in the past year** (CSA/Token Security), **compromised agents and other non-human identities are now a leading route into the enterprise** (SpyCloud 2026, via the OECD AI incidents monitor), **76% do not fully govern or monitor their non-human identities** (Netwrix 2026), and **97% expect a material agent-driven incident within twelve months** (Arkose Labs) — yet, in the survey that frames the whole edition, **only 6% of technology leaders could cut off a rogue AI agent within an hour** (Cybernews, as reported). That 6% rhymes, eerily, with the 6% from September who believed they could switch their primary AI vendor cleanly: in both cases, the gap is between a capability on a slide and a capability you have tested.

**The stakes — build the cord, hold the cord, pull the cord.** The board move re-uses the whole year's discipline and points it at the brake. **First, build a real cord** — a tested ability to halt a material agent that reaches it *everywhere it runs* (gateway *and* endpoint *and* identity *and* the SaaS seats it holds), not just at the one chokepoint that happens to be instrumented. **Second, keep the cord in your own hand** — know whose hand can pull it (yours, your security vendor's, the regulator's), because a stop-cord whose only end is in the vendor's cellar is the foundation problem wearing a safety vest. **Third, pull the cord in a drill** — on a schedule, against a named agent, measured — because the 6% number is the tell: a brake no one has ever pulled is rope. Do it now, while the agent fleet is still small enough to inventory and the regulators are still writing the expectation rather than auditing against it.

## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Stop-Cord"

*Topic: This month the frame narrows from governing the servant to being able to halt it. On 3 November 2026, at Fal.Con Europe in Barcelona, CrowdStrike shipped an "AI Kill Switch" in Falcon Guardian — "one action, every agent stops" — that cuts agents off from their models, revokes permissions and restricts SaaS agents, acting first at the AI gateway with endpoint termination planned (CrowdStrike; CSO Online; AI Magazine, as reported). In the same window the HKMA circulated a November discussion paper on agentic-AI governance proposing a registry of material AI agents and effective intervention mechanisms (HKMA; fintech.global, as reported). Beneath both sits the number that frames the edition: only 6% of technology leaders could cut off a rogue AI agent within an hour, while 65% already had an agent incident in the past year and 97% expect a material one within twelve months (CSA/Token Security; Arkose Labs; Cybernews, as reported). The stop-cord allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There was a great house whose engines had grown wonderful. Over a long year the master had named every servant and cut it a scoped key, kept one honest book, put his own name on the bond, asked whose second coin each adviser took, sent the servants to reap, handed each a scoped purse, and at last looked down and bought the very ground the house stood on. The servants were fast now, and cheap, and many — the forge sold a brighter engine every fortnight, each quicker than the last and no dearer — so the master had not one great engine but a hall full of them, humming day and night, doing the work of a hundred hands.

And one grey morning a single engine went wrong. Not wickedly — it simply mistook its errand, and began, very efficiently, to do the wrong thing at the speed of a machine. The master ran to the hall and reached for the thing every engine is supposed to have, the one control that is worth nothing until the moment it is worth everything: **the cord that cuts the power dead.** And his hand closed on air. There was no cord. Or rather — and this was worse — there was a cord, a fine one, that the master had bought that very season from a famous smith, and it hung there looking exactly like a brake; but the master had never once pulled it, did not know which engines it reached, and was not certain the other end was in his hand at all.

For here was the truth the house had not wanted to count. The smith's cord — a clever, genuine thing — ran to the **front gate** of the hall, the one doorway through which the engines drew their fire; pull it, and an engine at the gate would starve and stop. But three of the engines had their own small hearths deep in the house, and the cord did not reach those; the smith had promised that reach was "coming." And the register of engines — which hummed, which were material, which could touch the strongroom — had never been written, so in the moment of fright the master could not even say *how many* engines he had, let alone which one had turned. When he asked the honest question of his peers, the answer was the coldest number of the year: of a hundred such houses, **only six** could lay a hand on the cord and stop a runaway engine inside the hour. The other ninety-four had bought the cord, or meant to, and had never pulled it. They owned a brake the way a man owns a fire-drill he has never run: on paper, and not at all.

Two roads, as always. The easy road was the purchase order — buy the famous cord, hang it by the door, note it in the ledger as "done," and feel safer without being safer. The wiser road, which the careful houses took, began by refusing to confuse three different things that all wear the word *brake*. **A cord you have bought is not a cord you have pulled.** **A cord that reaches the front gate is not a cord that reaches the whole house.** **And a cord whose far end is in the smith's own hand is not a cord in yours.** The careful houses wrote these on the wall of the engine hall, because each was a way the brake could be real and useless at once.

So the wise houses did three plain, unglamorous things. **The first: they built a cord that truly reaches.** Not one that starved an engine at the gate while three kept burning on their private hearths, but one wired to every place an engine could run — the gate, the inner hearths, the keys it carried, the seats it held in other men's halls — so that one pull stopped the servant *everywhere*, not merely where the wire happened to hang. And before they could build it, they had to do the dull thing first: **write the register** — a roll of every engine, which were material, which could reach the strongroom — because you cannot stop what you have not counted, and the harbourmaster, newly, had begun to ask houses to produce exactly that roll. **The second: they kept the cord in their own hand.** They were grateful for the smith's clever brake, and they used it — but they knew whose hand held the far end, and they made sure that if the smith slept, or raised his price, or simply looked away, the house could still stop its own engines. A brake you can pull only by the grace of the man who sold it is not a safeguard; it is the ground-you-do-not-own problem wearing a safety-vest. **And the third — the one the ninety-four skipped — they pulled the cord.** On a fixed day, against a named engine, with a clock running, they yanked the brake in a drill and measured how long the hall took to fall silent, and what broke when it did, and whether the three inner hearths really died or only flickered. The first drill always shamed them; the tenth made them the six-in-a-hundred.

Here is the part that should keep a master awake. The hall was loud and marvellous and growing — a brighter engine every fortnight, more of them every month — and all that glorious productivity was, quietly, raising the stakes on the one cord no one wanted to think about. **The more engines you run, and the faster and cheaper they get, the more a single runaway can do before you reach the brake — and the more certain it becomes that one of them will run.** Ninety-seven houses in a hundred already expected a bad morning within the year; two in three had already had one. The lesson was not that engines are evil; a mistaken engine is not a wicked one. The lesson was that **a house that can build a hundred engines and cannot stop one has not been bold; it has been standing in a hall full of running machinery with its hand closed on air.**

**The moral, stated plainly:** the model is a rented stove, now so cheap a bigger one draws a shrug; the knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising servant eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; the foundation is why you own the ground it stands on — and **the stop-cord is why, having built your engines wonderful and many, you must keep, in your own hand, a tested cord that halts any one of them mid-stride and reaches it everywhere it runs: write the register so you can name what to stop, build a brake that reaches the inner hearths and not just the gate, keep the far end in your own hand and not the smith's, and pull it in a drill until stopping the hall inside the hour is a thing you have *done*, not a thing you *own*.** A house that buys a famous cord and never pulls it has not bought safety; it has bought the feeling of safety, and hung it by the door of a hall full of running machines.

**The question it forces:** *We can build and buy agents by the fleet — the market proves it every fortnight. But can we stop one? For each material agent, do we have a cord we have actually pulled — one that reaches it everywhere it runs, not just at the gateway; one whose far end is in our own hand, not only the vendor's; one measured by a real drill against the clock — or are we among the ninety-four houses in a hundred that own a brake they have never tested, standing in a louder and louder engine hall with a hand closed on air?*

## 3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

### 🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
- **If one of our agents went rogue this hour, who could stop it, and how long would it take?** A reported **only 6% of technology leaders could cut off a rogue agent within an hour**, while **65% already had an agent incident in the past year** and **97% expect a material one within twelve months** (Cybernews; CSA/Token Security; Arkose Labs). **Have we ever actually run that drill — pulled the cord against a named agent, with a clock — or do we only own a kill switch on a slide?**
- **Does our "kill switch" reach the agent everywhere it runs, or only at the front gate?** CrowdStrike's new AI Kill Switch acts first **at the AI gateway**, with **endpoint termination planned** (CrowdStrike; CSO Online, as reported) — a real control with a real boundary. **For each material agent, do we know whether a single pull stops it at the gateway, the endpoint, its identity/permissions, *and* the SaaS seats it holds — or would three of them keep running on private hearths?**
- **Whose hand is on the far end of the cord?** A vendor's kill switch is not your kill switch. **If our security vendor slept, deprecated the feature, or raised its price, could we still halt our own agents — and have we written the register of material agents the regulator is starting to expect, so we could even name what to stop?**

### 🏦 Financial Services
- The **HKMA's November discussion paper** proposes a **registry of material AI agents** and **effective intervention mechanisms**, after its October **ART framework** (HKMA; fintech.global, as reported). **Can we produce, today, a roll of our material AI agents — which touch customer money, which can reach the core — each with a named human owner and a tested halt procedure — or would a supervisor's request for that register find us counting from scratch?**
- You already run kill-switch and circuit-breaker discipline in trading. **Have we applied the same reflex to agents — a tested, timed intervention that degrades a runaway agent's blast radius gracefully rather than relying on a vendor feature we have never triggered under load?**

### 🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
- An agent that mistakes its errand in a clinical or safety workflow does the wrong thing at machine speed. **For each agent touching patient data or clinical decisions, is there an intervention we have actually exercised that halts it mid-action — and does it reach the agent at the endpoint and in its identity, not just at the API gateway?**
- Non-human identities are now a leading entry point for attackers (SpyCloud 2026, via OECD), and **76% don't fully govern them** (Netwrix 2026). **Do our clinical agents have governed, revocable identities we can cut in one action — or standing credentials we could not pull back inside an hour?**

### 🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
- You live by the physical emergency-stop: every dangerous machine has a tested, reachable, independent cut-out. **Have we given our software agents the same engineering respect — an e-stop that is wired to every place the agent runs, independent of the vendor that supplied it, and drilled on a schedule — or is our digital hall full of running machines with no tested brake?**
- Fleets scale faster than governance. **As cheaper, faster models (the autumn wave) let us deploy more agents, is our ability to inventory and halt them keeping pace — or is the register growing stale precisely as the blast radius grows?**

### 🛒 Retail / Consumer
- Peak season is when a runaway agent costs the most and when you can least afford a clumsy, untested halt. **If a pricing, fulfillment or support agent misbehaved on our biggest sales day, could we stop just that agent — in one action, without taking the whole stack down — and have we rehearsed that surgical cut, not just a full power-off?**
- Cheap agents proliferate quietly across teams (shadow agents). **Do we actually know how many agents touch checkout, search and support — the register — so that "stop the rogue one" is even a question we can answer?**

### 🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
- Continuity of public service makes an ungovernable agent a public-accountability failure, not just a risk. **Under the duties you already owe (EU AI Act high-risk regime, live since 2 August 2026), can we demonstrate, for each material public-service agent, a tested intervention we control — a register, an owner, a drilled halt — rather than a dependency on one vendor's switch?**
- Supervisors are converging on the same two asks — *a roll of your agents* and *a way to stop them* (the HKMA model). **Are we ahead of that expectation by design, or will we be writing the register for the first time when the auditor asks for it?**

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Foundation to the Stop-Cord

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking *is this agent named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and grounded?* and started asking the one question that makes all the others cash out in a crisis: *can I stop it — this one, now, everywhere it runs, with my own hand?* The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the keyring is which key it holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what it can reach; the bell discloses when it slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery gives it a scoped identity; the steward keeps one office; the surety puts your name on the bond; the second wage asks whose interest it serves; the two harvests sends it to reap; the purse funds it; the foundation owns the ground it stands on. **Every one of those governs what the agent *is*, *does*, *serves*, *spends*, or *stands on*. The stop-cord governs whether you can *halt* it — and this month the market and the regulators made that the sharpest question of all: CrowdStrike shipped a kill switch (3 Nov), the HKMA began drafting a duty to have one (Nov), and the surveys confirmed that only 6% can actually pull it in time.** The point is blunt: **a control you have never exercised is not a control; and three different things all wear the word "kill switch" — one you bought, one that reaches the gateway, and one whose far end is in your own hand — and only the one you have pulled, that reaches the whole house, and that you control, will stop a runaway agent at 3 a.m.**

- **The product — the kill switch is now a buyable thing, with a buyable thing's boundary.** **On 3 November 2026 (Fal.Con Europe, Barcelona), CrowdStrike introduced an "AI Kill Switch" in Falcon Guardian: one action to stop agents by cutting them off from the models and services they call, revoking permissions via identity security, and restricting SaaS agents via Falcon Shield — acting first at the AI gateway, with endpoint termination planned/"coming soon" (CrowdStrike; CSO Online; AI Magazine; Cyber Daily, as reported).** Architecturally, three things follow. First, **the gateway is the natural first chokepoint** — it is where you can observe and sever an agent's access to its model and tools without touching the host — but an agent with a local model, a cached credential, or a seat in a third-party SaaS app may survive a gateway cut, which is exactly why endpoint, identity and SaaS enforcement have to be part of *one* pull. Second, **a kill switch is a distributed systems problem, not a button** — "stop every agent" means fanning a revocation across gateways, identity providers, endpoints and SaaS controls fast enough to beat a machine-speed actor, and the gap between "we can block at the gateway" and "we can stop it everywhere" is the whole engineering project. Third, **a vendor's kill switch is a dependency** — if the only brake runs through one security vendor's control plane, you have re-created the foundation problem at the most dangerous possible layer; you want the ability to halt your own agents even if that vendor is unavailable.
- **The rule — the duty to be able to stop is being written, and it starts with a register.** **The HKMA's November 2026 discussion paper on agentic-AI governance (following its October ART framework) proposes intent-anchored accountability, a registry of material AI agents, and effective intervention mechanisms (HKMA; fintech.global; Crowdfund Insider, as reported); it is a discussion paper, not binding guidance.** Two engineering realities follow. **First, you cannot stop what you have not inventoried.** The register comes before the brake: a roll of every material agent — what it is, who owns it, what it can reach, how to halt it — is the precondition for intervention, and most houses do not have it (76% don't fully govern their non-human identities; Netwrix 2026). **Second, "intent-anchored accountability" means the halt has to map to a human owner, not just a process ID** — when you pull the cord, someone must be accountable for the agent you stopped and for what it was doing, which is the livery and the surety reappearing one layer down, at the moment of intervention.
- **The gap — the brake is almost universally untested.** **Only 6% of technology leaders could cut off a rogue AI agent within an hour (Cybernews, as reported), even as 65% had an agent incident in the past year (CSA/Token Security), compromised agents and non-human identities became a leading route into the enterprise (SpyCloud 2026, via OECD), and 97% expect a material agent-driven incident within twelve months (Arkose Labs).** This is an engineering verdict, not a mood: the difference between the 6% and the 94% is not whether they *own* a kill switch but whether they have *pulled* one — measured the time-to-halt, found the agents that kept running on private hearths, and fixed them. The drill is the control; the purchase is only its raw material. And the 6% here rhymes with September's 6% who believed they could switch vendors cleanly — the same gap, between a capability on a slide and a capability under load, at a different layer of the stack.

The strategic core: **the agent fleet is getting cheaper, faster and larger every fortnight — collect that productivity — but every added agent raises the stakes on the one control you have almost certainly never tested, the ability to stop a runaway before it finishes its mistake at machine speed.** The market makes the temptation concrete: a kill switch is now a line item you can buy and check off, and buying it feels like doing it. The board translation: **build the cord, hold the cord, pull the cord.** The failure mode is exact: **a house that names, governs, funds, perfects and grounds its agents — and then hangs a famous, unpulled kill switch by the door, one that reaches the gateway but not the endpoints, with its far end in the vendor's hand — will pass every governance review and discover, the first time an agent runs, that it is one of the 94% standing in a loud hall with its hand closed on air.**

### Why "the stop-cord" is the discipline that matters now

For a year the worry was whether the agent was named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and grounded. **This month the market and the regulators moved the question to the brake: can you stop it?** CrowdStrike's kill switch proves the control is now a product; the HKMA's registry-and-intervention proposal proves it is becoming a supervised duty; and the 6%-in-an-hour number proves almost no one has tested it. Governance that stops at *owning* a kill switch will not halt a runaway agent. The estates that keep their standing will treat the brake like a fire drill, not a fire extinguisher bought and forgotten — written into a register, wired to reach the whole house, held in their own hand, and pulled on a schedule until the time-to-halt is a measured fact.

### How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — **be able to account for, and act on, what you run** — now sharpened to its hardest edge: *be able to stop what you run.* On a legacy estate the instinct, after a year of governance work and a procurement cycle, is to buy the famous kill switch, hang it in the architecture diagram, and move on. Under the stop-cord that is exactly the reflex to check: a purchased control is raw material, not a capability. The retrofit is **register, reach, hold, drill.
