The Chalkboard Price

Sunday, 18 October 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

Three weeks after the model commoditized, the market shows what a commoditized stove-market looks like up close — a loud fair of loss-leaders and doubling dates. Three frontier labs shipped in ten days, and the prices are designed to be misread. The question isn't which stove is cheapest. It's: are we reading the chalkboard price, or the bill?

1 · Executive Summary

The lesson moves one step down from "The Foundation": in a commoditized market, the price on the board is not the price of the meal. A ten-day burst of frontier launches arrived wearing prices designed to be misread.

1. The reality — the caravan arrived at once; the market is now a fair. In ten days: Meta shipped Muse Wonder (12 Oct) — its first frontier model, standard $4/$20 per M tokens, with a "Full" variant gated behind a $50–$200/mo Muse Code subscription and no public API; Google made Gemini 4 Argon generally available (13 Oct) at an introductory $2/$10 that doubles to $4/$20 after 30 November; and Anthropic, after Haiku 5.5 and a cache-price cut (~7 Oct), told investors Q3 revenue reached a reported $16.8B, up ~45%, at an adjusted — not GAAP — profit, ahead of a targeted $2T+ IPO (TechCrunch; eesel; InfoWorld; Reuters/FT, as reported).

2. The collision — the chalkboard price is not the bill. Gemini 4 Argon topped the Vals index at 68.90% across 41 models "using fewer tokens," yet the same index put its estimated cost at $15.68 per task; Grok 4.7's cheap $2/$6 still "threatens ROI" on token consumption (Vals; alphasignal; VentureBeat, as reported). Cost-per-task = price-per-token × tokens-per-task × retries — only the first number is on the board, and Argon's $2/$10 doubles on 30 November, a cliff on Google's calendar.

3. The answer — buy by the meal, price to the standard rate, keep the hearth hot. Compare on measured cost-per-useful-task (tokens and retries included), not price-per-token; write the business case on the post-promo rate and track every doubling/deprecation date; and keep the abstraction + live second fire that let you switch stalls when the chalk changes.

Bottom line: the foundation kept the ground yours; the chalkboard price is about reading the stall honestly once the ground is yours. A cheap, loud fair is the most expensive place in the world for a house that budgets off the biggest number on the brightest board. Shop the fair ruthlessly — it has never been cheaper. Just read the bill, not the board.

2 · Allegory — The Chalkboard Price

A great house had, at long last, got the ground right — named, booked, bonded, waged, sent to reap, funded, and lately refused to let the stove-seller pour the foundation, keeping the hearth its own so any stove could be wheeled in and out before supper. The master walked down to the fair the way a man walks who believes he has finally learned the game. And what a fair it was: the whole caravan arrived at once — three great merchants in ten days — and the prices chalked on the boards were the loveliest things he had ever seen. The long-jobs merchant had chalked a number half what the master paid now; he nearly signed. But he leaned in to read the small line beneath the big one: "Introductory — until the turn of the month. Thereafter, twice." The merchant had not lied. He had written tomorrow's price small and today's price large.

This was the first lesson: the price on the board is not the price of the meal. A travelling cook took him aside with the second: "Reckon it by the meal, not the sack. I cooked on the stove you admire — it won the fair's blind tasting on less coal, which is why its board looks cheap, and yet a full dinner cost me more, for how long it ran and how often a dish went back. No guest ever ate a sack of coal." So the careful houses did three plain things: they bought by the meal, not the board (cooking a real dinner and counting the whole cost before moving the kitchen); they refused to let another man's calendar set their budget (writing their accounts against the doubled price from day one, and keeping an almanac of every merchant's dates); and they kept the hearth hot (so walking to the next stall was a Tuesday, not a ransom).

For the noise and the cheapness were not generosity. The largest merchant had just told his backers he had, for the first time, turned a profit — and in the same breath priced a new stove to be given away, because the prize was not this month's coal-money but next year's kitchens, and the lock a kitchen built around one stall becomes. A cheap fair is the most expensive place in the world for a house that cannot read a board or move a kitchen.

The question it forces: The fair has never been cheaper or louder — three stoves in ten days. But are we budgeting off the chalkboard or the bill? Do we know what one finished task actually costs us on each model, or only the per-token price chalked largest? Did we build the case on the price that doubles on 30 November, or the price we'll pay in December — and if the chalk turns dear overnight, can we walk to the next stall by Tuesday?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Chalkboard Price

The chalkboard price is the one number everyone quotes and almost no one validates: price-per-token — and a ten-day burst of launches made its decoupling from cost-per-task impossible to ignore. The sticker is engineered to be read; the bill is engineered to be discovered later; so measure the bill before you sign the sticker. The reality: capability has clustered and the differentiation moved to price structure ($4/$20, an intro $2/$10, a subscription tier), so the question is "cheaper for my task, at my token profile, after the promotion ends?" — and a subscription-gated model with no API (Wonder Full) is not an enterprise component. The collision: cost-per-task = price-per-token × tokens-per-task × (1 + retries), and only the first factor is on the board; some cheap stickers (Argon's $2/$10) have an expiry you don't control. The answer: stand up a cost-per-task harness on your real workloads, price every case to the standard rate and track the cliffs, and keep the abstraction + live second fire so a price change is a routing decision, not a migration.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Buy by the meal, not the board. The headline price-per-token has decoupled from the bill (Argon topped Vals "using fewer tokens" yet ran ~$15.68/task; Grok 4.7's cheap $2/$6 "threatens ROI"). Run your top three workloads on each candidate and report measured cost-per-finished-task — tokens, retries and latency included — then make that number, not the pricing-page sticker, the one procurement compares.

2. Never let a doubling date on another man's calendar set your margin. Argon's $2/$10 doubles to $4/$20 on 30 November. Re-base every AI business case on the standard rate, treat any intro discount as a temporary rebate, and build an almanac of every provider's promo-expiry, price-step and deprecation dates — each a scheduled risk event with an owner.

3. Keep the hearth hot. A cheap, crowded fair only rewards the house that can switch. Confirm your top workloads can be routed to a second model behind your own gateway, and run one real cut-over driven by cost-per-task — switch a workload to the genuinely cheapest model per outcome and measure what it saved and what broke. Report the time-to-switch and the cost-per-task saving.
