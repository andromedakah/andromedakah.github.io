The Loss Leader

Thursday, 19 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

The floor-owner won't fight you on the stove — it will give the stove away. At Microsoft Ignite (17 Nov), a near-frontier model at a twentieth of the cost: cheap, genuinely good, and cheap only inside its own house. A fire sold below cost at the door is the oldest way to pour a foundation under you. Price the bundle, not the stove — then own the floor, keep the larder, light a second fire.

1 · Executive Summary

Two editions ago the model became a cheap, swappable stove and the lock-in moved to the foundation. This morning the floor-owners reveal the counter-move: stop fighting on the stove, give the stove away — to win the floor.

1. The news — the foundation-owner sells the fire below cost. At Microsoft Ignite (17 Nov 2026), Microsoft debuted a reported six first-party MAI models (coding, cybersecurity, image, voice); MAI Code 2 is claimed at ~98% of GPT-6.1 Sol at ~40% of the price and ~96% of Claude Opus 5.5 at ~5% of the cost, available in GitHub Copilot, with a cyber model scoring 92.3% vs GPT-6 Astra's 91.5% (Axios; Decrypt; ITPro; Windows Report, as reported — promotional pricing, vendor benchmarks). "Good enough" is now a shipping product, sold by the floor-owner.

2. The trap — cheap only inside one house is an entrance fee, not a bargain. A 95%-off fire that burns cheaply only when plumbed into the vendor's flue (Copilot, Azure, Foundry) is a loss leader for the foundation, not a swappable stove — and it is how the "only 6% could switch cleanly" figure is manufactured, one irresistible discount at a time (Zapier 2026: 81% fear vendor dependency, 47% a function stops on an outage/price change, only 6% confident of a clean switch).

3. The move — price the bundle, not the stove; then own, keep, light. Add the flue, woodpile, chimney and exit cost before you take the discount; put the cheap model behind an abstraction you own, keep your data and evals in your own larder, and keep a second fire lit — so the dividend ("good enough at a fraction of the cost") is a gift you collect, not bait you swallow.

Bottom line: the foundation taught you the model is a swappable stove and the lock-in lives in the floor. This morning the floor-owner will give the stove away — ~96% of the best model at ~5% of the cost, in GitHub Copilot — because a fire sold below cost at the door is the cheapest way ever devised to pour a foundation under a house. The fine print is not the benchmark or the promotional pricing; it is the word in. The 94% who can't move didn't get there by being gouged — they got there one wonderful bargain at a time. Collect the dividend ruthlessly; but never let the stove-seller give you the fire for free in exchange for your foundation.

2 · Allegory — The Loss Leader

A wise house had learned the hardest lesson — the fire was a rented, swappable stove, and what truly held it was the foundation — so it had built its own hearth, kept its own larder, and lit a second fire. The merchant, who had watched the master grow wise, tried the old trick of a bigger stove and got a shrug. So he came back with something cleverer: a small one, and a number that stopped the kitchen — "nineteen-twentieths of the warmth of the grandest fire in the valley, for one part in twenty of the cost."

The master walked around it and found it genuinely fine — the warmth real, the price real, no trick in the fire at all. And that was the danger, because the trick was never in the fire. The bargain price held only while the stove drew its wood from the merchant's woodpile, vented up his chimney, and sat on his floor. Unplumb it and the price evaporated. It was the grocer's loss leader — butter below cost at the door so the whole week's shop happens inside — now aimed at the foundation. The master had spent a year learning to guard against being gouged; he had not thought to guard against being delighted.

So the wise houses kept the three disciplines and added a fourth, cut to the shape of the loss leader: price the bundle, not the stove. Write down not the price of the fire but the price of everything it commits you to — the woodpile, the chimney, the floor, and the wagons to ever leave. And remember: the 94 houses in 100 that could not move were not robbed. Every one got there the pleasant way — one wonderful bargain at a time, each individually sensible. The loss leader does not overcome your judgment; it recruits it.

The question it forces: The cheapest near-frontier fire just arrived at a twentieth of the cost — and it's genuinely good. But is it cheap everywhere, or only inside the house of the company that also owns our floor? Have we priced the bundle, or only the stove? Will we take this real discount the wise way — onto a hearth we own, larder in our own pantry, a second fire lit — or the pleasant way the other 94 houses took it, one irresistible bargain at a time?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Loss Leader

The thing that commoditized (the model) is not the thing that locks you in (the foundation). The loss leader is the countermove to "own the floor": give the stove away — near-frontier quality at a fraction of the cost — on the unstated condition that it only burns cheaply on the seller's floor. The news: Microsoft MAI at Ignite — the discount is real and the quality is real, and that is exactly why it's dangerous; "good enough" is now a product category, not a compromise, but the cheapest path to it is the seller's platform, so the saving and the lock-in arrive in the same box. The trap: a 95%-off rate that evaporates the moment you route through an abstraction you own, or to a second vendor, is not a cheaper stove but an entrance fee to the seller's floor — it buys back the lock-in commoditization gave away, and the real cost moves off the per-token line onto the bundle (platform + data gravity + orchestration + exit). The answer: price the bundle, not the per-token headline; then take the saving only onto a floor you own, keep your larder portable, and keep a second source lit so the discount is a lever, not a leash.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Price the bundle, not the stove. The headline is near-frontier quality at ~5% of the cost (Microsoft MAI) — real, and worth having. For every "good enough at a fraction of the cost" model, require a one-page bundle price: the per-token rate plus the platform it only runs cheaply on, the data you'd move into it, the orchestration you'd wire around it, and the measured cost of exiting — and put that number, not the discount, to the decision. Report the share of discounted-AI decisions made on bundle price versus headline price.

2. Own the floor before you take the saving. The discount captures value only if switching stays cheap; a price conditioned on the seller's platform buys the lock-in back. Ensure any near-frontier-cheap model you adopt plugs into a gateway/abstraction you own, with your data, prompts and evals kept portable and never moved into the vendor's platform merely to unlock the rate. Report the share of cheap-model spend behind an abstraction you own versus wired into the seller's floor.

3. Light a second fire so the bargain stays a choice. A discount you can't walk away from is a leash; the 6% who could switch cleanly kept a live alternate. Pick one workload you've moved onto a cheap vendor model and stand up a qualified second source running it on ground you own — measure the time-and-cost to route to it — so the discount is a lever, not a commitment. Report the measured time-to-switch and a dated plan to extend it.
