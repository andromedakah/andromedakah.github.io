## 1 · Executive Summary (90-second read)

Two months ago the radar turned from the servant to the ground the house stands on, and named the discipline of the foundation: the model had commoditized into a cheap, swappable stove, so **own the floor, keep the larder, light a second fire.** This morning the market reveals the counter-move the foundation-owners had ready all along, and it is cleverer than a price war. **They have stopped trying to win on the stove and started giving the stove away — to win the floor.**

**The news — the foundation-owner now sells the fire below cost.** At **Microsoft Ignite on 17 November 2026**, Microsoft unveiled a family of its own first-party **MAI models** — a reported six, spanning coding, cybersecurity, image and voice — and pushed hard on one message: *smaller and cheaper can beat the frontier.* Its claim for the coding model, **MAI Code 2**, is deliberately eye-watering: **~98% of GPT-6.1 Sol's performance at ~40% of the price, and ~96% of Claude Opus 5.5's performance at ~5% of the cost**, available **in GitHub Copilot**; its cybersecurity model is pitched at **"world-class performance at ~50% of the cost of leading models"** and scored a reported **92.3% against GPT-6 Astra's 91.5%** on a vulnerability-finding benchmark (Axios — "Microsoft debuts six AI models as it pushes toward frontier"; Decrypt; ITPro; Windows Report, as reported). Decrypt's caveat rides with it: the most dramatic price comparisons **use promotional pricing**, and the benchmarks are **Microsoft's own**. Strip the showmanship and the signal is real and important: **"good enough" at a fraction of the cost is now a shipping product, and the company shipping it also owns the floor you'd run it on.**

**The trap — a stove that is cheap only inside one house is an entrance fee, not a bargain.** The radar has said for two editions that cheap, swappable intelligence is *good news you must actually collect* — and you collect it only by owning the floor the model plugs into. The loss leader is the elegant attack on exactly that discipline. A 95%-off fire that burns cheaply **only when it is plumbed into Microsoft's flue (GitHub Copilot, Azure, Foundry), fed from its woodpile and vented up its chimney** is not a swappable stove at all; it is the cheapest, most charming way yet to pour a foundation under you. This is the grocer's oldest move — milk below cost at the door so the whole week's shop happens inside — now aimed at the AI floor, and it works *because* the discount is genuine. The prior rail still reads the same way from the outside: a Zapier 2026 survey reports **81% of enterprise leaders worried about AI-vendor dependency, 47% saying a key function would stop on a primary-vendor outage or price change, and only 6% confident they could switch their primary provider without material disruption** (as reported). The loss leader is how the 6% is manufactured — one irresistible discount at a time.

**The move — price the bundle, not the stove; then own, keep, light.** The board translation is one new rule bolted onto the three you already have. **Price the bundle, not the stove:** before you take any near-frontier model at a fraction of the cost, add up the flue, the woodpile, the chimney and the cost of ever leaving — the per-token discount is the smallest number in the deal, and the cheapest fire in the valley is often the dearest house. Then the three disciplines hold and sharpen: **own the floor** so even the bargain fire plugs into an abstraction *you* built, not the vendor's platform; **keep the larder** so your data, prompts and evals never move into the vendor's woodshed to qualify for the discount; **light a second fire** so the bargain stays a *choice* you can walk away from, not a leash. And — the genuinely good news — **collect the dividend**: "good enough at a twentieth of the cost" is real, and routed behind a floor you own it is a gift; plumbed into a floor the seller owns it is bait.

## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Loss Leader"

*Topic: The foundation-owners have stopped fighting the stove war on size and started giving the stove away to win the floor war. At Microsoft Ignite on 17 November 2026, Microsoft unveiled a family of first-party MAI models and claimed its coding model, MAI Code 2, delivers a reported ~98% of GPT-6.1 Sol's performance at ~40% of the price and ~96% of Claude Opus 5.5's performance at ~5% of the cost — available in GitHub Copilot — with a cybersecurity model scoring 92.3% to GPT-6 Astra's 91.5% and pitched at "world-class performance at ~50% of the cost of leading models" (Axios; Decrypt; ITPro; Windows Report, as reported; Decrypt notes promotional pricing and Microsoft's own benchmarks). Read against the "Foundation" edition — the model is a cheap, swappable stove; the lock-in lives in the floor — the cheap-model wave is the loss leader: the fire sold below cost to pull the house onto the seller's foundation. The loss-leader allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There was a great house that, after a long and careful year, had learned the hardest lesson of all: that the fire it cooked on was a **rented, swappable stove** — cheap, plentiful, interchangeable before supper — and that the thing which truly held it was not the stove but the **foundation** the whole house stood on. So the master had resolved the three right things. He had built his own **hearth**, so that any stove plugged into it and could be pulled out again. He had kept his **larder** — his grain, his recipes, the tests by which he knew good cooking from bad — in his own pantry, portable, never in another man's cellar. And he had kept a **second fire** actually lit, a real flame in a second grate, so that the morning his first stove went cold he would still serve dinner. It was, by any fair reckoning, a wise house, and the master slept better for it.

Now the merchant who sold the fires was no fool, and he had watched the master grow wise. He had tried, that autumn, the old trick of wheeling in a *bigger* stove — a great brass thing, half again the size of last season's — and he had watched the cook glance at it and shrug, because fire was fire and a bigger one at the same price impressed no one anymore. So the merchant went home and thought, and came back with something far cleverer than a bigger stove. He wheeled in a *small* one — plain, half the size of the master's finest — and before the master could shrug, he spoke the number. *"This little stove,"* he said, *"gives you all but a whisker of the warmth of the grandest fire in the valley — nineteen parts in twenty of it — and I will let you have that warmth for **one part in twenty** of what the grand fire costs. Not half. Not a quarter. A twentieth."* And the whole kitchen went quiet, because that is a number that stops a kitchen.

The master was no fool either, and he had done the year's work, so he did not say yes at once. He walked around the little stove, and he found — as the merchant knew he would — that it was a genuinely fine stove. The warmth was real. The twentieth-of-the-cost was real. There was no trick in the fire at all. And that was precisely the danger, because **the trick was never in the fire.** The master crouched to see how the little stove was plumbed, and there it was, so quietly built he had almost missed it: the bargain price held *only* while the stove drew its wood from the merchant's own woodpile, and vented its smoke up the merchant's own chimney, and sat upon the merchant's own floor. Unplumb it from the merchant's house — carry it to the master's own hearth, the one he had built so carefully — and the wonderful price evaporated like morning smoke. The fire was a twentieth of the cost, yes. But only *inside the merchant's house.*

And the master understood, crouched there on the floor, that he was looking at the oldest trick in the market, dressed in new brass. It was the grocer's loss leader: the churn of butter sold at the door for less than the grocer paid for it, not because the grocer had grown generous overnight, but because a man who comes in for cheap butter stays to buy his flour, his salt, his cloth and his candles, and goes home having spent his whole week's wage under one roof. The merchant had stopped trying to sell the master a *better* fire. He had started trying to give the master a fire *for almost nothing* — because a fire given away at the door is the cheapest, most charming, most welcome way ever devised to **pour a foundation under a house.** The master had spent a year learning to guard against being gouged. He had not thought to guard against being *delighted.*

Two roads, as always. The easy road ran straight and downhill and smelled of savings: take the little stove, and the three next like it, and the one after that — for who in his right mind turns down nineteen-twentieths of the warmth at a twentieth of the price? — and wake one morning to find that his kitchen drew *all* its wood from one woodpile, vented *all* its smoke up one chimney, and that the hearth he had built so carefully stood cold in the corner because nothing cheap would ever plug into it again. The wiser road, which the careful houses took, began with a cold question the loss leader is designed to make you skip: *not "how cheap is this fire?" but "what does this fire cost once I price the woodpile, the chimney and the floor it only burns on — and the wagons I would need to hire on the day I wanted to leave?"* And when the careful houses did that sum honestly, the twentieth-of-the-cost fire was very often the **dearest thing in the valley**, because its true price was not paid in coin at the counter but in the slow surrender of the ground beneath their feet.

So the wise houses kept the three disciplines they had learned — **own the hearth, keep the larder, light a second fire** — and they added a fourth, cut exactly to the shape of the loss leader. **Price the bundle, not the stove.** When a merchant offers you a fire for a twentieth of its worth, do not write down the price of the fire; write down the price of *everything the cheap fire quietly commits you to* — the woodpile you must now buy only from him, the chimney you must now vent only through him, the floor your whole house now rests on, and the cost, in wagons and razed walls, of ever moving off it. Add that sum, and *then* decide. Sometimes the fire is still worth it — a cheap, genuinely good stove plugged into a hearth *you* own, fed from a woodpile *you* keep, is a gift, and the master took several such gifts gladly. But a cheap fire you may only burn on another man's floor is not a gift and not even really a stove. It is a toll-gate, and the toll is your foundation, and the merchant has merely found the one way to make you *thank him* as you pay it.

Here is the part that should keep a master awake. The ninety-four houses in a hundred that could not move — the ones who lay awake over their dependence, the ones who knew a whole room would go dark the day the merchant stumbled — **not one of them got there by being robbed.** No merchant ever kicked in their door and poured a foundation against their will. Every one of them got there the pleasant way: one wonderful bargain at a time, each individually sensible, each a genuine saving, each so obviously worth taking that to refuse it would have looked like leaving money on the counter. That is the terrible efficiency of the loss leader. It does not overcome your judgment; it **recruits** it. The thriftier and shrewder the master, the more surely a fire at a twentieth of the cost pulls him across the merchant's threshold — until the day he reaches for a different woodpile and finds he has forgotten the way to anyone's door but this one.

**The moral, stated plainly:** the model is a rented stove, now so cheap that the foundation-owner will sell it to you *below cost* to pull you onto his floor; the cheap fire is a loss leader, and a fire that is cheap only inside one house is an entrance fee to that house written as a discount; your knowledge is the larder; the hearth is the floor you own; the second fire is the fallback you have actually lit; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks for the roll; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising servant eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; the foundation is why you own the ground the house stands on — and **the loss leader is why, having resolved to own the floor, you must now price the whole bundle and not the stove: add the woodpile, the chimney, the floor and the cost of leaving before you take the wonderful discount, take the cheap fire only onto a hearth you own with your larder still in your own pantry and a second fire actually lit, and remember that the 94% who cannot move did not get there by being gouged — they got there one irresistible bargain at a time.** A house that lets the stove-seller give it the fire for free has not driven a hard bargain; it has sold its foundation at the counter and been handed a saving as the receipt.

**The question it forces:** *The cheapest near-frontier fire in the valley just arrived at a twentieth of the cost — and it is genuinely good. But is it cheap everywhere, or only inside the house of the company that also owns our floor? Have we priced the bundle — the woodpile, the chimney, the floor and the wagons we'd hire to leave — or only the stove? Will we take this real, wonderful discount the wise way (onto a hearth we own, larder in our own pantry, a second fire lit) — or the pleasant way the other ninety-four houses took it, one irresistible bargain at a time, until the day we reach for another woodpile and find we have forgotten every door but this one?*

## 3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

### 🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)

- **Have we priced the bundle, or only the stove?** Microsoft's Ignite claim is a near-frontier coding model at ~5% of Claude Opus 5.5's cost — genuinely cheap, and genuinely good. **Before we adopt any such model, have we costed the *whole* commitment — the platform it only runs cheaply on (GitHub Copilot, Azure, Foundry), the data we'd have to move into it, and the price in time and money of ever leaving — or have we written down only the per-token discount, which is the smallest number in the deal?**
- **Is this a cheaper stove, or an entrance fee to someone's foundation?** **For each "good enough at a fraction of the cost" model we're tempted by, is the low price available behind an abstraction *we* own and portable to a second vendor — or does the discount hold only inside the seller's proprietary platform, so that taking it wires us one notch deeper into a floor we can't leave?**
- **Are we collecting the commoditization dividend without swallowing the bait?** The good news is real: "good enough" is now a twentieth of the cost. **Are we routing cheap, capable models per task behind a gateway we control — taking the saving onto our own floor — or are we taking the saving *as* the seller's floor, one irresistible bargain at a time, the exact pleasant path by which only 6% of firms end up able to switch cleanly (Zapier 2026)?**

### 🏦 Financial Services

- A 95%-off model that runs cheaply only inside one platform is a concentration risk dressed as a cost saving. **Can we show our board and our regulator a bundle-priced view of every discounted AI service — not the headline per-token rate, but the platform dependency, data gravity and exit cost it commits us to — and a tested exit for each one we've adopted on price?**
- Microsoft pitched a cyber model at "~50% of the cost of leading models" scoring 92.3% on vulnerability-finding (its own benchmark). **For security-critical AI we buy on a cost-and-benchmark claim, have we independently validated the benchmark on our own data, and confirmed the model and its guardrails are portable — or are we hard-wiring defence into one vendor's floor because the price was irresistible?**

### 🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences

- Cheap, "good enough" models are most tempting for high-volume clinical and administrative work — exactly where validation and portability matter most. **When we adopt a near-frontier model at a fraction of the cost, do our validated workflows and evals travel with us to a second supplier, or does the discount quietly require our clinical data and context to live inside the seller's platform — the larder moved into his cellar to earn the saving?**
- The loss leader recruits the thrifty. **Do our cost-reduction targets reward taking the cheapest model regardless of where it locks us, or do they require the total-bundle price — platform, data residency, exit — so that a 95% saving that surrenders our foundation is scored as the expense it actually is?**

### 🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials

- You already know a component priced below cost usually carries a captive-consumables or single-source catch. **Have we applied the same procurement discipline to "good enough at a twentieth of the cost" AI — demanding the total cost of ownership including lock-in and exit — or are we evaluating AI models on headline price in a way we would never tolerate for a bearing or a controller?**
- Dual-sourcing is second nature here. **For any cheap model we adopt, do we keep a qualified second source that runs the same workload on ground we own — so the discount is a negotiating lever, not a leash — or does the bargain price depend on single-sourcing onto the seller's platform?**

### 🛒 Retail / Consumer

- Your margins love a 95%-off inference bill — and that is exactly the appetite the loss leader is built to exploit. **Are we capturing cheap inference behind an abstraction we own (so we can keep arbitraging the cheapest capable model per task), or are we taking the cheapest price *as* a platform commitment that will cost us far more than the saving the day we want to route elsewhere?**
- Loss leaders work because each bargain is individually sensible. **Across the dozen "too good to refuse" AI deals we've signed this year, have we totted up the *cumulative* platform concentration they add to — or has each one looked cheap in isolation while together they've quietly poured most of a foundation?**

### 🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated

- Below-cost pricing that wins the floor is a procurement and sovereignty question, not just a budget win. **Under the duties you already owe (EU AI Act, with the Article 50(2) marking obligation and new prohibitions due 2 December 2026, as reported), can we show that no critical public service has been drawn onto a single vendor's platform by a discount, with no tested, affordable exit — and that our data and models remain portable by design?**
- Citizens' services outlive procurement cycles. **Are we taking cheap, capable models onto ground we own (open weights where fit, portable data, an abstraction layer we control) — or are we letting a twentieth-of-the-cost offer pour a single-vendor foundation under services that must still run, and still be movable, in a decade?**

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Foundation to the Loss Leader

Two editions ago the distinction was drawn and it still holds: **the thing that has commoditized (the model) is not the thing that locks you in (the foundation).** The "Foundation" edition said to collect the good news — cheap, swappable intelligence — by owning the floor the model plugs into. This week the foundation-owners revealed the countermove built precisely to defeat that discipline, and it is not a price war on the stove. **It is the loss leader: give the stove away — near-frontier quality at a fraction of the cost — on the condition, usually unstated, that it only burns cheaply on the seller's floor.** Microsoft's Ignite launch is the move in the open: a family of first-party **MAI** models whose headline is "smaller and cheaper beats the frontier," whose flagship coding model is claimed at **~96% of Claude Opus 5.5 at ~5% of the cost and ~98% of GPT-6.1 Sol at ~40% of the price**, and whose distribution is **GitHub Copilot** — the seller's own platform. The point is blunt: **the discount is real, the quality is real, and that is exactly why it is dangerous — a loss leader does not overcome your judgment, it recruits it.**

- **The news — the foundation-owner is selling the fire below cost, and the fire is good.** **At Microsoft Ignite (17 Nov 2026), Microsoft debuted a reported six first-party MAI models (coding, cybersecurity, image, voice); MAI Code 2 is claimed at ~98% of GPT-6.1 Sol at ~40% of the price and ~96% of Claude Opus 5.5 at ~5% of the cost, available in GitHub Copilot, and a cyber model scored 92.3% vs GPT-6 Astra's 91.5% on a vulnerability-finding test, pitched at "~50% of the cost of leading models" (Axios; Decrypt; ITPro; Windows Report, as reported).** Three engineering points follow. First, **the quality claim is plausible and the price claim is partly promotional** — Decrypt flags that the most dramatic comparisons use *promotional* pricing and the benchmarks are Microsoft's own, so the durable number is "near-frontier at materially lower cost," not literally "5%." Second, **"good enough" is now a product category, not a compromise** — for a large share of enterprise workloads (routine coding, triage, classification, high-volume agent steps), a model at near-frontier quality and a fraction of the cost is the *rational* default, and that is genuinely good news. Third, **the distribution is the catch** — the cheapest path to that model is the seller's platform, so the saving and the lock-in arrive in the same box.
- **The trap — a price that holds only inside one platform is a lock-in mechanism, not a market price.** **A model is a swappable component only if its economics survive being moved; a 95%-off rate that evaporates the moment you route the workload through an abstraction you own, or to a second vendor, is not a cheaper stove but an entrance fee to the seller's floor.** Two realities cut against the reflex to just take the deal. **First, the loss leader attacks the "own the floor" discipline directly.** The whole value of commoditization is captured only if switching is cheap; a discount conditioned on *not* abstracting away from the vendor is engineered to make switching expensive again — it buys back the lock-in the commoditization gave away. **Second, the cost moves off the per-token line and onto the bundle.** The real price of a below-cost model is the platform you must adopt, the data gravity you accumulate inside it, the proprietary orchestration you wire around it, and the exit cost you're quietly signing up for — none of which appears on the quote. The prior rail still reads true from outside: **81% worried about vendor dependency, 47% a function stops on an outage or price change, only 6% confident of a clean switch (Zapier 2026, as reported)** — and the loss leader is the single most effective manufacturing process for that 6%, precisely because every step toward it is a genuine saving.
- **The answer — price the bundle, then own, keep, light.** **Before adopting any near-frontier-at-a-fraction model, compute the total-bundle cost (platform dependency + data gravity + proprietary orchestration + exit cost), not the per-token headline; then take the saving *only* onto a floor you own — route the cheap model behind a gateway/abstraction you control, keep your data, prompts and evals in a portable form (never moved into the vendor's platform to qualify for the price), and keep a qualified second source lit so the discount is a lever, not a leash.** This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — *be able to account for, and act on, what you run* — now pointed at the sharpest commercial trick in the market: not what the model costs per token, but what the *bundle* costs you in freedom, and whether you can still walk out the door.

The strategic core: **the commoditization dividend is real and worth collecting — "good enough" at a fraction of the cost is a genuine gift — but the foundation-owners have learned to hand you that gift in a box whose lid is your floor.** The failure mode is exact and it is *not* the obvious one: it is not the firm that overpays, it is the shrewd, cost-disciplined firm that takes a dozen individually-sensible 90%-off deals and wakes to find its whole estate runs on one platform it can no longer leave. The board translation: **price the bundle, not the stove; take the cheap fire only onto a floor you own.** A house that congratulates itself on a 95% saving while that saving quietly re-pours its foundation has not been thrifty — it has bought the most expensive thing in the market and been handed a discount as the receipt.

### Why "the loss leader" is the discipline that matters now

The "Foundation" edition told you to collect cheap, swappable intelligence by owning the floor it plugs into. The loss leader is the foundation-owners' answer: **make the intelligence cheap only on their floor.** Microsoft's Ignite numbers — near-frontier quality at a fraction of the cost, distributed through GitHub Copilot — are that answer stated plainly, and the trap is not in the benchmark or the promotional pricing but in the word *in*. Governance that stops at "is the model cheap and capable?" will wave the bargain straight through, because the bargain is genuine. The estates that keep their standing will ask the one question the discount is designed to make them skip — *cheap where, and movable at what cost?* — and will take the real, wonderful saving only onto ground they own.

### How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — **be able to account for, and act on, what you run** — now sharpened to *be able to price what a bargain truly costs you, and to walk away from it.* On a legacy estate the loss leader is nearly irresistible, because the pressure to cut AI spend is real and the discount is enormous and the easiest way to claim it is to adopt the seller's platform wholesale. That is exactly the reflex to check: a saving that re-pours your foundation is not a saving. The retrofit is **price, then own, keep, light. Price** the bundle: for every discounted model, cost the platform, the data gravity, the orchestration and the exit, and put that total — not the per-token rate — in front of the board. **Own** the floor: route the cheap model behind an abstraction you control, so the saving is yours to keep when you switch. **Keep** the larder: never move your data, prompts and evals into the vendor's platform merely to unlock the price. **Light** a second fire: keep a qualified alternate running the same workload, so the discount is a lever you pull, not a leash on your neck.
