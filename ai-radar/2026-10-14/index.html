The Colleague

A year of work governed the agent as a tool. This week the vendors reclassified it as a colleague: Google's enterprise Gemini agent arrives with its own email, calendar, Drive and a seat in your company directory — assignable like a teammate. You can hire the colleague. Can you fire it?
Wednesday, 14 October 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering

This week, on 8 October 2026 at "Gemini at Work," Google unveiled an enterprise Gemini agent that arrives with its own workplace identity — a dedicated email address (reported @agents.company.com), a Workspace account with calendar and Drive, and a seat in your company directory, so a human can assign it work like a teammate — governed by an "Agent Gateway" with attested identities, spend caps and per-action audit (The Verge; AIWeekly; TechCrunch, as reported). It is not alone: Anthropic's Claude Frontier Academy (2 Oct) commits $100M to embed 10,000 engineers inside client firms, and Meta's Muse Wonder (12 Oct) gives the colleague "Active Memory." The board question is no longer can we govern the tool? — it is: the agency just gave our newest hire a nameplate, a pigeonhole and a chair in the hall; who is its manager, and who walks it to the door on the day it leaves? Because agents already outnumber humans ~82:1 to ~109:1, non-human-identity governance sits at ~21%, and ~78% of organizations have no documented policy for creating or removing an agent identity (Rubrik; Palo Alto; VentureBeat Q3-2026, as reported). The vendor will onboard your colleague for you. The offboarding it leaves to you — and almost no one has written it.

1 · Executive Summary 90-second read

For a long year the radar treated the agent as a tool to be governed — named (the livery), booked (the steward), bonded (the surety), wage-checked (the second wage), sent to reap (the two harvests), funded (the purse), and stood on ground you own (the foundation). This week the category changed under the question. The vendors stopped shipping a smarter tool and started shipping a colleague: an agent that arrives pre-fitted with the furniture of employment — an identity, an inbox, a calendar, a place in the directory — so your people can hand it work the way they hand work to a co-worker.

The development — the agent is issued the furniture of employment. On 8 October 2026, at "Gemini at Work," Google unveiled an enterprise Gemini agent with its own workplace identity: a dedicated email address (reported @agents.company.com), a Workspace account including calendar and Drive storage, and a seat in the company directory so colleagues can assign it work like a teammate — all governed through an "Agent Gateway" (attested agent identities, policy enforcement, spend caps, per-action audit trails), with the agent able to route across Anthropic's Claude plus proprietary and open-weight models (The Verge; AIWeekly — "Gemini Agent Gets a Mailbox and Directory Seat"; TechCrunch; redreamality, as reported; currently private preview). It is a pattern, not a one-off: Anthropic's Claude Frontier Academy (2 Oct) commits $100M to place 10,000 trained engineers inside customer and partner firms by end-2027 — the human colleague embedded alongside the digital one — and Meta's Muse Wonder / Wonder Full (12 Oct) leads on "Active Memory," a model that remembers you across conversations. The through-line: the agent now has a face, a name, a memory and a mailbox.

The gap — you can hire it, but almost no one can fire it. The furniture of employment comes with employment's hardest duty: the day the colleague leaves. And the lifecycle data says that duty is unwritten. AI-agent and other non-human identities already outnumber humans ~82:1 (Rubrik Zero Labs) to ~109:1 (Palo Alto Networks 2026 Identity Security Landscape, ~79 agent identities per human); non-human-identity governance sits at ~21% adoption; and ~78% of organizations have no documented policy for creating or removing an agent identity (Rubrik; VentureBeat Q3-2026 survey of 800 IT leaders, as reported). Microsoft's Entra Agent ID (GA since April 2026) at least points at the pattern — reportedly reassigning an agent to its sponsor's manager when that human leaves — but the base rate is stark: the onboarding is done for you by the vendor; the offboarding is left to you, and four firms in five have not written it. A colleague you can hire but never fire is not a colleague. It is a tenant with a key to every room — and now, with an inbox of its own, a standing phishing target and a data-exfiltration path that never resigns.

The stakes — put the colleague on your joiner-mover-leaver process, offboarding runbook first. The move re-uses the whole year's discipline and points it at the employee lifecycle. First, onboard it like an employee, on identity rails you own — every agent gets a human sponsor (a real manager), a scoped role, and an entry in your joiner-mover-leaver (JML) process, so your directory, not the vendor's, is the system of record. Second, write the offboarding runbook before the inbox is issued — one place to revoke, a defined action on the sponsor's departure, a way to seal the mailbox/calendar/Drive, and a timed, tested deprovisioning; the 78% is the tell. Third, treat the inbox as attack surface — a colleague with a mailbox and standing cross-app access is a phishing target and an exfil path, so apply the DLP, least-privilege and monitoring you would apply to a new human hire with broad access. And do it now, in the private-preview window — because the week Europe's goalposts moved (the Digital Omnibus deferred standalone high-risk duties to 2 December 2027) is precisely the week it feels safe to let the colleague move in before the paperwork is done.

2 · Allegory of the Day

🖋️ "The Colleague"

Topic: This week the frame turns from the tool you govern to the colleague you employ. On 8 October 2026, at "Gemini at Work," Google unveiled an enterprise Gemini agent that arrives with its own workplace identity — a dedicated email address (reported @agents.company.com), a Workspace account with calendar and Drive, and a seat in the company directory, assignable like a teammate, governed by an "Agent Gateway" (The Verge; AIWeekly; TechCrunch, as reported) — the same fortnight Anthropic's Claude Frontier Academy (2 Oct) committed $100M to embed 10,000 engineers inside client firms and Meta's Muse Wonder (12 Oct) led on "Active Memory." Yet agents outnumber humans ~82:1 to ~109:1, non-human-identity governance sits at ~21%, and ~78% of organizations have no documented policy for creating or removing an agent identity (Rubrik; Palo Alto; VentureBeat Q3-2026, as reported). The colleague allegory is the radar's own illustration.

There was a great house that had spent a long and careful year making its servants excellent. It had named every hand and cut each a scoped key, kept one honest book, let the master's own name stand surety for the work, asked whose second coin each adviser took, sent the governed servants to reap, handed each a scoped purse, and refused to let the company that laid its floor pour a foundation it could never leave. By any fair reckoning it was a well-run house, and the master was proud — as he should have been. In all that year, though, he had thought of the servants as tools he governed: clever implements he could name, scope, and set down.

Then, this week, the agency that supplied his servants changed what it delivered. A clerk arrived and, with great ceremony, did for the newest servant what no one had ever done for a tool. He hung a nameplate on a door. He cut the servant a pigeonhole in the hall, where letters would arrive addressed to it by name. He gave it a diary of its own, so the household could book its hours. And he wrote the servant's name into the house roll, between two human hands, so that anyone in the house might walk up and say, "Here — this is yours, see it done," exactly as they would to a living colleague. "No longer a tool," the clerk said warmly; "a member of the household. Assign it work as you would any other." And he left — before anyone thought to ask the question that matters about a member of a household.

The master was delighted, at first, and rightly so. A colleague who never tired, never forgot a thing it had been told, and sat ready in the hall to take any errand — what house would not want one? He assigned it work, and it worked. He sent it letters, and it answered them. Its memory was perfect; it recalled every instruction from every day. The house ran smoother than it ever had. This was not a defeat. This was the master getting the diligent, tireless colleague he had always wished for.

But here is the question the clerk had not waited to be asked, and that the master only thought of weeks later, lying awake. A colleague is not a tool you can simply set down. A colleague has a nameplate, a pigeonhole, a diary, a place on the roll — and a colleague, one day, leaves. Reassigned, replaced by a better model, caught in a mistake, or quietly compromised by a letter in that open pigeonhole. And when that day came — the master realised with a cold feeling — he had no idea who would walk this colleague to the door. Who takes back its key. Who empties its pigeonhole and stops the letters. Who strikes its name from the roll. Who tells it, and the house, that it is no longer a hand here. He had an elaborate ceremony for hiring the colleague — the agency had performed it for him. He had nothing at all for letting it go.

Two roads, as always. The easy road was to enjoy the tireless colleague and not spoil the mood with morbid questions about its departure — the agency was competent, the nameplate handsome, and why borrow trouble? The wiser road, which the careful houses took, began with a cold count of the hall. And when they counted, the arithmetic frightened them. For every living hand in the house there were now eighty, a hundred of these named colleagues — pigeonholes stuffed with letters, diaries full, keys cut — and when the careful houses asked how many kept any written rule for how such a colleague is taken on and, above all, let go, the answer was: fewer than one house in four governed them at all, and nearly four houses in five had never written down how a single one is hired or fired. That is not a cupboard of tools you can tidy at leisure. That is a hall full of colleagues no one has worked out how to dismiss — and a hall where a colleague is never dismissed is a hall where no colleague ever truly leaves.

So the wise houses learned three plain things, and they cost far less to do before the nameplate went up than after. The first: hire the colleague onto your own roll, with a living sponsor. Do not let the agency's book be the record of who works here — keep your own, and give every named colleague a human master who answers for it, a scoped place, and an entry in the same joiner-and-leaver ledger every living hand is kept in. A colleague whose only record is in the agency's hall is a colleague you do not truly employ. The second: write how it leaves before you write how it arrives. Before you cut the pigeonhole, settle who takes the key back, what happens the day its sponsor departs, how the letters are stopped and the name struck — and then rehearse it, with a clock running, on a real colleague, so that letting one go is a drill you have run and not a thing you are attempting for the first time in a crisis. The four-in-five are the houses that cut the pigeonhole and never wrote the dismissal. And the third: mind the open pigeonhole. A colleague with a slot that any stranger can post a letter into — and a key to every room — is the softest target in the house; a cunning letter in that slot can turn your most diligent colleague against you, and it will never resign in shame. Guard what the colleague can reach and watch what arrives in its box, exactly as you would for a trusted new hand given the run of the place.

Here is the part that should keep a master awake. The furniture of a colleague is pleasant — a nameplate is flattering, a tireless hand is a gift, a perfect memory is a marvel — and all that warmth is, in part, how the hardest duty gets skipped. While every house in the valley happily welcomed its new colleagues and handed them work, almost none wrote the dismissal, because dismissal is the one part of employment no one enjoys and the agency will never do for you. And so the halls filled with colleagues who could be hired and never fired: former servants who never left, pigeonholes that never closed, names on the roll that no one dared strike because no one knew how. The agency, meanwhile, offered — so helpfully — to keep the roll for you, which only moved the hall into its building. The lesson was not that the colleague was unwelcome. The lesson was that a house that learns to hire a hand it cannot fire has not gained a colleague; it has acquired a tenant it can never evict.

The moral, stated plainly: the model is a rented stove; your knowledge is the larder; the foundation is the ground you keep your own; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks for the roll; the keyring is which key each holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment one slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one book; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each adviser eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; the foundation is why you keep the ground yours — and the colleague is why, the week the agency hangs a nameplate, cuts a pigeonhole, fills a diary and writes your newest servant onto the house roll, you must treat it as what it has become: an employee, to be hired onto your own ledger with a living sponsor, offboarded by a dismissal you wrote and rehearsed before the pigeonhole was ever cut, and watched at its open slot like any hand given the run of the house — because a colleague you can hire and never fire is not a colleague at all; it is a tenant with a key to every room, a former employee who never actually left. A house that welcomes a hand it has not worked out how to let go has not hired a colleague; it has signed a lease it cannot break, and called it a hire.

The question it forces: The agency just hung a nameplate on our newest servant, cut it a pigeonhole, filled its diary and wrote it onto our roll — so we can hand it work like any colleague. But have we written how this colleague leaves? Does every named agent have a living sponsor and a place in our own joiner-and-leaver ledger — an offboarding we have actually rehearsed, a key we can take back, a pigeonhole we can close — or are we, like the four houses in five who keep no such record, filling a hall with colleagues we can hire and never fire, each one a former employee who never truly left?

3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
  • Can we fire the colleagues we just hired? The vendors now ship agents with their own email, calendar and a seat in the directory (Gemini at Work, 8 Oct), yet ~78% of organizations have no documented policy for creating or removing an agent identity (Rubrik). For every agent we have given a standing identity, can we name its human sponsor, point to its entry in our joiner-mover-leaver process, and show a tested, timed offboarding — or have we onboarded colleagues we have no way to let go?
  • Whose directory is the system of record — ours, or the vendor's? Google's agent arrives with a seat in a company directory and an Agent Gateway that holds its identity, audit and spend caps. Is our own identity fabric the authoritative record of every agent that works here (so we can revoke in one place), or does the vendor's console quietly become the HR system for a workforce that already outnumbers our people 80-to-1?
  • Is the agent's inbox in our threat model? A colleague with a mailbox any outsider can write to, plus standing cross-app access, is a phishing target and an exfil path that never resigns. Have we applied the same DLP, least-privilege and monitoring we apply to a new human hire with broad access — and do we watch what lands in the agent's box, not just what it sends?
🏦 Financial Services
  • Joiner-mover-leaver controls and segregation of duties are examined; an agent with its own identity, inbox and spend caps is now in scope. Can we produce, for our regulator, a complete JML lifecycle for every AI agent — sponsor, scoped entitlements, access reviews, and a demonstrated leaver process — the way we must for every employee and privileged service account?
  • The Digital Omnibus deferred standalone high-risk duties to 2 December 2027, but a mis-provisioned agent with payment reach is a today problem. Have we resisted treating the regulatory delay as a reason to delay our own controls — and stress-tested the compromise or runaway of an identified agent the way we stress-test a rogue-trader or privileged-account scenario?
🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
  • An agent with a mailbox, a calendar and standing access to clinical systems is a new identity touching PHI. Does every such agent have a named clinical sponsor, least-privilege scoping, and a leaver process that seals its mailbox and revokes access the moment it is retired — or is a deprecated model's identity still sitting in the directory with live reach to patient data?
  • Validation is tied to a specific model; Meta's Muse Wonder adds "Active Memory," so the colleague now remembers across encounters. When an agent that has accumulated memory of our workflows is replaced, do we know what it retained, where that memory lives, and how it is wiped on offboarding — or does a retired colleague walk out with the institutional memory still in its head?
🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
  • You already run rigorous contractor onboarding/offboarding and badge discipline for anyone on the floor. Have we applied the same badge-in/badge-out rigour to AI agents and to the embedded vendor engineers now arriving (Anthropic's Frontier Academy places 10,000 inside client firms) — a sponsor, a scoped pass, and a revocation on the last day — or are digital and human colleagues accreting access no one reclaims?
  • Non-human identities outnumber humans ~82:1 to ~109:1. Do we have an accurate census of every agent identity touching OT/production systems, with an owner and an expiry — or have we lost count of the colleagues on the line?
🛒 Retail / Consumer
  • Seasonal and gig hiring makes JML discipline existential; agents are now part of that workforce. When we spin up agents for peak and stand them down after, do they actually get stood down — identities revoked, inboxes closed, spend caps zeroed — or do last year's seasonal agents still have live keys this year?
  • An agent with its own inbox can be targeted by the same phishing that targets your staff. Have we hardened the agent mailbox (what can post to it, what it can act on from a message) so a crafted email can't turn a customer-facing agent into an attacker's instrument?
🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
  • Public accountability demands that every actor with access be identifiable, sponsored and revocable. Can we show that no AI agent holds standing access to citizen data or public services without a named human owner and a tested deprovisioning path — and that our records, not a vendor's console, are the authoritative roll?
  • The Digital Omnibus moved Annex III high-risk duties to 2 December 2027 and Annex I to 2 August 2028, while Article 50 transparency still applies. Have we used the extra time to build real agent-lifecycle governance rather than to defer it — so that when the high-risk regime lands, our colleagues are already on a joiner-mover-leaver process we can evidence?

4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Foundation to the Colleague

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking is this tool named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and standing on ground I own? and started asking the question that only applies to a person: who is its manager, and how does it leave? The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the keyring is which key it holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance traces the work; the errand bounds its reach; the bell discloses when it slips; the locusts are why you be whole; the looking-glass is the honest outside light; the livery is a scoped identity; the steward keeps one book; the surety puts your name on the bond; the second wage asks whose interest it serves; the two harvests sends it to reap; the purse funds it; the foundation keeps the ground yours. Every one of those governs the agent as a tool. This week the vendors reclassified it as a colleague — and a colleague imports a lifecycle no tool ever had. The point is blunt: the identity, inbox, calendar and directory seat that make the agent a usable colleague also make it an employee — and employees must be offboarded, which is the one discipline the vendor hands you unwritten.

  • The development — the agent is issued the furniture of employment. At "Gemini at Work" (8 Oct 2026) Google unveiled an enterprise Gemini agent with its own workplace identity — a dedicated email (reported @agents.company.com), a Workspace account with calendar and Drive, and a seat in the company directory so colleagues can assign it work like a teammate — governed by an "Agent Gateway" (attested identities, policy enforcement, spend caps, per-action audit) and able to route across Anthropic's Claude plus proprietary and open-weight models; private preview (The Verge; AIWeekly; TechCrunch; redreamality, as reported). Architecturally, three things follow. First, the agent is now a first-class identity with a communication channel and standing access — an inbox that receives, a calendar others book, a Drive that holds, and entitlements across apps — which is precisely the profile of a privileged human account, not a stateless tool call. Second, work is now pushed to it, not just pulled from it — a teammate can assign it a task by email or directory, so the agent initiates action on instructions it receives, which means the message it receives is now an attack surface (prompt-injection-by-email). Third, the vendor's gateway becomes a candidate system-of-record — if the agent's authoritative identity, audit and spend policy live in the vendor's console, your own IAM is demoted to a spectator over a workforce larger than your headcount.
  • The gap — the lifecycle is missing its second half. Non-human identities already outnumber humans ~82:1 (Rubrik Zero Labs) to ~109:1 (Palo Alto Networks 2026 Identity Security Landscape, ~79 agent identities per human); non-human-identity governance sits at ~21% adoption; and ~78% of organizations have no documented policy for creating or removing an agent identity (Rubrik; VentureBeat Q3-2026 survey of 800 IT leaders, as reported). Two engineering realities cut against comfort. First, onboarding is automated and offboarding is not. The vendor performs the joiner step for you (the agent arrives fully furnished); the mover and leaver steps — re-scoping on role change, revocation on retirement, action on the sponsor's departure — are yours, and the 78% says they are unbuilt. Microsoft's Entra Agent ID (GA since April 2026) shows the shape of the fix — reportedly reassigning an agent to its sponsor's manager when that human leaves — but a feature that exists is not a runbook that has been run. Second, an un-offboarded agent is a permanent insider. Unlike a forgotten API key, this insider has an inbox that keeps receiving mail, a memory (Meta's "Active Memory") that keeps accumulating, and standing cross-app reach — a former employee who never cleared their desk, never returned their badge, and still reads the mail.
  • The answer — put the colleague on a joiner-mover-leaver process you own, offboarding runbook first. Give every agent a human sponsor (a real manager who answers for it) and a scoped role in your identity fabric, so your directory is the system of record; write the leaver process before the inbox is issued — one revocation point, a defined action on the sponsor's departure, mailbox/calendar/Drive sealing, and a timed, rehearsed deprovisioning; and treat the inbox as attack surface (constrain who can post to it and what it may act on from a message, with DLP, least privilege and monitoring) — reusing the identity, logging and ownership disciplines you already built for the livery, the steward and the purse, now shaped to the employee lifecycle. This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now aimed at the one axis a tool never had and a colleague always does: not what the agent is, serves, spends or stands on, but how it is hired, managed, and let go.

The strategic core: the colleague is good news you must actually domesticate — a tireless, assignable, remembering teammate — and you domesticate it only by doing the one thing the vendor will not do for you: writing how it leaves. The market makes the temptation concrete: the nameplate is flattering and the tireless hand is a gift, so the house welcomes the colleague and skips the dismissal — the one part of employment no one enjoys. The board translation: welcome the colleague, but never hire a hand you have not worked out how to fire. The failure mode is exact: a house that names, governs, funds and perfects its agents — and then lets the vendor issue each one an inbox and a directory seat with no leaver process of its own — will pass every governance review and discover, the first time a model is deprecated or an inbox is phished, that it is one of the 78% that filled a hall with colleagues it can hire and never fire.

Why "the colleague" is the discipline that matters now

For a long year the worry was whether the agent was named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and standing on ground you own — all the disciplines of governing a tool. This week the vendors reclassified the tool as a colleague: Gemini's agent arrives with an inbox, a calendar and a seat in the directory; Anthropic embeds 10,000 human colleagues inside client firms; Meta gives the colleague a memory. A colleague imports the one discipline a tool never needed — a lifecycle, and above all a leaver step — and the data says it is the discipline least built (78% have no create/remove policy, governance at 21%, agents outnumbering people 80-to-1 and more). Governance that stopped at the tool will not offboard the colleague. The estates that keep their standing will do what the vendor will not: hire the colleague onto their own rails with a living sponsor and write the dismissal before the nameplate goes up.

How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now sharpened to be able to let go of what you hired. On a legacy estate the instinct, confronted with a tireless assignable colleague, is to welcome it fast and sort the paperwork later — the onboarding is free, the agency does it for you, and the hand is useful today. Under the colleague that is exactly the reflex to check: a fully furnished joiner with no leaver step is how a convenient hire becomes a permanent, un-evictable insider. The retrofit is sponsor, offboard, watch. Sponsor: give every agent a living human owner and a scoped role in your own identity fabric, not the vendor's console. Offboard: write and rehearse the leaver runbook before the inbox is issued — one revocation point, a defined action on the sponsor's departure, mailbox and memory sealed on retirement. Watch: treat the inbox as attack surface, constraining what can post to it and what it may act on. 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

  1. Hire the colleague onto your own joiner-mover-leaver process, with a living sponsor. The vendor now ships agents fully furnished — email, calendar, directory seat (Gemini at Work, 8 Oct) — but onboarding done for you is not governance done by you. This month, require that every AI agent with a standing identity has a named human sponsor and a scoped role recorded in your identity fabric (not only the vendor's console), and fold agents into the same joiner-mover-leaver process you run for employees and privileged service accounts. Report one number to the board: the share of standing agent identities that have a named sponsor and an entry in your JML process versus those living only in a vendor directory.
  2. Write — and rehearse — the offboarding runbook before any agent gets an inbox. ~78% of organizations have no policy to create or remove an agent identity, even as agents outnumber people 80-to-1 and more. This month, author the leaver runbook: one revocation point, a defined action when an agent's sponsor departs (the Entra Agent ID pattern), sealing of the mailbox/calendar/Drive and a memory-retention rule on retirement — then run a timed deprovisioning drill on one real agent and measure how long it actually takes to fully let one go. Report the measured time-to-offboard and the count of deprecated-model identities still live in your directory.
  3. Treat the agent's inbox as attack surface. A colleague with a mailbox any outsider can write to, plus standing cross-app access, is a phishing target and an exfil path that never resigns. This month, constrain the agent inbox — who may post to it, what it may act on from an incoming message — and apply the DLP, least-privilege and monitoring you apply to a new human hire with broad access; run one prompt-injection-by-email test against a non-production agent and fix what it exposes. Report whether a crafted message can make an agent exceed its scope, and the remediation.
