The Colleague

Wednesday, 14 October 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

This week the vendors reclassified the agent from a tool you govern into a colleague you employ — Google's enterprise Gemini agent arrives with its own email, calendar, Drive and a seat in your company directory, assignable like a teammate. You can hire the colleague. Can you fire it? Hire it onto your own joiner-mover-leaver process, write the offboarding runbook first, treat the inbox as attack surface.

1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from governing the agent as a tool to employing it as a colleague — because the vendors just shipped agents pre-fitted with the furniture of employment, and a colleague imports a lifecycle, above all the leaver step the vendor hands you unwritten.

1. The development — the agent gets the furniture of employment. On 8 October 2026, at "Gemini at Work," Google unveiled an enterprise Gemini agent with its own workplace identity — a dedicated email (reported @agents.company.com), a Workspace account with calendar and Drive, and a seat in the company directory, assignable like a teammate, governed by an "Agent Gateway" (The Verge; AIWeekly; TechCrunch, as reported; private preview). Anthropic's Claude Frontier Academy (2 Oct) embeds 10,000 engineers inside clients; Meta's Muse Wonder (12 Oct) gives the colleague "Active Memory." The tool became a colleague.

2. The gap — you can hire it but not fire it. AI-agent and other non-human identities already outnumber humans ~82:1 (Rubrik Zero Labs) to ~109:1 (Palo Alto Networks 2026, ~79 agent identities per human); non-human-identity governance sits at ~21% adoption; and ~78% of organizations have no documented policy for creating or removing an agent identity (Rubrik; VentureBeat Q3-2026, 800 IT leaders, as reported). The onboarding is done for you; the offboarding is left to you.

3. The answer — put it on your JML process, offboarding first. Give every agent a human sponsor and a scoped role on identity rails you own; write and test the deprovisioning runbook before the inbox is issued; and treat the mailbox as attack surface (DLP, least privilege, monitoring).

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each governed the agent as a tool — this week the vendors handed it the furniture of a colleague: an email, a calendar, a Drive, a seat in your directory, a memory. The furniture of employment is seductive and quietly imports employment's hardest duty, offboarding — which four firms in five have never written. Welcome the colleague, but never hire a hand you have not first worked out how to let go.

2 · Allegory — The Colleague

A great house had spent a careful year making its servants excellent — named, booked, bonded, wage-checked, sent to reap, funded, and stood on ground it owned — but it had thought of them all as tools it governed. Then the agency that supplied them changed what it delivered: a clerk hung a nameplate on a door, cut the newest servant a pigeonhole for letters, gave it a diary, and wrote its name into the house roll between two human hands — "a member of the household; assign it work as you would any other" — and left before anyone asked the question that matters about a member of a household.

The master was delighted: a tireless colleague that never forgot and sat ready for any errand. But weeks later, lying awake, he thought of the one thing the clerk had not: a colleague, one day, leaves — reassigned, replaced, caught in a mistake, or compromised by a letter in that open pigeonhole — and he had no idea who would take back its key, empty its pigeonhole, or strike its name from the roll. He had an elaborate ceremony for hiring; he had nothing for letting go. And when the careful houses counted, the arithmetic frightened them: for every living hand there were eighty or a hundred named colleagues, and nearly four houses in five had never written how a single one is hired or fired.

So the wise houses learned three things: hire the colleague onto your own roll, with a living sponsor (keep your own book, not the agency's); write how it leaves before you write how it arrives (who takes the key, what happens when its sponsor departs — and rehearse it with a clock running); and mind the open pigeonhole (a colleague with a slot any stranger can post to, and a key to every room, is the softest target in the house). For the agency onboards your colleague — the dismissal it never writes.

The question it forces: The agency just hung a nameplate on our newest servant, cut it a pigeonhole and wrote it onto our roll. But have we written how this colleague leaves? Does every named agent have a living sponsor and a place in our own joiner-and-leaver ledger — or are we, like the four houses in five, filling a hall with colleagues we can hire and never fire?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Colleague

Every prior control governs the agent as a tool; the colleague imports a lifecycle no tool ever had. The identity, inbox, calendar and directory seat that make the agent a usable colleague also make it an employee — and employees must be offboarded, the one discipline the vendor hands you unwritten. The development: the agent is now a first-class identity with a communication channel and standing access (an inbox that receives, a calendar others book, cross-app entitlements) — a privileged human account, not a stateless tool call; work is pushed to it by message, so the message is now an attack surface; and the vendor's gateway is a candidate system-of-record. The gap: onboarding is automated, offboarding is not — the mover and leaver steps are yours, and 78% have no create/remove policy; an un-offboarded agent is a permanent insider with an inbox that keeps receiving and a memory that keeps accumulating. The answer: give every agent a human sponsor and a scoped role in your own identity fabric; write and rehearse the leaver runbook before the inbox is issued; treat the inbox as attack surface.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Hire the colleague onto your own joiner-mover-leaver process, with a living sponsor. Onboarding done for you (email, calendar, directory seat) is not governance done by you. Require that every agent with a standing identity has a named human sponsor and a scoped role in your identity fabric (not only the vendor's console), folded into the same JML process as employees and privileged service accounts. Report the share of agent identities with a sponsor and a JML entry.

2. Write — and rehearse — the offboarding runbook before any agent gets an inbox. ~78% have no policy to create or remove an agent identity. Author the leaver runbook: one revocation point, a defined action when a sponsor departs (the Entra Agent ID pattern), sealing of the mailbox/calendar/Drive and a memory-retention rule — then run a timed deprovisioning drill on one real agent. Report the measured time-to-offboard and the count of deprecated-model identities still live.

3. Treat the agent's inbox as attack surface. A colleague with a mailbox any outsider can write to, plus standing cross-app access, is a phishing target and an exfil path that never resigns. Constrain the inbox (who may post, what it may act on from a message) and apply the DLP, least-privilege and monitoring you apply to a new human hire; run one prompt-injection-by-email test against a non-production agent. Report whether a crafted message can make an agent exceed its scope, and the remediation.
