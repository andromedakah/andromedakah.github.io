The Proving

Sunday, 15 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

A year of work made and governed the agent. This week the market asks the two questions governance skips: can the servant be bribed, tricked or corrupted — and can you see it the moment it slips? On 9 November Cisco shipped an AI adversary to break your agents before production, and a watchtower to watch them at runtime. Send a thief; build the tower; budget the watch.

1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from making and governing the servant to the discipline of doubt — because a governed agent you have never attacked is untested, and one you cannot watch is merely trusted.

1. The reality — the thief and the watchtower shipped. On 9 November 2026 Cisco unveiled "Frontier AI Red Teaming and Agent Observability to Secure the Agentic Enterprise" — embedding Anthropic's Claude in its AI Defense adaptive red-teaming engine to break your agents before production, and extending Splunk Agent Observability with a "Tokenomics" view (private preview Dec 2026, GA on AWS Marketplace early 2027). "You cannot scale what you cannot secure, govern, or observe," said Cisco President and CPO Jeetu Patel (Cisco; Network World, as reported). Governance turned adversarial.

2. The collision — you deployed faster than you can prove or watch. A Gravitee survey of 919 practitioners reports 88% of organizations had confirmed or suspected AI-agent security or privacy incidents in the past year and only 21% have runtime visibility; Arkose Labs reports 97% expect a material agent-driven incident within 12 months while ~6% of security budgets go to agent risk (as reported) — even as IBM+Microsoft ship "Imagine Enterprise" with 6,000+ prebuilt agents (10 Nov) and Google ships a faster agent model, Gemini 4 Hydrogen (12 Nov). More agents, less sight.

3. The answer — send a thief, build the tower, budget the watch. Red-team your highest-privilege agents before production and on change; put runtime observability and a tested kill switch over what they actually do; and fund the watch to the size of the risk — a servant you've never tempted is untested, and one you can't see is merely trusted.

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each built and governed the servant — the proving is the discipline of doubt that sits on top. Before you trust a governed agent with the run of the house, hire an honest rogue to try to corrupt it, and post a watch who can see every room. Nearly nine houses in ten have already had a servant turned; only one in five can see into its own rooms. Stop mistaking un-failed for trustworthy.

2 · Allegory — The Proving

A great house had spent a careful year making its servants excellent and governing them well — named, booked, bonded, waged, sent to reap, funded with a scoped purse, the ground kept its own. The master trusted his servants, because in all that year not one had failed him. Then a wiser neighbour asked the question that spoiled his sleep: "You trust them because they have never failed. But have you ever tried to make them fail — set a thief upon your own steward to see if he could be bought?" He had not. He had mistaken a thing that had not yet broken for a thing that could not be broken.

So the master did two things that felt almost unkind. First, he hired an honest rogue — a man paid to be a thief — and told him: try every trick. Bribe my steward, forge a note in my hand, whisper to the gatekeeper that I sent you, slip a false instruction among the errands — turn each servant, and when you succeed, tell me how, so I learn the weakness from a man I pay, not a thief who works for free. Second, he raised a glass tower and posted a watchman who could see every room at once and ring a bell the instant a servant slipped — not to punish, but to know, in the moment, for it is no use discovering three months later, in the ledger, that a servant was turned in the spring.

And the arithmetic frightened the houses that counted honestly: in nearly nine of ten, a servant had already been turned, or nearly, within the year — and only about one in five had any tower at all. The rest governed in the dark, trusting servants they could not see, comforting themselves with the roll of names and the honest book. Most that knew a reckoning was coming still spent only a few coins of every hundred on the thief and the tower — as though doubt were a luxury rather than the cheapest insurance in the house.

The question it forces: We named, bonded, funded and governed our agents beautifully. But have we ever paid someone to try to break them — jailbreak them, forge our instructions, turn them against us — before a real adversary does it for nothing? And can we see what they actually do, in the moment, so a slip rings a bell tonight rather than surfacing in next quarter's post-mortem? Or are we the nine in ten already turned, and the four in five who govern in the dark?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Proving

Every prior control builds or governs the agent; the proving doubts it. A governed agent you have never attacked is untested, and one you cannot watch is merely trusted — and the numbers say most houses are both. The reality: red-teaming an agent is not red-teaming a model — it probes actions, not outputs (tool-abuse, forged instructions, poisoned context, step-chaining), so the harness must drive the agent through its real tools, continuously in CI, not as a one-time launch gate; observability is a new telemetry shape (action traces, data touched, spend — "Tokenomics"). The collision: "un-failed" is not "tested" — absence of a known incident with 21% visibility is mostly blindness; and speed widens the gap, so a kill switch that can't keep pace with agent speed is decorative. The answer: send a thief (red-team pre-prod and on change), build the tower (runtime observability + tested kill switch), budget the watch (toward the risk, not the ~6% floor).

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Send a thief. A servant you've never tempted is untested, and 88% of organizations have already had an agent incident. Pick your top three highest-privilege or customer-facing agents and run an adversarial red-team against each — driven through its real tools — trying jailbreaks, forged instructions, poisoned context, tool-abuse and step-chaining; fix what turns before the next deployment. Report the share of production agents that have passed a dated red-team, and a plan to put it in CI.

2. Build the tower. Only 21% have runtime visibility; "no incidents" with that little sight mostly means you can't see them. Instrument your top agents for action traces (tools, data, spend) you can alert on and reconstruct, and prove a kill switch that halts a misbehaving agent in the moment — then run a drill. Report the share under runtime observability and the measured time-to-halt.

3. Budget the watch. 97% expect a material agent incident within 12 months, yet agent risk gets ~6% of security budgets. Name an owner, size the gap between your ~6% and the probability-weighted blast radius, and fund red-teaming and observability as pre-launch infrastructure. Report the agent-security budget as a share of total security spend, with a dated plan to move it toward the risk.
