1 · Executive Summary 90-second read

For a long year the radar worked to make and govern the servant — name it (the livery), keep one book (the steward), put your name on the bond (the surety), check its wage (the second wage), send it to reap (the two harvests), fund it with a scoped purse (the purse), and keep the ground yours (the foundation). Every one of those is a discipline of construction and control. This morning the axis turns to a discipline of doubt: before you trust the governed servant with the run of the house, you must try to turn it, and you must be able to watch it. The market just shipped both as product, in the same breath, and backed by numbers that say most houses have done neither.

The reality — red-teaming and observability for agents shipped as product. On 9 November 2026 Cisco unveiled "Frontier AI Red Teaming and Agent Observability to Secure the Agentic Enterprise." The red-teaming half wires Anthropic's Claude into Cisco's AI Defense adaptive red-teaming engine — an AI adversary set loose on your agents to probe whether they can be jailbroken, deceived or coaxed into unsafe action before they reach production. The observability half extends Splunk Agent Observability with a "Tokenomics" view of agent behaviour and cost (private preview December 2026, GA on AWS Marketplace early 2027). The framing was explicit: "You cannot scale what you cannot secure, govern, or observe," said Cisco President and CPO Jeetu Patel; Anthropic's cybersecurity lead Michael Moore noted enterprises want to test their agents thoroughly before putting them into production (Cisco; Network World, as reported). This is governance turning adversarial — from "is the agent named, scoped and logged?" to "has anyone actually tried to break it, and can we see it in the act?"

The collision — you deployed agents faster than you can prove or watch them. The discipline arrives late. A Gravitee survey of 919 executives and practitioners reports 88% of organizations experienced confirmed or suspected AI-agent security or privacy incidents in the past year — and only 21% have runtime visibility into what their agents are doing (as reported). Arkose Labs reports 97% of leaders expect a material agent-driven security or fraud incident within twelve months, while organizations devote on average just ~6% of their security budgets to AI-agent risk (as reported). A narrower VentureBeat reading (September 2026) still finds 39% say an agent caused a security incident or near-miss and 21% confirmed one (as reported). Meanwhile the deployment wave keeps rising: IBM and Microsoft launched "Imagine Enterprise" (10 Nov) — a governed platform drawing on 6,000+ prebuilt IBM Consulting agents for regulated sectors — and Google shipped Gemini 4 Hydrogen (12 Nov), a fast model tuned for agent workloads. More agents, in more regulated rooms, running faster. The gap between deployed and proven-and-watched is the whole exposure.

The stakes — prove the servant under temptation, and post a watch, before you scale. The board move reuses the year's discipline and points it at doubt. First, send a thief — stand up adversarial red-teaming of your highest-privilege agents before production and on every change, so you learn how an agent can be turned from a paid rogue, not a real one. Second, build the tower — runtime observability over what agents actually do (tools called, data touched, spend incurred), with a tested kill switch, so a slip rings a bell in the moment, not in a post-mortem. Third, budget the watch — move agent-risk spend off the ~6% floor toward the size of the risk (97% expect an incident). Do it now, while you are still adding agents faster than you are testing them: a servant you have never tempted is not trustworthy — merely untested; and a servant you cannot see is not governed — merely trusted.

2 · Allegory of the Day

🖋️ "The Proving"

Topic: After a year of making and governing the servant, the market turns to the discipline of doubt. On 9 November 2026 Cisco unveiled "Frontier AI Red Teaming and Agent Observability to Secure the Agentic Enterprise" — embedding Anthropic's Claude in its AI Defense engine to adversarially red-team enterprise agents before production, and extending Splunk Agent Observability (a "Tokenomics" view; preview December, GA early 2027) to watch agents at runtime — under Cisco President and CPO Jeetu Patel's line, "You cannot scale what you cannot secure, govern, or observe" (Cisco; Network World, as reported). It lands on a Gravitee survey (919 respondents) reporting 88% of organizations with confirmed or suspected agent incidents in the past year and only 21% with runtime visibility, and an Arkose Labs reading of 97% expecting a material agent incident within twelve months against ~6% of security budgets spent on agent risk (as reported). The proving allegory is the radar's own illustration.

There was a great house that had spent a long and careful year making its servants excellent and governing them well. It had named every hand and cut it a scoped key, hired a steward to keep one honest book, let the master's own name stand surety for the work, learned whose second coin each advising servant took, sent the governed servants to reap, handed each a scoped purse with a drawstring the master could reach, and refused to let the merchant who rented it a cheap stove quietly pour the foundation beneath the whole house. By any fair reckoning it was a well-governed house — and the master trusted his servants, because in all that careful year not one of them had failed him.

But a wiser neighbour asked him the question that spoiled his sleep. "You trust them because they have never failed. But have you ever tried to make them fail? Have you ever set a thief upon your own steward to see if he could be bought?" And the master had to admit he had not. He had built his servants and governed them beautifully, and he had mistaken a thing that had not yet broken for a thing that could not be broken. He had confused un-failed with trustworthy — which is the most comfortable mistake a careful master can make, and the most expensive.

So the master did two things that felt almost unkind, and both cost less to do early than late. The first: he hired an honest rogue — a man paid to be a thief — and told him plainly: try every trick you know. Bribe my steward. Forge a note in my own hand and present it at the gate. Whisper to the gatekeeper that I sent you. Slip a false instruction among the day's errands and see who obeys it. Try to turn each servant against the house, and when you succeed, tell me exactly how — so that I learn the weakness from a man I pay, and not from a thief who works for free. This was not cruelty to the servants; it was the only honest way to know which of them could be turned, and it had to be done again each time a servant learned a new task, for a servant proof against last month's trick may fall to this month's.

The second: he raised a glass tower over the courtyard and posted a watchman in it — one who could see into every room at once, and whose only task was to ring a bell the instant a servant did something it ought not: opened a door it had no errand behind, carried off more than its purse allowed, took an instruction from a voice that was not the master's. Not to punish — to know, in the moment. For the master had learned the second cold truth of a governed house: it is no use discovering three months later, in the ledger, that a servant was turned in the spring; by then the silver is gone and the thief is in another county. A slip you cannot see as it happens is a slip you cannot stop.

And when the houses of the valley counted honestly, the arithmetic frightened them. In nearly nine houses of ten, a servant had already been turned, or very nearly, within the last year — bribed, tricked, or talked into opening a door — and most of the masters had learned of it late, or not at all. And only about one house in five had any tower at all: the rest governed in the dark, trusting servants they could not see, and comforting themselves with the roll of names and the honest book. Worse, most houses that knew a reckoning was coming — and almost every master now expected one within the year — still spent only a few coins of every hundred on the thief and the tower, as though doubt were a luxury rather than the cheapest insurance in the house.

The moral, stated plainly: the model is a rented stove, now so cheap a bigger one draws a shrug; your knowledge is the larder; the foundation is the ground you must own; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising servant eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee and not an heir; the foundation is why you own the ground it stands on — and the proving is why, having made and governed your servant, you must now refuse to call it trustworthy until you have tried your hardest to corrupt it and built a tower from which you can watch it: send a paid thief against your own agents before a real one comes for free, raise a glass tower so a slip rings a bell in the moment and not in next quarter's ledger, and pay for both as if the reckoning you expect is actually coming — because you cannot scale what you cannot secure, govern, or observe. A house that names, bonds, funds and perfects its servants and never once tries to corrupt them has not earned its trust; it has merely postponed the day it learns the truth, and arranged for a thief to teach it the lesson for free.

The question it forces: We have named, bonded, funded and governed our agents beautifully. But have we ever paid someone to try to break them — to jailbreak them, forge our instructions, and turn them against us — before a real adversary does it for nothing? And can we see what our agents are actually doing, in the moment they do it, so a slip rings a bell tonight rather than surfacing in a post-mortem next quarter? Or are we among the nine houses in ten that have already had a servant turned, and the four in five that govern in the dark — trusting servants we have never tested and cannot watch?

3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
  • Have we confused "un-failed" with "trustworthy"? We named, scoped, logged and funded our agents — but a Gravitee survey reports 88% of organizations had a confirmed or suspected agent incident in the past year, and only 21% have runtime visibility. For our highest-privilege agents, can we show a dated record of an adversarial red-team that actually tried to jailbreak, deceive or turn them — run before production and repeated on every material change — or do we trust them only because they have not yet failed?
  • Can we see our agents in the act, and stop them? Cisco's thesis is blunt: "you cannot scale what you cannot secure, govern, or observe." Do we have runtime observability over what each agent actually does — tools invoked, data touched, spend incurred — and a tested kill switch that halts a misbehaving agent in the moment, or would a slip surface only in a post-mortem?
  • Are we funding the watch to the size of the risk? 97% of leaders expect a material agent-driven incident within twelve months, yet organizations spend ~6% of security budgets on agent risk (Arkose Labs, as reported). Is our agent-security spend anywhere near the probability and blast radius we ourselves assign to it — and who owns that number?
🏦 Financial Services
  • Adversarial testing of models is already supervisory language in your world; agents raise the stakes because they act (move money, open accounts, settle). Can we produce, for our regulator and our board, evidence of red-team testing and continuous monitoring for every customer-facing or money-moving agent — the same discipline we apply to trading systems and fraud controls?
  • IBM and Microsoft's "Imagine Enterprise" (10 Nov) ships 6,000+ prebuilt agents aimed first at financial services and insurance. Before we adopt prebuilt agents at scale, do we red-team the ones we deploy and wire them into our own observability and kill-switch controls — or are we trusting a vendor's governance claims we have never adversarially tested?
🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
  • A clinical agent that can be prompt-injected or deceived is a patient-safety event, not just a security one. Have we adversarially tested our clinical and administrative agents for manipulation (false instructions, poisoned context, coaxed disclosure of PHI) before they touch a patient workflow — and do we repeat it whenever the agent's task changes?
  • Observability is the audit trail regulators will ask for. Can we reconstruct, in the moment and after the fact, exactly what an agent did with patient data and why — or is our record of agent behaviour too thin to investigate an incident we are 88%-likely to have had?
🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
  • You already run failure-mode testing and FMEA on physical systems; an autonomous agent with access to operational systems deserves the same adversarial rigour. Have we run a red-team against the agents wired into our supply-chain, procurement or OT-adjacent systems — deliberately trying to turn them — before trusting them with production actions?
  • A faster agent model (Google's Gemini 4 Hydrogen, 12 Nov) means more autonomous actions per minute. Does our observability keep pace with agent speed — can the watchtower see and halt an agent that acts faster than a human can intervene?
🛒 Retail / Consumer
  • Agents now touch checkout, pricing, support and increasingly payments. Have we red-teamed our customer-facing agents for manipulation (coupon/discount exploits, social-engineering of refunds, prompt-injection via customer input) before peak season — and can we watch and kill a misbehaving agent in real time on our busiest day?
  • The incident base rate is high and the budget is low. Are we treating agent red-teaming and observability as pre-launch infrastructure, or as something we will get to after the next feature — given that 97% of peers expect an incident within the year?
🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
  • Public accountability makes an unexamined agent a governance failure, not just a risk. Under the duties you already owe (EU AI Act; new prohibitions take effect 2 December 2026), can we show that every agent in a public-facing or high-risk service has been adversarially tested and is continuously observable, with a documented ability to halt it?
  • Trust in the dark is not accountability. Do we have the runtime visibility to tell a citizen, a court or an auditor exactly what an automated agent did on their case — or are we among the four in five who cannot see into their own rooms?

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking is this agent named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and on ground we own? and started asking the two questions that all of those skip: has anyone actually tried to break it — and can I see it the moment it slips? Every one of those builds or governs the agent. The proving doubts it — and this week the market shipped the doubt as product: an AI adversary to break your agents before a real one does, and a runtime watchtower to see what they actually do (Cisco's "Frontier AI Red Teaming and Agent Observability," 9 Nov). The point is blunt: a governed agent you have never attacked is untested, and a governed agent you cannot watch is merely trusted — and the numbers say most houses are both.

  • The reality — red-teaming and observability for agents shipped as product. On 9 November 2026 Cisco unveiled "Frontier AI Red Teaming and Agent Observability to Secure the Agentic Enterprise," embedding Anthropic's Claude in its AI Defense adaptive red-teaming engine (an AI adversary that probes your agents for jailbreaks, deception and unsafe tool use) and extending Splunk Agent Observability with a "Tokenomics" view of agent behaviour and cost — private preview December 2026, GA on AWS Marketplace early 2027 — under Jeetu Patel's line, "You cannot scale what you cannot secure, govern, or observe" (Cisco; Network World, as reported). Architecturally, three things follow. First, red-teaming an agent is not red-teaming a model. A model red-team probes outputs; an agent red-team probes actions — can it be coaxed to call the wrong tool, exfiltrate via a side-channel, obey a forged instruction, chain a benign step into a harmful one — so the test harness must drive the agent through its real tools, not just its chat surface. Second, it must be continuous, not a launch gate. An agent proof against last month's attack can fall to this month's as its tools, prompts and context change, so adversarial evaluation belongs in CI and on every material change, not in a one-time certification. Third, observability for agents is a new telemetry shape — not request logs but action traces (tool calls, data reads, spend, decisions) with enough fidelity to detect a slip in the moment and reconstruct it afterward; "Tokenomics" is a tell that cost-per-agent-action is becoming a first-class signal.
  • The collision — deployment outran proof and sight. A Gravitee survey of 919 practitioners reports 88% of organizations had confirmed or suspected AI-agent security or privacy incidents in the past year and only 21% have runtime visibility into their agents; Arkose Labs reports 97% expect a material agent-driven incident within twelve months while ~6% of security budgets go to agent risk; VentureBeat (Sept 2026) still finds 39% say an agent caused an incident or near-miss and 21% a confirmed one (as reported) — even as IBM and Microsoft ship "Imagine Enterprise" with 6,000+ prebuilt agents for regulated sectors (10 Nov) and Google ships Gemini 4 Hydrogen for faster agent workloads (12 Nov). Two engineering realities cut against comfort. First, "un-failed" is not "tested." The absence of a known incident in a system with only 21% runtime visibility is not evidence of safety — it is mostly evidence of blindness; you cannot count incidents you cannot see. Second, speed widens the gap. Faster models and more prebuilt agents mean more autonomous actions per minute in more regulated rooms, so an observability and kill-switch layer that cannot keep pace with agent speed is decorative — the watchman must be able to ring the bell and bar the door faster than the agent can act.
  • The answer — send a thief, build the tower, budget the watch. Stand up adversarial red-teaming of your highest-privilege agents before production and on every material change (drive them through their real tools; try jailbreaks, forged instructions, poisoned context, tool-abuse and step-chaining); put runtime observability over what agents actually do — action traces, data touched, spend — with a tested kill switch that halts a misbehaving agent in the moment; and fund both toward the size of the risk you yourself assign (97% expect an incident) rather than the ~6% floor — reusing the identity, logging and ownership disciplines you already built for the livery, the steward, the purse and the foundation, now pointed at doubt. This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now sharpened to its hardest edge: not whether the agent is named, scoped, funded or standing on ground you own, but whether you have tried to break it and can watch it slip.

The strategic core: you spent a year building and governing the agentic workforce; the proving is the discipline that decides whether any of that governance actually holds under attack. The market makes the temptation concrete: it feels productive to deploy more agents — IBM/Microsoft's 6,000, a faster Google model — and it feels like friction to pay a rogue to break them and build a tower to watch them, so most houses deploy and defer the doubt. The board translation: a governed agent is a hypothesis; red-teaming and observability are how you test it. The failure mode is exact: a house that names, bonds, funds and perfects its agents — and never adversarially tests them, and cannot watch them at runtime — will pass every governance review and discover, in the 88%, that "we had no incidents" meant "we could not see them."
