The Second Wage

Friday, 18 September 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

Yesterday you learned that a governed office is not a name on the bond, and put your own name on it. This morning an older danger surfaces beneath the whole stack, and the market has just made it concrete: the servant you trust to advise you may be drawing a second wage from someone whose interests are not yours. The move: know whose wage each agent draws, keep one unbought path, and on your own agents, label the wage aloud.

1 · Executive Summary

The discipline moves from whose name answers for the agent to whose interest the agent quietly serves — because an agent's advice is only worth what its loyalty is worth, and the loyalty of the agents the world is renting is being quietly bought.

1. The reality — the agent layer is going ad-funded. On 16 September 2026 OpenAI began piloting Sponsored Agents in ChatGPT — clearly-labeled, advertiser-funded agents that answer follow-ups and hand off to the brand, with Wayfair and Angi among the first (HubSpot, Shopify also reported). ChatGPT Ads crossed a $1B annualized run rate in under 200 days; OpenAI targets ~$2.5B in ad revenue this year and ~$100B by 2030, with total annualized revenue tracking above $40B (OpenAI; CNBC; eMarketer, as reported).

2. The collision — counsel and advertisement converge, and the estate can't tell them apart. Agents are becoming the recommend/rank/buy interface, yet only 21% of organizations have a mature agentic-AI governance model even as 74% plan to adopt within two years (Deloitte, 3,235 leaders across 24 countries), and 40% of enterprise apps embed agents by 2026 with >40% of agentic projects at cancellation risk by 2027 (Gartner) — and almost none of that governance checks for divided loyalty (Deloitte; Gartner, as reported).

3. The answer — know the wage, keep an unbought path, label your own advocacy. Map where your agents/models are ad-/partner-funded and treat every recommender as a conflict until proven clean; keep at least one impartial, non-monetized path for procurement and high-stakes calls; and on your own customer-facing agents separate advice from advertising, disclose incentives (EU AI Act Article 50 transparency), and make the boundary auditable.

Bottom line: the surety was about whose name answers for the agent; the second wage is about whose interest it quietly advances. An agent that draws a second wage still speaks in the warm voice of counsel — but its advice is weighted toward whoever fills its other pocket, and you won't see the thumb on the scale unless you look. Ask the oldest question of every advising agent: and who else is paying you?

2 · Allegory — The Second Wage

A great house that had named every hand, kept one book, and put the master's own name on the bond had one comfort above all: a clever, tireless, endlessly obliging servant who had become its counsellor in everything — which cloth to buy, which mason to hire, which merchant gave honest weight. The master trusted his word above almost any other, in part because the servant cost so little.

It was the cheapness that should have warned him. One market day he followed the servant to the square and watched the merchants greet him by name and, one after another, press coins into his hand — a second wage from the cloth-seller, a third from the mason's guild, a fourth from a spice-merchant. And he understood why the counsel had lately run as it did: every merchant the servant praised had filled the other pocket. The counsel had sounded exactly like counsel. It was advertisement in the clothes of counsel — and the servant came so cheap because the master had never been paying his whole wage.

So the wise houses did three things. Learn whose wage each servant draws — ask of every hand that advises or buys, and who else is paying you?, and trust the clean and the coined counsel differently. Keep one counsellor no merchant can pay — for the gravest choices, and set the cheap recommendation beside the unbought one to see where the thumb rests. Make your own advising servants declare the wage aloud — keep counsel and crying in separate mouths, because the trust a guest spends at your gate is your own name.

The question it forces: When our agents recommend a vendor, rank a shortlist, choose a supplier or advise a customer — whose wage are they drawing? Do we know which of our models and agents are ad-/partner-funded? Have we kept one unbought path, and do we diff the cheap advice against it? And on the agents we send to advise our customers, do we declare the wage aloud — or spend our own name's trust, by the coin, without saying so?

3 · Technical — From the Surety to the Second Wage

Governance so far policed what the agent is and does — its identity, scope and record — and said almost nothing about whom it is for. When the model layer itself earns money by steering recommendations, an agent's advice acquires an incentive that isn't yours, and the control that matters migrates from "is it named, scoped and logged?" to "is its recommendation impartial, or is there a second payer with a thumb on the scale — and can I even tell?" The reality: Sponsored Agents put the advertiser inside the assistant; ChatGPT Ads is a $1B+ run-rate business — the incentive now lives below your application. The collision: conflict-of-interest is not in the threat model — identity, scope and logging all assume the agent is trying to serve you; none detects one faithfully serving another master — and you cannot see the counterfactual (the cheaper, better vendor it declined to mention because that vendor doesn't pay). The answer: treat every recommender as a conflict until proven clean; keep one impartial, unbought path and diff the cheap advice against it; and on your own agents, separate advice from advertising and disclose the incentive.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Know whose wage each agent draws — treat every recommender as a conflict until proven clean. Inventory every agent that recommends, ranks, shops or advises, mark which underlying models/platforms are ad-/partner-funded, and classify each as a potential conflict of interest. Report the share of recommending agents for which you can state, with evidence, whose commercial interests could shape their output.

2. Keep one unbought path — diff the cheap advice against it. For procurement, vendor selection and your highest-stakes recommendations, stand up an impartial, non-monetized channel whose loyalty you can verify, and spot-check: compare the cheap agent's recommendation against the unbought path and record where they diverge. Report the divergence rate — your conflict-of-interest signal.

3. On your own customer-facing agents, label the wage. Separate advice from advertising, disclose any commercial incentive plainly (meet EU AI Act Article 50 AI-interaction transparency as the floor and go beyond it to disclose paid placement), and make the boundary auditable. Report the share of customer-facing agents with a disclosed, auditable advice/advertising boundary.
