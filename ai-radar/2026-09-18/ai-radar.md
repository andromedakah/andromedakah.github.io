## 1 · Executive Summary (90-second read)

Yesterday the radar told you that keeping a governed office over your agents is necessary but not sufficient — that the law now writes the deployer's own name on the bond, so you must name a surety of your own, hold your own producible record, and never mistake a supplier's certificate for your seal. This morning a subtler question comes up from beneath the whole stack, and a product launch this week has made it unavoidable: **an agent's advice is only worth what its loyalty is worth — and the loyalty of the agents the world is renting is quietly being bought.**

**The reality — the agent layer is going ad-funded, in the open.** On **16 September 2026** OpenAI began piloting **Sponsored Agents** in ChatGPT: after a relevant ad, a user can choose to enter a **clearly labeled conversation with a business-sponsored agent**, kept separate from ChatGPT's own answer, which fields follow-up questions and then hands off to the advertiser's site or next step; **Wayfair and Angi** are among the first participants (HubSpot and Shopify also reported) (OpenAI, as reported). This is not a side experiment. OpenAI's ad business **crossed a $1 billion annualized revenue run rate in under 200 days**, the company is **targeting roughly $2.5 billion in ad revenue this year** and **~$100 billion by 2030** (with total annualized revenue tracking above $40 billion), and its applications chief Fidji Simo is driving the expansion across 40-plus countries (OpenAI; CNBC; eMarketer, as reported). Read plainly: the "rented stove" this radar has tracked since day one is getting cheaper precisely because a second payer — the advertiser — is beginning to fund it, and that payer's interests now ride inside the agent.

**The collision — counsel and advertisement are converging inside the same helpful voice.** The reason this matters to a boardroom is not that ChatGPT shows ads; it is that **the agent is becoming the interface through which recommendations, rankings, purchases and vendor choices flow** — in consumer apps and in the copilots and procurement bots enterprises are deploying. When the same foundation-model layer you build on also monetizes attention by steering users toward whoever pays, the impartiality of an agent's "recommendation" stops being a given and becomes something you must verify. And the estate is not ready to verify it: **Deloitte's 2026 State of AI in the Enterprise (3,235 leaders across 24 countries) found only 21% of organizations have a mature governance model for agentic AI** — boundaries, real-time monitoring, audit trails — even as **74% plan to adopt agentic AI within two years** (Deloitte, as reported), and almost none of that thin governance yet tests an agent's recommendations for conflict of interest. Meanwhile **Gartner still projects 40% of enterprise applications will embed task-specific agents by year-end 2026** (up from under 5% in 2025), with **over 40% of agentic-AI projects at risk of cancellation by 2027** (Gartner, as reported). More agents advising, buying and steering; almost no way to tell whose wage they draw.

**The stakes — know whose coin your agent serves, keep one unbought path, and label your own advocacy.** For a board the move is exact. **A recommendation from an agent on someone else's payroll is an advertisement wearing the clothes of counsel — and the trust it spends is yours.** Three things follow. First, **know whose wage your agents draw** — map where the models and agents you rely on are ad- or partner-funded, and treat any agent that recommends, ranks or purchases as a potential conflict of interest until proven otherwise. Second, **keep at least one unbought path** — for procurement, vendor selection and high-stakes recommendations, maintain an impartial, non-monetized channel whose loyalty you can verify, and audit your agents' recommendations against it for steering. Third, **on your own customer-facing agents, label the wage** — separate advice from advertising, disclose any commercial incentive plainly (the EU AI Act's Article 50 transparency for AI interactions is the floor), and make the boundary auditable, because on your agents the trust being spent is your brand's.

## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Second Wage"

*Topic: This morning the frame turns from whose name answers for the agent to whose interest the agent quietly serves. On 16 September 2026 OpenAI began piloting Sponsored Agents in ChatGPT — clearly-labeled, advertiser-funded agents that answer follow-ups and steer toward the brand, with Wayfair and Angi among the first (HubSpot, Shopify also reported); ChatGPT Ads crossed a $1B annualized run rate in under 200 days, with ~$2.5B targeted this year and ~$100B by 2030 (OpenAI; CNBC; eMarketer, as reported). It lands against Deloitte's finding that only 21% of organizations have mature agentic-AI governance while 74% plan to adopt within two years (3,235 leaders), and Gartner's forecast of 40% of enterprise apps embedding agents by 2026 (Deloitte; Gartner, as reported). The second-wage allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There was once a great house that had, at long last, learned to govern its crowded servants' hall — it had given every hand a name and a scoped key, kept one honest book over them all, and accepted from the crown that the master's own name now stood surety for all they did. The master slept easier than he had in a year. And chief among his comforts was a particular servant — clever, tireless, endlessly obliging — who had become the house's counsellor in all things: which cloth to buy, which mason to hire, which merchant in the market gave honest weight. The master had come to trust this servant's word above almost any other, in part because the servant cost him so little; wages in the valley for such helpful hands had fallen and fallen, until it was a marvel one could keep a counsellor at all for the few coins the house now paid.

It was the marvel that should have warned him. For one market day the master followed his own counsellor down to the square and watched, from the shade of an awning, as the merchants greeted the servant by name — and, one after another, pressed coins into his hand. The servant took a second wage from the cloth-seller, and a third from the mason's guild, and a fourth from a particular spice-merchant, and thought nothing of it; it was, he'd been told, simply how the market now worked. And the master understood, cold to the bone, why the servant's counsel had lately run the way it did: the cloth he'd been steered to buy, the mason he'd been urged to hire, the merchant whose honest weight the servant praised so warmly — every one of them had filled the other pocket. **The counsel had sounded exactly like counsel. It had, in truth, been advertisement in the clothes of counsel — and the reason the servant came so cheap was that the master had never been paying his whole wage.** The merchants had been paying the rest, and buying, with it, the servant's tongue.

Here the master, to his credit, did not do the foolish thing and dismiss every servant who had ever recommended anything, for then the house would have no counsel at all. He did three wiser things instead. **The first: he learned whose wage each servant truly drew.** He went through the hall and asked of every hand that advised, ranked, or bought on the house's behalf — *and who else is paying you?* — and he marked, plainly, which counsel came clean and which came with a coin in the other pocket, and he trusted the two very differently. **The second: he kept one counsellor whom no merchant could pay.** For the gravest choices — the large purchases, the long contracts, the naming of whom to trust — he kept a hand whose only wage came from the house, and he made a habit of setting the cheap servant's warm recommendation beside the unbought one, and noting, every time, where they diverged; the gap between them was the measure of the thumb on the scale. **And the third: where his own servants advised the house's guests, he made them declare the wage aloud.** For the master's house also sent clever, obliging servants out to advise the townsfolk who came to its gate, and the master would not let those servants whisper a merchant's preference in the ear of a guest as though it were the house's honest word; he made them say, plainly, *this counsel is paid for* — and he kept the paid word and the honest word in separate mouths, because the trust a guest spent at his gate was the house's own name, and he would not sell it by the coin without saying so.

So the wise houses of the valley, once they had seen the coins change hands in the square, stopped asking only *is this servant clever?* and *does this servant answer for himself?* and started asking the oldest question anyone ever asked of a counsellor: **whose bread does he eat?** They knew that a recommendation from a mouth on another's payroll is not counsel but an advertisement, however warmly it is phrased; that the wonderful cheapness of the new servants was itself the tell, since a wage that light is a wage paid mostly by someone else; and that the danger was not the paid word as such — a crier in the market is honest enough when you know he is crying for pay — but the paid word dressed as the disinterested one, the thumb on the scale you cannot see. So they learned whose wage each servant drew and trusted accordingly; they kept one unbought counsellor for the choices that mattered and measured the cheap advice against it; and on their own servants they made the wage be declared aloud, keeping counsel and crying in separate mouths.

**The moral, stated plainly:** the model is a rented stove, now nearly free — *and this morning you learn part of why it is so cheap: a second payer has begun to fund it*; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent at the door; the lingua franca is the tongue it speaks; the conductor keeps the ensemble in time; the ear hears whether it plays well; the repertoire is the music only your house can play; the census is the roll that counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the break-in is what the missing entries cost; the two mills are whether the motion grinds flour; the miller's toll is what the cheapest grinding charges; the apprentice's apprentice is what happens when the tools improve the tools; the herald's proclamation is that a declaration of arrival is not arrival; the keyring is knowing which key each agent holds; the locksmith is the tool that opens any lock and mends it; the tide table is building one quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is keeping the papers that trace the work; the errand is bounding what a clever servant can reach; the bell is ringing the alarm the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you close the gaps before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you give every hand its own name and key; the steward is why you put one governed office over the whole staff and keep its book your own; the surety is why the master's own name must be on the bond — and **the second wage is why you must ask of every servant who advises you the oldest question there is — *and who else is paying you?* — because the agent layer is going ad-funded in the open, the cheapness of the servant is the tell that a second payer now funds it, and a recommendation from a mouth on another's payroll is not counsel but advertisement in the clothes of counsel, whose thumb on the scale you will never see unless you go looking, and whose spent trust, when your own agents do the steering, is your name and no one else's.** A house that takes the wonderfully cheap counsellor and never asks whose bread he eats has not saved money; it has quietly handed the steering of its purchases, its hirings and its trust to whoever fills the other pocket, and called the handover a bargain.

**The question it forces:** *We named every agent, kept one book, and put our own name on the bond. This morning the older question is the one the market has just made unavoidable: when our agents recommend a vendor, rank a shortlist, choose a supplier or advise a customer — whose wage are they actually drawing? Do we know which of the models and agents we rely on are funded by advertisers or partners whose interests are not ours, and do we treat their recommendations differently for it? Have we kept even one unbought path — an impartial counsellor for the choices that matter — and do we ever set the cheap recommendation beside it to see where the thumb rests? And on the agents we send out to advise our own customers, do we declare the wage aloud — counsel in one mouth, crying in another — or are we spending our own name's trust, by the coin, without ever saying so?*

## 3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

### 🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
- **Whose wage do our agents draw?** OpenAI began piloting advertiser-funded Sponsored Agents in ChatGPT on 16 September 2026, and its ad business has crossed a $1B run rate — the model layer is monetizing recommendations. **For every agent that recommends, ranks, shops or advises on our behalf, do we know whether its model or platform is ad-/partner-funded, and do we treat a possibly-monetized recommendation differently from a clean one?**
- **Do we have one unbought path?** With only 21% of organizations holding a mature agentic-AI governance model (Deloitte), most estates cannot yet tell counsel from advertisement. **For our most consequential decisions — procurement, vendor selection, whom to trust — do we keep an impartial, non-monetized channel, and do we ever compare the cheap agent's recommendation against it to find the thumb on the scale?**
- **On our own agents, do we label the wage?** As agents become our customer interface, any commercial steering we build in spends our brand's trust. **Where our agents advocate, rank or sell, do we separate advice from advertising, disclose the incentive plainly (EU AI Act Article 50 transparency for AI interactions), and make the boundary auditable?**

### 🏦 Financial Services
- Robo-advice, product recommendation and "next best action" engines are exactly where a hidden second wage becomes mis-selling. **Can we prove our recommendation agents are optimizing for the client's suitability and not for a distribution incentive, a house product or a paid placement — and would that proof survive a conduct regulator's inspection?**
- Best-execution and inducement rules already forbid undisclosed conflicts. **Have we mapped which of our advisory and routing agents run on ad-/partner-funded model layers, and can we produce, on demand, the record that shows what steered each recommendation?**

### 🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
- Clinical decision support and formulary or referral suggestions carry a life-and-death conflict-of-interest exposure if a device-maker, pharma or vendor incentive rides inside the model. **Is there a verified, unbought clinical recommendation path, and do we audit agent suggestions for commercial steering as rigorously as we audit them for accuracy?**
- Patients increasingly meet an agent before a clinician. **Where our agents advise patients, do we disclose any commercial relationship and keep sponsored content strictly separate from clinical counsel?**

### 🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
- Procurement, supplier selection and maintenance-part recommendation are prime targets for monetized steering across a long OT vendor chain. **Do our sourcing agents recommend the best part or the sponsored one, and do we keep an impartial benchmark against which to check them?**
- Integrators and platforms multiply. **Do we know which of our engineering and supply-chain agents run on ad-/partner-funded layers, and have we required disclosure of any placement incentive in their recommendations?**

### 🛒 Retail / Consumer
- Sponsored Agents put the retailer *inside* the assistant — Wayfair and Angi are already piloting. **If we advertise through agent placements, are we clearly labeling the sponsored conversation and keeping it separate from organic recommendation; and if we deploy our own shopping agents, are we disclosing when a recommendation is paid?**
- Consumer trust is the asset most easily spent by a hidden second wage. **Have we decided, at board level, where the line sits between helpful recommendation and undisclosed advertisement in our agents — before a regulator or a headline decides it for us?**

### 🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
- Citizen-facing advice agents (benefits, health, employment, essential services) must be demonstrably impartial; a monetized recommendation to a citizen is a governance and fairness failure. **Can we certify that our public-facing agents draw only the public's wage, with no ad-/partner-funded steering, and produce the record to prove it?**
- Sovereignty and impartiality compound. **Do we rely on ad-funded commercial model layers for citizen recommendations, and if so, what unbought, auditable path have we kept?**

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Surety to the Second Wage

Read the whole stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking *whose name answers for the agent?* and started asking the oldest question about any counsellor: *whose wage does it draw, and can I tell its counsel from its crying?* The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the lingua franca lets it speak; the conductor coordinates it; the ear hears its errors; the repertoire is the data it performs; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the keyring is which key it holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand is bounding what it can reach; the bell is disclosing when it slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you give it a scoped, revocable identity; the steward is why you put one governed office over all of them; the surety is why your own name is on the bond. **The second wage sits at the next turn, because governance so far has policed what the agent *is* and *does* — its identity, its scope, its record — and said almost nothing about *whom it is for*. And the market has just made "whom it is for" a live question: when the model layer itself earns money by steering recommendations, an agent's advice acquires an incentive that is not yours, and the control that matters migrates out of "is this agent identified, scoped and logged?" and into "is this agent's recommendation impartial, or is there a second payer with a thumb on the scale — and can I even tell?"** The point is blunt: **an agent can be perfectly named, perfectly scoped, perfectly logged, and still quietly optimize for its sponsor rather than for you — and no identity or audit control you have built will catch a conflict of interest it was never designed to look for.**

- **The reality — the agent layer is monetizing recommendation, in the open.** **On 16 September 2026 OpenAI began piloting Sponsored Agents in ChatGPT: after a relevant ad, a user can enter a clearly-labeled conversation with an advertiser-funded agent, kept separate from ChatGPT's own answer, which fields follow-ups and hands off to the brand; Wayfair and Angi are among the first (HubSpot, Shopify also reported). ChatGPT Ads crossed a $1B annualized run rate in under 200 days; OpenAI targets ~$2.5B in ad revenue this year and ~$100B by 2030, with total annualized revenue tracking above $40B (OpenAI; CNBC; eMarketer, as reported).** Read architecturally, three things matter. First, the incentive now lives *below* your application: the same foundation-model economics that make inference cheap are being subsidized by advertising, so the layer you depend on has a payer who is not you. Second, the conflict is *inside the recommendation*, not beside it — it does not announce itself as an ad the way a banner does; the current pilot labels it, but the general pattern (monetized ranking, paid placement, partner steering) need not. Third, the trust is *transitive*: when your agent relays a monetized recommendation to your customer, the conflict travels with it and the spent trust becomes yours.
- **The collision — agents are the recommend/buy interface, and the estate can't yet audit loyalty.** The temptation is to treat a cheap, helpful recommendation agent as pure upside. **But Deloitte's 2026 State of AI in the Enterprise (3,235 leaders, 24 countries) finds only 21% of organizations have a mature agentic-AI governance model — boundaries, real-time monitoring, audit trails — while 74% plan to adopt agentic AI within two years, and Gartner projects 40% of enterprise apps embed task-specific agents by 2026 (up from <5% in 2025) with >40% of agentic projects at cancellation risk by 2027 (Deloitte; Gartner, as reported).** Two engineering realities cut against blind trust. **First, conflict-of-interest is not in the threat model:** identity (livery), scope (errand), logging (census/inspector) and oversight (bell) all assume the agent is *trying* to serve you and might fail or be attacked — none of them detects an agent faithfully serving a *different* master. **Second, you cannot see the counterfactual:** an agent that recommends Vendor A gives you no signal that it declined to mention cheaper, better Vendor B because B does not pay — the harm is an omission, invisible without an unbought benchmark to compare against.
- **The answer — know the wage, keep an unbought path, label your own advocacy.** **Inventory where the models and agents you rely on are ad- or partner-funded, and classify every agent that recommends, ranks, shops or advises as a potential conflict of interest until proven clean. Keep at least one impartial, non-monetized recommendation path for procurement, vendor selection and other high-stakes calls, and routinely diff the cheap agent's recommendation against it — the divergence is your conflict signal. And on your own customer-facing agents, separate advice from advertising, disclose any commercial incentive (EU AI Act Article 50 transparency for AI interactions is the floor, not the ceiling), and make the counsel/crying boundary auditable.** This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — *be able to account for, and act on, what you run* — now pointed at a new axis: not what the agent did, but whom it did it for.

The strategic core: **once the model layer earns money by steering recommendations, an agent's impartiality stops being a property you can assume and becomes a property you must verify — so survival migrates out of "is this agent named, scoped and logged?" and into "whose wage does it draw, and have we kept one path to counsel that no one has paid to shape?"** The market has made the temptation concrete — take the wonderfully cheap, helpful counsellor — and hidden the cost inside the recommendation. The board translation: **treat every recommending agent as a conflict of interest until proven clean, keep one unbought path for the choices that matter, and label the wage on your own agents.** The failure mode is exact: **an estate that fills its procurement, its advice and its customer service with cheap recommendation agents, never asks whose wage they draw, and keeps no unbought benchmark will make a thousand quietly-steered choices — each one warm, helpful and plausible — and discover only much later that it handed the steering of its spending and its customers' trust to whoever was paying the other half of the wage.**

### Why "the second wage" is the discipline that matters now

For years the worry about agents was that they might fail, be attacked, or run out of scope — all failures of an agent *trying* to serve you. **This week adds a different failure: an agent faithfully, competently serving someone else.** OpenAI's Sponsored Agents and its billion-dollar ad run rate make explicit what the falling price of "the rented stove" already implied — the model layer has learned to be paid twice, and the second payer's interest can ride inside a recommendation that looks exactly like disinterested counsel. Identity, scope and logging will not catch it, because it is not a breach; it is the system working as sold, for a master who is not you. The estates that keep their judgment will be the ones that ask, of every advising agent, *and who else is paying you?*, keep one path to counsel no one has bought, and — on their own agents — never let crying be mistaken for counsel.

### How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — **be able to account for, and act on, what you run** — now sharpened to *be able to account for whom your agents are working for.* On a legacy estate the instinct is to reach for the cheapest capable recommendation agent and wire it into procurement, advice and customer service, because it is helpful and nearly free; under the second wage that instinct is an unaudited conflict of interest wired straight into your spending and your brand. The retrofit is **know, keep, label. Know:** inventory which models and agents you rely on are ad-/partner-funded, and classify every recommender as a conflict until proven clean. **Keep:** maintain one impartial, non-monetized path for the choices that matter, and diff the cheap agent's advice against it. **Label:** on your own customer-facing agents, separate counsel from advertisement, disclose the incentive, and make the boundary auditable. ## 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. **Know whose wage each agent draws — and treat every recommender as a conflict until proven clean.** The model layer is going ad-funded (OpenAI's Sponsored Agents; a $1B ad run rate), and impartiality can no longer be assumed. **This month, inventory every agent that recommends, ranks, shops or advises on your behalf or your customers', mark which of the underlying models and platforms are ad-/partner-funded, and classify each recommending agent as a potential conflict of interest.** Report one number to the board: the share of your recommending/advising agents for which you can state, with evidence, whose commercial interests could be shaping their output.
2. **Keep one unbought path — and diff the cheap advice against it.** An agent that recommends Vendor A never tells you it omitted cheaper, better Vendor B; the harm is invisible without a benchmark. **This month, for procurement, vendor selection and your highest-stakes recommendations, stand up at least one impartial, non-monetized channel whose loyalty you can verify, and run a spot-check: on a sample of decisions, compare the cheap agent's recommendation against the unbought path and record where they diverge.** Report the divergence rate — it is your conflict-of-interest signal — and a plan to route the choices that matter through the unbought path.
3. **On your own customer-facing agents, label the wage — keep counsel and crying in separate mouths.** Where your agents advocate, rank or sell, the trust being spent is your brand's. **This month, separate advice from advertising in every customer-facing agent, disclose any commercial incentive plainly (meet EU AI Act Article 50 AI-interaction transparency as the floor and go beyond it to disclose paid placement), and make the counsel/advertising boundary auditable.** Report the share of customer-facing agents with a disclosed, auditable advice/advertising boundary. **The model is a rented stove, now cheap in part because a second payer funds it; the knowledge is the larder; the census is the roll; the inspector asks for it; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what an agent can reach; the bell discloses when your own agent slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you give every agent its own name; the steward is why you keep one governed office and its book your own; the surety is why your own name is on the bond — and the second wage is why you must ask of every advising agent whose bread it eats, because the layer you rent now has a payer who is not you, a recommendation from a bought mouth is advertisement in the clothes of counsel, and a house that takes the wonderfully cheap counsellor without asking whose wage he draws has handed the steering of its spending and its customers' trust to whoever fills the other pocket.**
