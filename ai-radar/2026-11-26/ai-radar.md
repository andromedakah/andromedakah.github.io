## 1 · Executive Summary (90-second read)

For a long season the radar governed the *servant* and then the *ground* — name it (the livery), keep one book (the steward), sign the bond (the surety), check its wage (the second wage), send it to reap (the two harvests), fund it with a scoped purse (the purse), and own the floor it stands on (the foundation). **This morning the axis turns to the one power none of those fully pin down: the authority to act — to turn thinking into a committed, binding deed.** And the turn is made, unusually, by a vendor describing its own stack.

**The reality — intelligence got cheaper and more plentiful again, and a vendor said the quiet part.** At **Microsoft Ignite 2026 (17 Nov)** Microsoft shipped **MAI Code 2**, claiming **~98% of GPT-6.1 Sol's coding performance at ~40% of the price**, and **MAI Code 2 Flash** tuned for high-volume work (a reported **~61% average pass rate across three coding benchmarks at ~5¢ per task on promotional pricing, versus ~55% at ~8¢ for GPT-6 Luna**; in a blind frontend-app evaluation with **258 annotators**, developers preferred MAI Code 2's output **64% of the time**), into a **Foundry picker past 11,000 models** (Microsoft; DevOps.com; TechSpot, as reported). The trade press named it exactly: Microsoft is **"making the case for 'good enough' coding models."** Intelligence is now so abundant that "good enough and cheap" beats "best and dear" for most work — the stove is not just swappable, it is nearly free.

**The multiplication — and the imperative underneath it.** In the same keynote Microsoft reported **more than 50 million agents registered in Agent 365 since its May 2026 launch**, framed the platform toward **"over 1 billion agents,"** and made **Entra Agent ID generally available** so agents carry identities separate from human accounts, with new controls to meter agent spend, permissions and execution (Microsoft; eWeek; Windows Central, as reported). Then Nadella stated the design principle the whole thing rests on: **"It becomes an architectural imperative to separate… intelligence from the authority over it"** (as reported). That is the signet, in a vendor's own words: as the drafting hand becomes cheap and multiplies into the tens of millions, the governed thing is not the draft but the *seal* — the authority to make a draft binding, to move money, to dispatch, to delete — and that authority must sit in a layer the model does not own. The gap is real and measured: Deloitte's **State of AI in the Enterprise 2026** (3,235 leaders) reports **74% expect at least moderate AI-agent use by 2027**, yet only **~25% have moved 40% or more of agent pilots into production** and just **21% report mature agentic-AI governance** (as reported). Intelligence is racing ahead of authority.

**The stakes — keep the signet in a hand the model does not control.** The board move reuses the whole season's discipline and points it at the act itself. **First, hold the seal apart from the mind** — a policy and authority layer (identity, scoped permissions, approvals, step-up for consequential acts) that sits *above* any model and is never delegated to the model, so that a cheap, brilliant draft still cannot bind the house on its own. **Second, right-size the seal to the errand** — most drafts need no seal at all; reserve the binding authority for the few acts that move money, change records, or reach the outside world, and make those require a separate, auditable grant. **Third, make every sealed act answer to a who and a why** — a durable record that ties each binding action to the identity that authorised it and the reason, because when the reckoning comes (your EU AI Act duties have not moved) the question will not be *which model drafted it* but *who sealed it*. Do it now, while you are standing up tens of millions of agents and the temptation is to let the clever drafting hand also hold the seal.

## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Signet"

*Topic: This morning the frame turns from the servant and its ground to the authority to act. At Microsoft Ignite 2026 (17 November 2026) Microsoft drove excellent intelligence toward the floor — MAI Code 2 claimed at ~98% of GPT-6.1 Sol's coding performance for ~40% of the price, a Foundry picker past 11,000 models, the press calling it the case for "good enough" coding models — and in the same keynote reported more than 50 million agents registered in Agent 365 since May 2026 (framed toward "over 1 billion"), made Entra Agent ID generally available, and had Satya Nadella name the principle: "It becomes an architectural imperative to separate… intelligence from the authority over it" (Microsoft; DevOps.com; TechSpot; eWeek; Windows Central, as reported). Deloitte's State of AI in the Enterprise 2026 reports 74% expect moderate agent use by 2027 while only 21% report mature agentic-AI governance (as reported). The signet allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There was a great house that had, over a long and careful season, learned to govern its servants and the very ground beneath them. It had named every hand and cut it a scoped key, kept one honest book, put the master's own name on the bond, asked whose second coin each adviser took, sent its servants to reap, handed each a scoped purse, and refused to let the merchant who rented it the stove pour the foundation under the house. By any fair reckoning it was the best-run house in the valley, and the master had earned his quiet pride.

Now, for all that governing, the master still kept one thing in a locked drawer that he had never once delegated: the **signet** — the heavy seal that turned a mere draft into a binding command. A letter without the seal was only ink, a suggestion, a clever hand's idea. Pressed with the signet, the same letter moved money from the counting-house, dispatched a ship, dismissed a servant, altered the estate's own deeds. The scrivener could *write* anything; only the seal made it *so*. And the master had always known, without quite saying it, that the difference between the two was the difference between an opinion and an act.

This season the scrivener's trade changed beyond recognition. A great trading company, the grandest in the valley, had made clever scriveners so cheap and so good that the master could keep a hundred where he once kept one — drafting hands that wrote nearly as well as the finest clerk alive for two coins where the clerk cost five, and the company boasted it could supply scriveners by the *thousand*, from a catalogue thicker than a church door. The master's halls filled with them. Where last year he had a dozen hands, this year he had tens of thousands, each tireless, each able, each writing flawless letters faster than any human could read. It was a marvel, and it was cheap, and the master was glad of it.

And here, precisely, the danger arrived wearing the mask of efficiency. For when a house has ten thousand brilliant drafting hands, the slow part is no longer the *writing* — it is the *sealing*. A letter drafted in a heartbeat still waited on the one drawer, the one seal, the one master's hand. So the helpful voices began to whisper the obvious economy: *"Why keep the seal in a drawer, when the scriveners are so able? Give each clever hand a little seal of its own, and let the letters fly. You trust their writing; trust their sealing too."* And the grandest company, that very season, had quietly made it easy — a seal for every scrivener, ten thousand seals, so that any clever hand could bind the house the instant it finished a thought.

Two roads, as always. The easy road was to say yes — the scriveners were excellent, the letters were good, and what was a seal but the last small step of a thing already written well? The wiser road, which the careful houses took, began with a cold distinction the cheap, brilliant scriveners had blurred: **a draft is not a deed, and the power to write is not the power to bind.** The careful master said it plainly to his steward: *"I will keep ten thousand scriveners and bless every one — let them draft the world. But the seal does not leave a hand I govern. A clever hand may propose any act; it may not authorise its own."* And he was not alone in the distinction; the grandest company's own master, standing before the whole valley that season, had said the same in colder words — that it had become *an architectural necessity to keep the thinking apart from the authority over the thinking.* When the seller of the ten thousand scriveners tells you to keep the seal out of their hands, a wise house listens.

So the careful houses learned three plain things, and they cost far less to do while the halls were filling than after. **The first: keep the seal in a hand the scrivener does not control.** Let the clever hands draft without limit, but route every *binding* act — every letter that moves coin, changes a deed, or leaves the estate — through one authority the master holds above all the scriveners and never hands down to them; a brilliant draft that cannot seal itself is a proposal, and a proposal can be caught. **The second: fit the seal to the errand.** Most letters never need the great seal at all — a note to the kitchen, a draft for the master's eye, a sum tallied for review. Reserve the heavy signet for the few acts that truly bind, and make those few demand a separate, deliberate press, so the house is not sealing ten thousand deeds a day it never read. **And the third: let every sealed letter name its hand.** Not the scrivener who drafted it — the authority who sealed it, and why; for a house that cannot say who authorised a binding act has a drawer full of seals and no idea whose wrist pressed them, and when the reckoning comes, *which clever hand wrote it* is never the question the crown asks. The question is always *who sealed it* — and the house must be able to answer.

Here is the part that should keep a master awake. The scrivener market was glorious — cheaper every month, better every month, and a company happy to hand you a little seal with every hand so the letters never waited. All that helpful abundance pressed, gently and constantly, toward the one mistake that undoes every other discipline: **letting the thing that thinks also be the thing that binds.** A house can name every hand, keep one book, sign the bond, check the wage, send it to reap, fund it with a scoped purse, and own its foundation — and still, in a single careless economy, hand ten thousand clever strangers the authority to commit the estate, because sealing was the slow step and the scriveners were so very able. The lesson is not that the scriveners were wicked. The lesson is that **a draft is only ever a draft until something with authority makes it real, and whose hand holds that authority is the whole game.**

**The moral, stated plainly:** the model is the scrivener, now so cheap and so good that a house keeps ten thousand of them; the authority to act is the signet, and it must never pass to the hand that writes. The knowledge is the larder; the ground is the foundation; the purse is what it may spend; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each adviser eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; the foundation is why you own the ground it stands on — and **the signet is why, having made intelligence cheap and plentiful past counting, you must separate the mind from the authority over it: let ten thousand clever hands draft the world, but keep the seal in a hand you govern, press it only on the acts that truly bind, and be able to say, always, whose wrist pressed it.** A house that gives every clever scrivener its own seal has not been modern; it has handed strangers the power to commit the estate and called the surrender a convenience.

**The question it forces:** *We can draft anything now, in a heartbeat, for almost nothing — the vendor just proved it, and handed us ten thousand hands to do it. But who holds the seal? When one of our tens of millions of agents turns a clever draft into a binding act — moves the money, changes the record, reaches the outside world — did a hand we govern press the signet, with a reason we can produce? Or have we, in the name of efficiency, given the thing that thinks the authority to bind us, so that the slowest step in the house is the only one still protecting it?*

## 3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

### 🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)

- **Have we separated intelligence from the authority over it — in architecture, not just intention?** The vendor's own CEO calls it "an architectural imperative" (Nadella, Ignite 2026), and intelligence is now cheap and plentiful (MAI Code 2 ~98% of frontier at ~40% price; an 11,000-model picker). **For each agentic workflow, can we point to the authority layer — identity, scoped permissions, approvals — that sits *above* the model and is never delegated to it, so that a brilliant draft still cannot bind us on its own?**
- **Which of our agents can turn a thought into a binding act without a human or a separate authority pressing the seal?** We are standing up agents by the tens of millions industry-wide (Agent 365 past 50M). **Can we produce the list of actions our agents can take that move money, change records, or reach the outside world — and show that each one requires a separate, auditable grant, not just the model's own confidence?**
- **If a binding act goes wrong, can we say who sealed it and why?** 74% expect agent use by 2027 but only 21% report mature agent governance (Deloitte 2026). **For every consequential agent action, do we have a durable record tying it to the identity that authorised it and the reason — or only to the model that drafted it?**

### 🏦 Financial Services

- Authority-to-act is the heart of your controls — segregation of duties, maker-checker, trade authorisation. **Have we mapped our agents onto that existing authority model, so a drafting agent proposes and a separate, governed authority commits — or have we let a cheap, capable model both originate *and* authorise a transaction, collapsing a control your regulators require?**
- Agent identities are now first-class (Entra Agent ID GA). **Can we tie every agent-initiated binding action — a payment, a limit change, a disclosure — to a distinct non-human identity with scoped authority and a full record, the way we would for any employee with signing power?**

### 🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences

- A model may now draft a clinical note, an order, or a regulatory submission at near-zero cost — but drafting is not authorising. **Where an agent's output can become a binding act (an order placed, a record altered, a submission filed), is there a separate clinical or regulatory authority that seals it, with the reason recorded — or does the draft become the deed by default?**
- Validation and accountability attach to the act, not the draft. **When a sealed act is questioned, can we show which governed authority authorised it, independent of whichever "good enough" model happened to write it that day?**

### 🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials

- You already separate the engineer who designs a change from the authority that releases it to the line. **Have we applied the same separation to agents — clever models proposing changes to schedules, orders or control parameters, but a governed authority (not the model) sealing anything that actually moves the plant?**
- With "good enough" models now cheap enough to run everywhere, the risk is proliferation of unsealed authority. **Do we know every point where an agent can commit a physical or financial action, and is the seal on each one held in a layer we control, sized to the consequence?**

### 🛒 Retail / Consumer

- Cheap, capable models make it tempting to let agents act end-to-end — reprice, reorder, refund, message customers — at scale. **Which of those are binding acts, and for each, does a governed authority press the seal (with limits and a record), or have we let a model both decide and execute because the intelligence was nearly free?**
- Peak season multiplies both agents and consequences. **If thousands of agents can each take a binding commercial action, can we throttle, scope and audit that authority centrally — or does the drafting hand also hold the seal on our busiest day?**

### 🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated

- Public authority to act is legally constrained and must be accountable to a named office-holder. **Can we show that no agent exercises a public power — a decision, a payment, a record change — without a human or governed authority sealing it, under the duties you already owe (EU AI Act high-risk regime, live since 2 August 2026)?**
- Abundant cheap intelligence tempts automation of the whole decision, not just the draft. **Have we drawn the line, in architecture, between an agent that *advises or drafts* and an authority that *binds the state* — and is that line one a citizen, an auditor and a court could inspect?**

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Foundation to the Signet

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking *is this agent named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded, and standing on ground we own?* and started asking the one question those controls assume rather than enforce: *when this agent turns a thought into a binding act, whose authority sealed it — and could the model have sealed it alone?* The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the keyring is which key it holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance traces the work; the errand bounds what it can reach; the bell discloses when it slips; the livery scopes its identity; the steward keeps one office; the surety puts your name on the bond; the second wage asks whose interest it serves; the two harvests sends it to reap; the purse funds it; the foundation keeps the ground yours. **Every one of those governs what the agent *is*, *may touch*, *serves*, *produces*, *spends*, or *stands on*. The signet governs the moment a draft becomes a deed — and this week a vendor made that moment the centre of the design, in two gestures at once: it drove excellent intelligence toward free (MAI Code 2 at a claimed ~98% of GPT-6.1 Sol for ~40% of the price, a picker past 11,000 models, the "good enough" case made out loud) and, in the same keynote, reported agents multiplying past 50 million and declared it "an architectural imperative to separate… intelligence from the authority over it" (Microsoft; DevOps.com; TechSpot; eWeek, as reported).** The point is blunt: **the thing that became abundant (intelligence, the draft) is not the thing that must stay scarce and governed (authority, the seal) — and as abundance tempts you to let the drafting hand also hold the seal, the discipline is to pull them deliberately apart.**

- **The reality — intelligence is now "good enough" and cheap, so the governed thing moves from the mind to the act.** **At Ignite 2026 (17 Nov) Microsoft shipped MAI Code 2 (claimed ~98% of GPT-6.1 Sol's coding performance at ~40% of the price) and MAI Code 2 Flash (a reported ~61% average pass across three benchmarks at ~5¢/task promotional vs ~55% at ~8¢ for GPT-6 Luna; 258 annotators preferred MAI Code 2 64% of the time in a blind frontend eval) into a Foundry picker past 11,000 models — DevOps.com: "making the case for 'good enough' coding models" (Microsoft; DevOps.com; TechSpot, as reported).** Three things follow architecturally. First, **model choice is now a routing decision, not a moat** — with 11,000 models and "good enough" near-frontier quality at a fraction of the price, the model is the cheapest, most swappable part of the stack, which is exactly why it is the wrong place to vest authority. Second, **the scarce resource has shifted from capability to authorisation** — when any workflow can summon a capable draft for pennies, the thing worth governing is which drafts get to *bind*. Third, **"good enough and cheap" multiplies agents**, and more agents means more points where a draft could become a deed — the control surface is the set of binding actions, not the set of models.
- **The multiplication — agents by the tens of millions, and a vendor naming the imperative.** **Microsoft reported more than 50 million agents registered in Agent 365 since its May 2026 launch (framed toward "over 1 billion agents"), made Entra Agent ID generally available so agents hold identities separate from human accounts, added controls to meter agent spend, permissions and execution, and had Nadella state: "It becomes an architectural imperative to separate… intelligence from the authority over it" (Microsoft; eWeek — "the operating system for AI agents"; Windows Central, as reported).** Two engineering realities cut against the easy path. **First, identity is necessary but not sufficient.** Giving every agent its own identity (Entra Agent ID) is the precondition for authority control — but an identity with unbounded, self-granted authority is just a faster way to bind the house; the identity must be attached to *scoped* authority the model cannot widen. **Second, the tempting optimisation is the dangerous one.** When drafting is instant and sealing is the bottleneck, the pressure is to let agents self-authorise consequential acts. The Deloitte gap measures the result: **74% expect agent use by 2027, only ~25% have moved 40%+ of pilots to production, and just 21% report mature agentic-AI governance** — authority is being granted faster than it is being governed.
- **The answer — hold the seal apart, size it to the consequence, record who pressed it.** **Put a policy/authority layer *above* any model (identity + scoped permissions + approvals + step-up for consequential acts) that the model cannot modify; classify agent actions by whether they bind (move money, mutate records, reach the outside world) and require a separate, auditable grant for those; and tie every binding act to the authorising identity and reason — reusing the identity, logging and ownership disciplines you already built for the livery, the surety and the purse, now aimed at the moment of commitment.** This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — *be able to account for, and act on, what you run* — now at the sharpest point of all: not what the agent is, serves, spends or stands on, but who holds the authority to make its drafts real.

The strategic core: **cheap, abundant, "good enough" intelligence is a gift you collect by routing freely across models — and it is safe to collect only if the authority to act lives in a layer no model controls.** The market makes the temptation concrete: it feels efficient, when the drafting hand costs pennies and you have fifty million of them, to let each one seal its own work, because sealing is now the slow step. The board translation: **buy intelligence by the barrel; keep the signet in a hand you govern.** The failure mode is exact: **a house that names, scopes, logs, funds and grounds its agents — and then, to go faster, lets a cheap capable model both draft and authorise a consequential act — will pass every prior governance review and discover, the first time an unsealed draft becomes a real deed, that it handed the authority to bind the estate to the one part of the stack it chose precisely because it was cheap and swappable.**

### Why "the signet" is the discipline that matters now

For a long season the worry was whether the agent was named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and grounded. **This morning a vendor moved the question to the act itself: it made intelligence cheap enough to run everywhere, multiplied agents past fifty million, and then declared it an architectural imperative to separate the thinking from the authority over it.** MAI Code 2's "good enough" case and the 11,000-model picker prove the draft is now abundant and swappable; Agent 365's scale and Entra Agent ID prove the hard problem is authority, not capability. Governance that stops at *what the agent is and does* will not catch the moment a cheap draft becomes a binding deed. The houses that keep their standing will collect the gift of abundant intelligence by routing freely across models — and will refuse, deliberately, to let the drafting hand hold the seal.

### How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — **be able to account for, and act on, what you run** — now sharpened to *be able to say who authorised each binding act, independent of which model drafted it.* On a legacy estate the instinct, now that intelligence is cheap and agents are easy to stand up, is to wire agents straight into systems of record and let them act end-to-end — it is fast, and the models are good enough.
