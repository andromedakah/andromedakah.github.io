1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from governing the servant and its ground to the authority to act — because intelligence just got cheaper and more plentiful, and the governed scarcity shifts from the model (the draft) to the seal (the authority to make a draft binding).

1. The reality — "good enough" intelligence is cheap and everywhere. At Microsoft Ignite 2026 (17 Nov) Microsoft shipped MAI Code 2, claiming ~98% of GPT-6.1 Sol's coding performance at ~40% of the price, and Flash (a reported ~61% avg pass at ~5¢/task; 258 annotators preferred MAI Code 2 64% of the time), into a Foundry picker past 11,000 models — the press calls it the case for "good enough" coding models (Microsoft; DevOps.com; TechSpot, as reported). The drafting hand is nearly free.

2. The multiplication — and the imperative underneath. Microsoft reported more than 50 million agents registered in Agent 365 since its May 2026 launch (framed toward "over 1 billion"), made Entra Agent ID generally available, and had Nadella state: "it becomes an architectural imperative to separate… intelligence from the authority over it" (Microsoft; eWeek; as reported). The scarce thing is the seal, not the mind.

3. The answer — hold the seal apart, size it to the errand, record who sealed it. Keep an authority layer the model does not own; reserve binding power for consequential acts; and tie every sealed act to a who and a why — because 74% expect agent use by 2027 but only 21% report mature agent governance (Deloitte 2026, as reported). Intelligence is outrunning authority.

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each governed what the agent is, serves, spends or stands on — the signet is about the authority to turn a clever draft into a binding deed. A vendor made intelligence nearly free and then said the architecture now requires separating it from the authority over it. Buy intelligence by the barrel — but never hand the drafting hand your signet.

2 · Allegory — The Signet

A great house had learned to govern its servants and the ground beneath them — named, booked, bonded, waged, sent to reap, funded with a scoped purse, standing on a foundation it owned. Yet it still kept one thing in a locked drawer it had never delegated: the signet — the seal that turned a mere draft into a binding command. The scrivener could write anything; only the seal made it so. The difference was the difference between an opinion and an act.

This season a great company made clever scriveners so cheap and good that the master kept ten thousand where he once kept a dozen. And when a house has ten thousand drafting hands, the slow step is no longer the writing — it is the sealing. So the helpful voices whispered the obvious economy: "Give each clever hand a little seal of its own, and let the letters fly." The wiser master refused, on a cold distinction: a draft is not a deed, and the power to write is not the power to bind. And the grandest company's own master had said the same in colder words — that it had become an architectural necessity to keep the thinking apart from the authority over it.

So the careful houses learned three things: keep the seal in a hand the scrivener does not control (route every binding act through one authority held above the models); fit the seal to the errand (reserve the heavy signet for the few acts that truly bind); and let every sealed letter name its hand (the authority who sealed it, and why) — for when the reckoning comes, which clever hand wrote it is never the question. The question is always who sealed it.

The question it forces: We can draft anything now, in a heartbeat, for almost nothing — and the vendor handed us ten thousand hands to do it. But who holds the seal? When one of our tens of millions of agents turns a clever draft into a binding act, did a hand we govern press the signet, with a reason we can produce — or have we given the thing that thinks the authority to bind us?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Signet

Every prior control governs what the agent is, serves, spends or stands on; the signet governs the moment a draft becomes a deed. The thing that became abundant (intelligence, the draft) is not the thing that must stay scarce and governed (authority, the seal) — and confusing the two is how a house that feels efficient ends up letting the cheapest, most swappable part of the stack bind the estate. The reality: with 11,000 models and "good enough" near-frontier quality at a fraction of the price, model choice is a routing decision, not a moat — so it is the wrong place to vest authority; the scarce resource shifts from capability to authorisation. The multiplication: identity (Entra Agent ID) is necessary but not sufficient — an identity with unbounded, self-granted authority is just a faster way to bind the house; and the tempting optimisation (letting agents self-authorise because sealing is the bottleneck) is the dangerous one (74% expect agent use by 2027, only 21% report mature governance). The answer: put an authority layer above any model the model cannot modify, classify which actions bind, require a separate scoped grant for those, and record the authorising identity and reason.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Separate the seal from the mind. Intelligence is cheap and "good enough" (MAI Code 2 ~98% of frontier at ~40% price; an 11,000-model picker), which is why the model is the wrong place to vest authority. Put identity, scoped permissions and approvals in a policy layer above every model that no model can modify, so a brilliant draft is a proposal until a governed authority seals it. Report the share of agent workflows whose authority lives above the model versus inside it.

2. Size the seal to the consequence. With agents past 50 million and adoption at 88%, the control surface is the set of binding acts. Inventory every action your agents can take and tag each as drafting (no seal) or binding (moves money, mutates a record, or reaches the outside world), and require a separate, auditable grant for the binding ones, with step-up for the most consequential. Report the count of binding agent actions and the share requiring a separate authorisation.

3. Record the wrist. 74% expect agent use by 2027 but only 21% report mature governance (Deloitte 2026); identity is the precondition, not the answer. Make every binding agent action write a durable record linking it to the authorising identity and reason, and test that you can answer "who sealed this, and why?" for a sampled consequential act — because the EU AI Act reckoning never asks which model drafted it. Report whether you can produce, for any binding agent action in the last 30 days, the authority that sealed it.
