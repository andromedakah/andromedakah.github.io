# 🗓️ AI Tech Radar — The Reckoning

**Wednesday, 25 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering**

> You spent a long season perfecting the servant and then securing the ground it stands on — you named it, kept one book, bonded it, checked its wage, sent it to reap, handed it a scoped purse, and refused to let the stove-seller pour your foundation. All of that was about *who the servant is, what it may touch, and whose ground it works on.* This morning a colder question arrives, and it comes in the form of the first reckoning. New analyst work out this month puts hard numbers on it: a **16 November IDC study commissioned by Quantexa — "The Context Gap: What Agentic AI Needs to Scale," surveying 366 technology leaders at large banks, insurers and government agencies — reports that AI agents already run in ~21.6% of the workflows organizations deem suitable, and leaders expect that to reach ~32.1% within twelve months; yet only ~8.7% of organizations have the context an agent needs "broadly available or embedded," and barely ~17% have broadly implemented knowledge graphs — the weakest capability of all** (IDC/Quantexa; CIO Dive, as reported). The study's blunt edge is the explainability one: *without lineage and decision data, a bank cannot answer how its own agent reached a decision* (as reported). So the house is sending governed, bonded, well-shod servants out into one field in five — soon one in three — **without the knowledge of the house in their heads, and without keeping any record of why they did what they did.** That is fine right up until the first reckoning: the morning the board, the regulator or the customer asks of a single decision, *"on what did it act, and why did it choose so?"* — and the honest answer, for ~91% of houses, is a shrug. You perfected the servant and you own the ground. **You have not yet given it the knowledge of the house, nor required it to keep the reasons for what it did** — and the reckoning does not accept a shrug.

## 1 · Executive Summary (90-second read)

For a long season the radar worked on the *servant* — name it (the livery), keep one book (the steward), put your name on the bond (the surety), check its wage (the second wage), send it to reap (the two harvests), hand it a scoped purse (the purse) — and then on the *ground*, refusing to let a vendor pour the foundation under the whole estate. Every one of those disciplines concerns **who the agent is, what it may touch, what it brings in, and whose floor it stands on.** **This morning the axis turns inward: not who the servant is, but what it *knows* — and whether it can give an account of what it did.**

**The reality — deployment is racing ahead of context.** A **16 November IDC study commissioned by Quantexa, "The Context Gap: What Agentic AI Needs to Scale"** (366 technology leaders at large banks, insurers and government agencies), reports that **AI agents already operate in ~21.6% of the workflows organizations judge suitable — roughly one in five — and leaders expect ~32.1% within twelve months, nearly one in three.** But the knowledge those agents need is not there: **only ~8.7% of organizations have context "broadly available or embedded," and only ~17% have broadly implemented knowledge graphs, the weakest context capability measured** (IDC/Quantexa; CIO Dive, as reported). The study's sharpest finding is about accountability: *without the right data — particularly lineage and decision data — a bank cannot explain how its agents reached a decision* (as reported). The house is deploying fast and remembering nothing.

**The collision — the rest of the week keeps handing agents more reach, not more memory.** In the same window, **IBM and Microsoft announced "Imagine Enterprise" (10 November), an agentic platform to build, run and govern industry-specific agents**; **Microsoft Foundry began provisioning agents with their own Microsoft Entra identities and Microsoft 365 accounts (19 November)**; and **LangChain opened the LangSmith Tool Gateway to public beta (19 November), centralizing credentials, access policies and audit traces for agent tool calls** (as reported). Identity, tool access and governance scaffolding are maturing quickly — the livery and the keyring are getting industrial-grade. **But none of that is context.** An agent can now have a name, a wallet, a key to every tool and a clean audit trail of *which* calls it made, and still not know *this* house — your history, your customers, your prior decisions — and still be unable to say *why* it chose as it did. The gap the IDC study measures is precisely the one all the new identity plumbing does not close.

**The stakes — give the servant the knowledge of the house, and make it keep its reasons.** The board move re-uses the whole season's discipline and points it at the one thing a name, a bond and a purse never supplied: **context, and the record of reasoning.** **First, close the context gap on the workflows you have already automated** — an agent running in production on ~8.7%-grade context is a confident guess, not a decision; ground it in your own data, lineage and a knowledge layer before you widen its remit. **Second, require every consequential agent decision to carry its reasons** — the inputs it saw, the context it drew on, the path it took — so that when the reckoning comes you can produce an account, not a shrug. **Third, stop widening the remit faster than you widen the memory** — the 21.6%→32.1% expansion is a plan to triple your unexplained-decision surface unless context scales with it. And do it **now**, because the first reckoning is not a drill you schedule; it is a question someone else asks, on a day you do not choose.

**Bottom line:** the livery named the servant, the steward kept the book, the surety put your name on the bond, the second wage checked whose interest it served, the two harvests sent it to reap, the purse funded it, and the foundation kept the ground beneath it yours. **The reckoning is about something none of those supplied: whether the servant *knows your house*, and whether it can *say why it did what it did.*** This month the numbers made the gap impossible to wave away — a governed, bonded, well-identified agent is already working one field in five, on the thinnest shred of context, and the plan is to push it to one in three while only about one house in eleven has given its agents the knowledge they need and almost none can reconstruct a decision's reasoning. The trap is quiet and flattering: identity, tooling and governance are the visible, purchasable parts, so houses buy them, watch the agent act crisply, and mistake *motion* for *understanding* — right up until the morning a board member, an auditor or a wronged customer asks, of one decision, "on what did it act, and why?" and the house discovers it cannot answer. The estates that keep their standing will do three unglamorous things before that morning: **give the servant the knowledge of the house** (your data, lineage and a knowledge layer, not a vendor's generic brain), **make it keep its reasons** (decision records that survive the reckoning), and **never widen the remit faster than the memory.** Deploy ambitiously — one field in five is real progress. ## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Reckoning"

*Topic: This morning the frame turns from who the servant is and whose ground it stands on to what it knows and whether it can account for itself. A 16 November IDC study commissioned by Quantexa ("The Context Gap: What Agentic AI Needs to Scale," 366 leaders at banks, insurers and government agencies) reports agents already in ~21.6% of suitable workflows, heading to ~32.1% within a year — while only ~8.7% of organizations have context "broadly embedded," ~17% have knowledge graphs, and without lineage and decision data a bank cannot explain how its agent decided (IDC/Quantexa; CIO Dive, as reported). In the same window IBM and Microsoft announced "Imagine Enterprise" (10 Nov), Microsoft Foundry began issuing agents their own Entra/M365 identities (19 Nov), and LangChain opened its LangSmith Tool Gateway to public beta (19 Nov) — more reach and more governance, but not more memory. The reckoning allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There was a great house that had, at long last, done everything right by its servants. It had named every hand and cut it a scoped key, kept one honest book, put the master's own name on the bond, asked whose second coin each advising servant took, sent the governed servants into the fields to reap, handed each a scoped purse with a drawstring the master could reach, and — most lately, and most proudly — refused to let the great trading company pour the foundation the house stood on. By any fair reckoning it was the best-run house in the valley, and the master had earned his sleep.

So the master did a bold and reasonable thing: he sent his excellent servants out to *act.* Not to fetch and carry, but to decide — to settle a tenant's account, to turn a wagon away at the gate, to choose which field to cut first, to answer a neighbour's claim. And it worked. In one field of every five the servants now acted in the master's place, crisply and without fuss, and the master was told — truthfully — that by next harvest it would be one field in three. The hall had never looked so capable.

But a careful friend, walking the estate that month, asked the master a plain question. *"That servant you sent to settle the miller's account — on what did it act? Does it know that the miller has paid late every autumn for twenty years and always made it good by Candlemas? Does it know which fields flood, which tenants are kin, why you sold last winter's grain early? Or did it act knowing nothing of this house at all — a clever stranger, deciding by the look of things?"* And the master, who had never asked, went and looked. And the answer froze him. His servants were superb — named, bonded, paid, shod — **and they did not know his house.** They carried no knowledge of the place in their heads; barely one estate in eleven had troubled to put it there, and almost none kept a map of how its own affairs connected. The servants were deciding the business of a house they had never truly been shown.

The friend pressed the colder question, the one that actually comes. *"Set aside whether it decided wisely. When the magistrate rides up — or the master's own council, or the wronged tenant — and asks of one single decision, 'on what did your servant act, and why did it choose so?' — what will you say?"* And the master opened his mouth and found he had nothing. His servants acted and left no account of their reasoning behind them. He could show *that* a decision was made, and by which hand, and even which tools the hand had touched — the livery and the keyring and the book gave him that much. But *why* — on what knowledge, down what path — was simply gone, evaporated the instant the thing was done. Four fields in five he could not reconstruct at all. **He had built a house that could act in his name and could not, for the life of it, say why.**

Two roads, as always. The easy road was to keep sending servants out to decide, faster and wider, because they were so visibly capable and the neighbours were doing the same — and to tell himself the reckoning would never actually come, or would accept a confident servant and a clean record of *which* tools it touched in place of an account of *why.* The wiser road, which the careful houses took, began with a humbling admission: **a servant that does not know the house is not deciding — it is guessing with conviction; and a decision whose reasons were never kept did not, for any purpose that matters, happen in a way anyone can defend.**

So the wise houses learned three plain things, and they cost far less to do before the reckoning than after. **The first: give the servant the knowledge of the house.** Not the trading company's great generic brain — *your* house: your ledgers and lineages, your customers and their histories, your prior decisions and the reasons they turned out right or wrong, laid out in a form the servant can actually draw on, with the connections mapped and not just the facts piled. A servant grounded in the house decides *about the house;* a servant grounded in nothing decides about the look of things. **The second: make it keep its reasons.** Require of every consequential decision a record of what the servant saw, what context it drew on, and the path it took — kept as faithfully as the steward keeps the coin — so that when the reckoning comes the house produces an account, not a shrug. **And the third: do not widen the remit faster than you widen the memory.** One field in five is honest progress; one field in three on the same thin context is simply three times the unexplained ground. Let the knowledge lead and the remit follow, never the other way.

Here is the part that should keep a master awake. All the loud, purchasable improvements of that season — the finer livery, the new master-keys that opened every tool at once, the splendid central gatehouse that logged each key turned — were real and good, and *none of them was knowledge.* **A servant can have a perfect name, a full set of keys, a wallet, and a spotless record of every door it opened, and still not know your house and still be unable to tell you why it decided anything.** The visible, buyable parts of the problem were being solved at a gallop, and the invisible, un-buyable part — the knowledge of the house, and the keeping of reasons — was being left exactly where it was, because no merchant sells it to you and it looks like nothing on the day it is missing. It looks like nothing right up until the reckoning, when it is the only thing anyone asks for.

**The moral, stated plainly:** the model is a rented stove; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the harbor pilot is the local knowledge that keeps the ship off the rocks; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising servant eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap and weigh what it brings in; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; the foundation is why you keep the ground beneath the house your own — and **the reckoning is why, having perfected the servant and secured its ground, you must now give it the knowledge of your house and require it to keep its reasons, because the morning will come — chosen by a board, a regulator or a wronged customer, never by you — when the whole worth of a governed estate turns on a single question none of the other disciplines can answer: 'on what did your servant act, and why did it choose so?'** A house that can act in the master's name but cannot say why it acted has not built a workforce; it has built a very fast way to make decisions it can never defend.

**The question it forces:** *We can send our agents out to decide — one field in five already, one in three by next harvest. But do they know *this* house — our data, our lineage, our customers, our past decisions — or are they clever strangers deciding by the look of things? And when the reckoning comes and someone asks of one decision, "on what did it act, and why did it choose so?" — can we produce an account, or only a shrug and the fact that something, by some hand, was done?*

## 3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

### 🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
- **Do our agents know *this* house, or are they clever strangers?** The new IDC/Quantexa study puts agents in ~21.6% of suitable workflows while only ~8.7% of organizations have context "broadly embedded" and ~17% have knowledge graphs. **For each workflow we have already automated, can we show what context the agent actually draws on — our data, our lineage, our history — or is it deciding our business on a generic model's guess at the look of things?**
- **When the reckoning comes, can we produce an account or only a shrug?** The study's sharpest finding is that without lineage and decision data, you cannot explain how an agent reached a decision. **Pick one consequential decision an agent made last week: can we reconstruct what it saw, what context it used, and why it chose as it did — or only *that* it did, and which tools it touched?**
- **Are we widening the remit faster than the memory?** The plan is 21.6%→32.1% in twelve months. **Is our context, grounding and decision-record capability scaling at the same rate as our agent deployment — or are we on track to triple the number of decisions we cannot explain?**

### 🏦 Financial Services
- The IDC study surveyed banks and insurers precisely because the explainability gap is a supervised risk in your world: regulators will ask *how* a model-driven decision was reached, and "the agent decided" is not an answer. **Can we, today, produce a defensible decision record — inputs, context, lineage, path — for an agent-made credit, claims or AML decision, to the standard our examiners already expect of a human-made one?**
- Only ~17% of organizations have broadly implemented knowledge graphs, the weakest context capability. **Is our customer, counterparty and transaction context connected in a form an agent can reason over — or siloed such that the agent sees fragments and decides anyway, with no map of how they relate?**

### 🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
- A clinical or safety decision an agent cannot explain is a decision you cannot stand behind in front of a regulator, a patient or a court. **Do our agent-touched clinical and pharmacovigilance workflows capture the reasoning and the context behind each recommendation, or only the output — and would that record survive an adverse-event reckoning?**
- Patient context is scattered across systems and histories. **Is the relevant patient and clinical context actually available to the agent at the point of decision (the ~8.7% problem), or is it deciding on a thin, generic slice while the knowledge that would change the answer sits unreachable in another system?**

### 🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
- You would never let a new engineer commission a line without the plant's own history — its failure modes, its tolerances, why the last retrofit was done the way it was. **Have we given our operational agents the equivalent knowledge of *this* plant, or are they optimizing by generic first principles against equipment whose quirks they have never been shown?**
- Agentic workloads are also reshaping the physical stack — AMD reports agent-heavy work pushing processor-to-GPU ratios from ~1:4 toward ~1:1, and one analysis this week estimates agent tasks generate ~450% more network traffic than the same human work (as reported). **Is our infrastructure plan sized for agents that are compute- and context-hungry, or for last year's chat assistants?**

### 🛒 Retail / Consumer
- An agent repricing, recommending or resolving a complaint without your merchandising history, your margin rules and this customer's past will act confidently and sometimes expensively wrongly. **Is our agent grounded in our own catalogue, pricing logic and customer history — or deciding on a generic model's priors about shoppers in general?**
- The first customer-facing reckoning is a viral screenshot of an unexplainable decision. **If a customer disputes an agent's refusal, refund or price, can our team reconstruct and defend exactly why the agent decided it — within the hour, not the week?**

### 🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
- Public decisions must be explainable by law and by legitimacy, not just by preference. The IDC study's government respondents face this first. **Can we guarantee that no agent makes a decision about a citizen that we cannot fully account for — the context it used, the data lineage, the reasoning path — before we expand agent use, not after?**
- The EU's rules are moving but still bite: GPAI and transparency obligations have been enforced since 2 August 2026, while the Digital Omnibus deal would defer stand-alone high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 and embedded ones to 2 August 2028, pending formal adoption (as reported). **Are we using the deferral as breathing room to build genuine explainability and context into agent decisions — or as an excuse to deploy now and worry about accounting for it later?**

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Foundation to the Reckoning

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking *is this agent named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and standing on ground I own?* and started asking the one question none of those controls touch: *does it know my house, and can it tell me why it did what it did?* The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the keyring is which key it holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the harbor pilot is the local knowledge that keeps the ship off the rocks; the errand bounds what it can reach; the bell discloses when it slips; the looking-glass is the honest outside light; the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests and purse govern who it is, what it serves, what it reaps and what it spends; the foundation keeps the ground its own. **Every one of those is about identity, permission, liability, loyalty, output, money or ground. The reckoning is about *knowledge and reasoning* — and this month the analysts put a number on exactly how far that has been left behind: agents in ~21.6% of suitable workflows, heading to ~32.1%, on context that only ~8.7% of organizations have actually embedded, with knowledge graphs in only ~17% (IDC/Quantexa, as reported).** The point is blunt: **the controls that are easy to buy (identity, tooling, governance) are racing ahead, and the capability that is hard to build and impossible to purchase (the house's own knowledge, and a durable record of why each decision was made) is being left exactly where it was — which is where the first reckoning will find it.**

- **The reality — deployment is lapping context, and the gap is now measured.** **The 16 November IDC study commissioned by Quantexa ("The Context Gap: What Agentic AI Needs to Scale," 366 leaders at large banks, insurers and government agencies) reports agents in ~21.6% of suitable workflows, expected ~32.1% within twelve months, while only ~8.7% of organizations have context "broadly available or embedded" and only ~17% have broadly implemented knowledge graphs — and concludes that without lineage and decision data, organizations cannot explain how their agents reached a decision (IDC/Quantexa; CIO Dive, as reported).** Architecturally, three things follow. First, **context is not a prompt; it is an architecture** — retrieval over your own governed data, a semantic/knowledge layer that encodes how your entities relate, and freshness guarantees, so the agent reasons about *your* house and not the model's priors. Second, **an unexplained decision is an unfinished decision** — if the inputs, retrieved context and reasoning path are not captured at decision time, they cannot be reconstructed later at any price, so explainability is a write-time discipline, not a read-time report. Third, **the knowledge-graph gap (~17%) is the tell** — most houses have facts but not the connections between them, and an agent that cannot traverse relationships decides on isolated fragments, confidently and shallowly.
- **The collision — the week's launches add reach and governance, not memory.** **IBM and Microsoft announced "Imagine Enterprise" (10 November), an agentic platform to build, run and govern industry-specific agents; Microsoft Foundry began provisioning agents with their own Microsoft Entra identities and Microsoft 365 accounts (19 November); and LangChain opened the LangSmith Tool Gateway to public beta (19 November), centralizing credentials, access policies and audit traces for agent tool calls (as reported).** Two engineering realities cut against comfort. **First, identity and audit are not context.** A first-class agent identity (the livery), a governed tool gateway (the keyring and gatehouse) and a clean trace of *which* calls were made are necessary and welcome — but they record *that* the agent acted and with what credential, never *on what knowledge* or *why.* The IDC gap sits one layer below all of it. **Second, more reach without more memory widens the blast radius.** Every new identity, tool and integration lets the agent act on more of the business — on the same thin context — so the industrial-grade plumbing shipping this month is, absent a context and decision-record discipline, a faster way to generate decisions you cannot explain.
- **The answer — ground it, make it keep its reasons, and let knowledge lead the remit.** **Close the context gap on the workflows already in production (retrieval over your own governed data plus a knowledge/semantic layer that maps relationships, not just facts); require every consequential agent decision to persist its inputs, retrieved context and reasoning path as a first-class record; and gate each expansion of agent remit on context and explainability coverage, so you never automate a decision you could not later account for — reusing the logging, ownership and record disciplines you already built for the steward, the inspector and the provenance, now pointed at the one thing they never captured: why.** This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — *be able to account for, and act on, what you run* — now aimed at its sharpest edge: not what the agent is, touches, reaps, spends or stands on, but whether it knows your house and can defend its own choices when someone finally asks.

The strategic core: **the parts of agentic AI that are easy to buy — identity, tools, governance scaffolding — are maturing fast and visibly, which is exactly why they lull you; the part that is hard to build and impossible to purchase — your house's own knowledge, embedded where the agent can reason over it, and a faithful record of why each decision was made — is the part the first reckoning will demand, and the part almost no one has done (~8.7% context, ~17% knowledge graphs).** The market makes the temptation concrete: it feels like progress to give every agent a name, a wallet and a key to every tool, and it feels like a cost centre to build the knowledge layer and the decision record — so most houses buy the visible parts and skip the invisible one. The board translation: **deploy ambitiously, but never send a servant where it cannot know the ground or account for its choices.** The failure mode is exact: **a house that names, governs, funds, perfects and grounds its servants — and then sends them to decide its business on ~8.7%-grade context while keeping no record of their reasoning — will pass every governance review and discover, the first time anyone asks "why did it decide this?", that it has built a very fast engine for making indefensible decisions.**

### Why "the reckoning" is the discipline that matters now

For a long season the worry was whether the servant was named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and standing on its own ground. **This month the analysts moved the question underneath all of that: the agents are already deciding your business, and they do it on almost no knowledge of your house and keep no record of why.** The ~21.6%→32.1% deployment curve and the ~8.7% context figure are not in tension by accident — they are the exact shape of a house automating decisions faster than it is giving those decisions either grounding or memory. Governance that stopped at identity, permission and liability will not survive the first time someone asks *why.* The estates that keep their standing will give the servant the knowledge of the house and make it keep its reasons — and will refuse, deliberately, to widen the remit one field further than the memory can follow.

### How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — **be able to account for, and act on, what you run** — now sharpened to *be able to say why your agent decided what it decided.* On a legacy estate the instinct, after a hard season of governance work, is to celebrate the shiny, visible wins — the agent has a name now, a wallet, a key to every system, a clean audit trail — and to let it loose on more decisions because it looks so capable. Under the reckoning that is exactly the reflex to check: capability you can see is not knowledge you can defend. The retrofit is **ground, record, pace. Ground** the agent: retrieval over your own governed data plus a knowledge/semantic layer that encodes how your entities relate, so it reasons about your house, not the model's priors. **Record** the reasons: persist the inputs, retrieved context and reasoning path of every consequential decision as faithfully as the steward keeps the coin. **Pace** the remit: gate every expansion on context and explainability coverage, so the ~8.7% figure becomes your reality to fix, not your exposure to grow. ## 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. **Ground it — close the context gap on the workflows you have already automated.** Agents are in ~21.6% of suitable workflows while only ~8.7% of organizations have context "broadly embedded" (IDC/Quantexa) — which means most production agents are deciding your business on a generic model's priors, not your house's knowledge. **This month, pick your two highest-stakes automated workflows and ground their agents in your own governed data — retrieval plus a knowledge/semantic layer that encodes how your entities relate, not just a pile of facts — and measure the before/after on decision quality.** Report one number to the board: the share of production agent decisions that draw on your own embedded context versus a generic model's guess.
2. **Record the reasons — make every consequential agent decision keep its account.** The study's sharpest finding is that without lineage and decision data you cannot explain how an agent decided; an unexplained decision is an unfinished one, because the reasoning cannot be reconstructed after the fact at any price. **This month, require that every consequential agent decision persists, at decision time, the inputs it saw, the context it retrieved and the path it took — a first-class record kept as faithfully as the steward keeps the coin — and dry-run a reckoning: pick one past decision and try to produce its full account.** Report the share of consequential agent decisions for which you can reconstruct the full "why," not just the "that."
