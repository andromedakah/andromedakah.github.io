The Reckoning

Wednesday, 25 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

Your governed, bonded agents already decide your business in one field of five — soon one in three — on almost no knowledge of your house, and keep no record of why (IDC/Quantexa, 16 Nov: agents in ~21.6% of suitable workflows, only ~8.7% have context embedded, ~17% have knowledge graphs). The week shipped more reach, not more memory. Give the servant the knowledge of the house; make it keep its reasons; don't outrun the memory.

1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from who the agent is and whose ground it stands on to what it knows and whether it can account for what it did — because agents are already deciding your business, on almost no context, and keep no record of why.

1. The reality — deployment is lapping context. A 16 November IDC study commissioned by Quantexa ("The Context Gap," 366 leaders at large banks, insurers and government agencies) reports AI agents in ~21.6% of suitable workflows, heading to ~32.1% within 12 months, while only ~8.7% of organizations have context "broadly embedded" and only ~17% have knowledge graphs — and concludes that without lineage and decision data, banks can't explain how their agents decided (IDC/Quantexa; CIO Dive, as reported).

2. The collision — the week handed agents more reach, not more memory. IBM and Microsoft announced "Imagine Enterprise" (10 Nov); Microsoft Foundry began provisioning agents with their own Entra/Microsoft 365 identities (19 Nov); and LangChain opened the LangSmith Tool Gateway to public beta (19 Nov) (as reported). A name, a wallet, keys to every tool and a clean audit trail — none of it is the knowledge of the house.

3. The answer — give it the knowledge, make it keep its reasons, don't outrun the memory. Ground your already-automated workflows in your own data and a knowledge layer; require consequential decisions to persist their inputs, context and reasoning path; and widen the remit no faster than you widen the context — so the first reckoning meets an account, not a shrug.

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each governed who the servant is, what it touches, reaps, spends or stands on — the reckoning is about whether it knows your house and can say why it decided. Identity, tooling and governance (easy to buy) are racing ahead; the house's own knowledge and a record of reasons (hard to build) are left behind — exactly where the first reckoning will find them. Deploy ambitiously, but never send a servant where it cannot know the ground or account for its choices.

2 · Allegory — The Reckoning

A great house had at last done everything right by its servants — named, booked, bonded, waged, sent to reap, funded with a scoped purse, and the ground beneath the house kept its own. So the master did a bold, reasonable thing: he sent his excellent servants out to decide — settle a tenant's account, turn a wagon away, choose which field to cut. In one field of five they now acted in his place, crisply; by next harvest it would be one in three. The hall had never looked so capable.

But a careful friend asked a plain question: "That servant you sent to settle the miller's account — does it know the miller has paid late every autumn for twenty years and always made it good by Candlemas? Or did it decide knowing nothing of this house — a clever stranger, deciding by the look of things?" The master went and looked, and froze. His servants were superb — and they did not know his house; barely one estate in eleven had put that knowledge in them. Then the colder question, the one that actually comes: "When the magistrate asks, of one decision, 'on what did it act, and why did it choose so?' — what will you say?" And he had nothing. His servants acted and left no account of their reasoning. He could show that a decision was made, and which tools the hand touched — but why was gone, evaporated the instant it was done.

So the wise houses learned three things: give the servant the knowledge of the house (your ledgers, lineages, customers and past decisions, with the connections mapped, not a generic brain); make it keep its reasons (what it saw, the context it drew on, the path it took, kept as faithfully as the steward keeps the coin); and do not widen the remit faster than the memory. All the loud, buyable improvements of the season — the finer livery, the master-keys, the central gatehouse — were real and good, and none of them was knowledge.

The question it forces: We can send our agents out to decide — one field in five already, one in three by next harvest. But do they know this house — our data, lineage, customers, past decisions — or are they clever strangers deciding by the look of things? And when someone asks of one decision, "on what did it act, and why?" — can we produce an account, or only a shrug?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Reckoning

Every prior control is about identity, permission, liability, loyalty, output, money or ground; the reckoning is about knowledge and reasoning — and the easy-to-buy parts are racing ahead while the hard-to-build part is left exactly where the first reckoning will find it. The reality: context is not a prompt, it is an architecture (retrieval over your own governed data plus a knowledge/semantic layer that maps relationships); an unexplained decision is an unfinished one, because reasoning not captured at decision time cannot be reconstructed later; the ~17% knowledge-graph figure is the tell — most houses have facts but not the connections. The collision: identity and audit are not context — a first-class agent identity and a clean tool-call trace record that it acted and with what credential, never on what knowledge or why; more reach without more memory just widens the blast radius. The answer: ground it (your data + a knowledge layer), record the reasons (inputs, context, path persisted as a first-class record), and pace the remit (gate each expansion on context and explainability coverage).

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Ground it. Agents are in ~21.6% of suitable workflows while only ~8.7% of organizations have context "broadly embedded" — most production agents decide on a generic model's priors, not your house's knowledge. Pick your two highest-stakes automated workflows and ground their agents in your own governed data (retrieval plus a knowledge/semantic layer that maps relationships). Report the share of production agent decisions that draw on your own embedded context versus a generic guess.

2. Record the reasons. Without lineage and decision data you cannot explain how an agent decided; an unexplained decision is unfinished. Require every consequential agent decision to persist, at decision time, the inputs it saw, the context it retrieved and the path it took — and dry-run a reckoning on one past decision. Report the share of consequential decisions for which you can reconstruct the full "why," not just the "that."

3. Pace the remit. The plan is 21.6%→32.1% in 12 months; on flat context that is three times the unexplained-decision surface. Put a gate on agent expansion: no workflow moves to agent-made decisions until its context grounding and decision-record coverage meet a bar you set. Report the policy and the first expansion it held back.
