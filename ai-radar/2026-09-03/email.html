The Two Mills

Thursday, 3 September 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

All week we built, counted, inspected and secured the agentic workforce; this morning the board asks the only question the treasury cares about — after all this motion, are we grinding any flour? McKinsey's State of AI 2026, re-circulated this week as a "two-speed race," says most firms are not: 40% of $1B+ organizations are scaling agents (from 27%), yet only ~6% are "AI high performers" and ~37% see any EBIT impact — flat on the year. Motion is not milling; a pilot is not a P&L.

1 · Executive Summary

For a week this radar assembled the agentic workforce, counted it (the census), faced the authority that asks for the count (the inspector) and priced the ungoverned agent (the break-in). This morning the frame turns from risk to return: now that everyone can deploy agents, who is actually making money from them — and why is almost no one?

1. The race is two-speed, and the gap is yield, not adoption. McKinsey's State of AI 2026 (re-circulated 2 Sep as "a two-speed race") finds 40% of $1B+ organizations now scaling AI agents (up from 27%) and 32% skipping a software purchase to build with agentic coding tools — yet only ~6% are "AI high performers" (≥5% of EBIT from AI, significant impact), unchanged from last year, and only ~37% attribute any EBIT impact at all, "about the same" as 2025. Everyone is running the wheel; few are grinding flour (as reported).

2. The flour comes from rewiring the mill, not adding paddles. McKinsey ties the 6% to one practice above all: high performers are nearly 3× as likely to fundamentally redesign their workflows end to end — fewer steps, different handoffs, humans on the exceptions — rather than laying agents over processes built for people. Value follows process redesign, not agent count.

3. The busy-but-empty mill gets shut down. Gartner expects >40% of agentic AI projects to be canceled by end 2027 on escalating cost, unclear value and weak controls, with much of the market still "agent washing" — only ~130 of thousands of agentic vendors are "real." A pilot that never touches the P&L is a cost line waiting to be cut.

Bottom line: the two-speed race is the market discovering that deploying agents is not the same as capturing value — the wheel can turn all day and grind nothing. The mill that prospered rebuilt its whole works so the river's power reached the stone; the one that only added paddles is loud, busy, and empty at harvest. Redesign the process before you add the agent, tie every deployment to a P&L line, and kill the pilots that never bite the grain.

2 · Allegory — The Two Mills

There were two mills on the same river, drawing the same current, grinding the same grain. In a year when everyone suddenly wanted flour, both millers built: more paddles on the wheel, more hands, the water rushing harder than ever — and from the road both looked magnificent, wheels roaring, spray flying. The first miller was certain the flour would follow the motion. But at harvest, his sacks were nearly as empty as the year before, while his neighbor's storehouse was full — on the same river, with a wheel that turned, if anything, faster. He walked downstream expecting a bigger wheel and found an ordinary one. What differed was everything between the wheel and the stone.

A mill does not grind by turning; it grinds when the wheel's motion is carried, through gearing built for the purpose, into a stone dressed to bite the grain. The first miller had spent everything on the wheel and nothing on the works — his grandfather's gearing, a stone smooth from a decade's use — so his river's power spun the axle magnificently and dissipated into noise. The second had torn out the old gearing and rebuilt the entire works around the new wheel, so every ordinary revolution ended in flour. The panickers built bigger wheels, roared louder into the same unchanged works, and left their great noisy wheels to rot, because a wheel that grinds nothing is only a cost. You do not get more flour by spinning the wheel faster over the old works. You get it by rebuilding the works so the wheel you already have actually reaches the stone.

The question it forces: our wheel is turning faster than ever — more agents, more pilots, more spend. So at harvest: how much more flour do we actually have? Can we name one process we redesigned end to end around agents — fewer steps, different handoffs, humans on the exceptions — and point to the EBIT line it moved? Or have we spent everything on a magnificent wheel bolted to the same old works? Are we the 6% that rebuilt the works, or the 94% still waiting for motion to become milling?

3 · Technical — Rebuilding the Works, Not the Wheel

The race is two-speed, and the gap is process redesign: McKinsey State of AI 2026 (1,719 respondents, 97 countries; published 25 Aug; re-circulated 2 Sep) — 40% of $1B+ firms scaling agents (from 27%); 32% building over buying with agentic coding tools; only ~6% high performers (≥5% EBIT, unchanged); ~37% any EBIT ("about the same" as 2025); high performers ~3× more likely to redesign workflows end to end. Value is not correlated with agent count or spend; it is correlated with whether the surrounding process was rebuilt. An agent over a workflow built for human handoffs inherits every inefficiency of that workflow. The cancellations are the empty mills being shut: Gartner — >40% of agentic AI projects canceled by end 2027 (cost, unclear value, weak controls); only ~130 of thousands of vendors "real" ("agent washing"). A pilot with no baseline, no redesigned process and no P&L owner is motion instrumented as motion. Rewiring is the works; the model is only the wheel: the tooling to build the works is abundant (MCP ~97M monthly SDK downloads, ~41% of software orgs run MCP servers in production; Genesys's Agentic Virtual Agent on Scaled Cognition's APT-2 large-action model, 25,000+ MCP-compatible tools) — the scarce input is the will to rebuild the process, not overlay it.

On legacy: the temptation is to bolt agents onto existing processes because it demos fast — this week made that the path to the 40% that get cancelled. Redesign: rebuild one high-volume process end to end around agents. Tie: attach every agent to a named P&L line and human owner. Measure: outcomes (EBIT, cycle-time, yield), never activity (tokens, tasks, pilots). Prune: kill the pilots with no baseline and no result, before Gartner's 40% does. The census is the roll; the inspector asks you to produce it; the break-in is what the missing entries cost — and the two mills are whether any of it grinds flour.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Rebuild one process end to end. Value tracks workflow redesign, not agent count: high performers are ~3× more likely to rebuild the works. Pick your highest-volume process and redesign it around agents — fewer steps, different handoffs, humans on the exceptions — rather than laying an agent over the process you already have. Report the EBIT, cycle-time or yield line that one redesigned process moved, versus the count of agents deployed.

2. Tie every agent to a P&L line. A pilot instrumented as activity (tokens, tasks, seats) is a candidate for Gartner's 40% cancellations. Require that every agent in production names a human owner and a measurable business result before it scales, and re-report your AI portfolio in outcomes — revenue added, cost avoided, cycle-time cut — not adoption. Report the share of production agents with a named P&L owner and a baseline metric.

3. Prune the loud, empty mills before the market does. With >40% of agentic projects headed for cancellation on unclear value, an unmeasured pilot is a cost line waiting to be cut — cut it deliberately and redirect the spend to rebuilding a process. Run one review this quarter: for every agent program, show the redesigned process and the result, or shut it down and free the budget for one that grinds flour.
