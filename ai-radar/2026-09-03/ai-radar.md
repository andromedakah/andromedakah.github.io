## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Two Mills"

*Topic: This week McKinsey's State of AI 2026 (published 25 August; re-circulated 2 September in board-level commentary from AIwire / BigDATAwire under the frame "enterprise AI is becoming a two-speed race") put a hard number on the gap between AI motion and AI value. It reports 40% of $1B+ organizations now scaling AI agents (up from 27%) and 32% of firms skipping a software purchase to build it themselves with agentic coding tools — yet only ~6% qualify as "AI high performers" (attributing at least 5% of EBIT to AI with significant impact), unchanged from a year ago, and only ~37% attribute any EBIT impact at all, "about the same" as last year. The differentiator: high performers are nearly three times as likely to fundamentally redesign their workflows end to end. Gartner's standing forecast that more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027 (on escalating cost, unclear business value and inadequate risk controls), and its estimate that only ~130 of thousands of agentic vendors are "real," sharpen the point. This follows this week's editions on the break-in (the cost of the ungoverned agent), the inspector (the authority that asks you to produce the roll) and the census before it. The lesson: deploying agents is not the same as capturing value — the wheel can turn all day and grind nothing. The two-mills allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There were two mills on the same river, drawing from the same current, grinding the same grain. In a year when everyone suddenly wanted flour, both millers did the obvious thing: they built. They fixed more paddles to their wheels, they hired more hands, they set the water rushing harder than it ever had, and from the road both mills looked magnificent — wheels roaring, spray flying, a great noise of industry that made the whole valley feel prosperous. The first miller, watching his wheel turn faster than his father's ever had, was certain the flour would follow the motion. It had to. Look how it turned.

But at harvest, when the sacks were counted, the two mills told very different stories. The first miller's sacks were nearly as empty as the year before — a little more, perhaps, but nothing like the roar of the wheel had promised. The second miller's storehouse was full. And the strange thing, the thing that kept the first miller up at night, was that they had the same river, the same grain, and if anything his wheel turned *faster*. So he walked downstream to see what his neighbor had done differently, expecting to find a bigger wheel, a stronger current, some secret paddle. He found none of it. The second miller's wheel was ordinary. What was different was everything *between* the wheel and the stone.

For the second miller had understood the one thing the first never did: **a mill does not grind flour by turning. It grinds flour when the turning of the wheel is carried, through gearing built for the purpose, into a millstone dressed to bite the grain.** The first miller had spent all his money on the wheel and none on the works. His river's power spun the axle magnificently and then dissipated into noise, because the gearing was the same gearing his grandfather had cut for a slower wheel, and the millstone was smooth from a decade of use and bit nothing. He had more motion than any mill on the river and less milling, because motion that never reaches the stone is just expensive spray. The second miller had done the unglamorous, invisible thing: he had **torn out the old gearing and rebuilt the entire works around the new wheel** — re-cut the cogs so the power actually transferred, re-dressed the stone so it bit, changed where the grain entered and where the flour came out — so that every revolution of his ordinary wheel ended in flour, not in noise.

The millers who panicked at the empty sacks did the loud, useless thing: they built *bigger wheels*. If motion had not produced flour, surely more motion would — so they doubled the paddles, dammed the river higher, and roared louder than ever into the same unchanged works, and the sacks stayed empty, and the money ran out, and one by one the great noisy wheels were left to rot because a wheel that grinds nothing is only a cost. **The millers who prospered had learned the quiet truth their neighbors would not hear: you do not get more flour by spinning the wheel faster over the old works. You get it by rebuilding the works so the wheel you already have actually reaches the stone.** The river was the same for everyone. What separated a full storehouse from an empty one was never the size of the wheel — it was whether anyone had done the dull, expensive labor of re-cutting the gearing and dressing the stone.

The moral is not that the wheel does not matter — you cannot grind without one. It is that **the wheel is the cheap part and the loud part, and the works are the hard part and the quiet part, and only the works turn motion into flour.** Everyone bought the wheel; almost no one rebuilt the works; and at harvest the valley divided cleanly into the few storehouses that were full and the many that were merely loud.

**The moral, stated plainly:** the model is a rented stove; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the lingua franca is the tongue it speaks; the conductor keeps the ensemble in time; the ear hears whether it plays well; the repertoire is the music only your house can play; the census is the roll that counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the break-in is what the missing entries cost — and **the two mills are whether all that motion actually grinds flour: the wheel can turn all day and grind nothing, because milling happens in the works, not the wheel.** Rebuild the process before you add the agent, dress the stone so it bites the grain, and count your harvest in flour — because a spinning wheel is not a full storehouse, and *a pilot is not a P&L.*

**The question it forces:** *Our wheel is turning faster than it ever has — more agents deployed, more pilots running, more spend than last year. So the only question that matters at harvest: how much more flour do we actually have? Can we name one process we redesigned end to end around agents — fewer steps, different handoffs, humans on the exceptions — and point to the EBIT line it moved? Or have we, like most of the valley, spent all our money on a magnificent wheel bolted to the same old works, mistaking the roar for the flour? Are we in the 6% that rebuilt the works, or the 94% still waiting for motion to become milling?*

## 3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

### 🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
- **Where is our flour?** McKinsey finds ~6% of firms are AI high performers and only ~37% see any EBIT impact, "about the same" as last year — while 40% of large firms are scaling agents. **Can we point to a single EBIT line that AI moved this year, or are we running a faster wheel over the same works and calling the noise progress?**
- **Did we rebuild the works, or just add paddles?** High performers are ~3× more likely to fundamentally redesign workflows end to end. **For our biggest AI bet, did we redesign the process — fewer steps, different handoffs, humans on the exceptions — or did we lay an agent over a workflow built for people and hope?**
- **Which pilots are cost lines waiting to be cut?** Gartner expects >40% of agentic projects canceled by end 2027 on unclear value. **Have we tied every agent in production to a measurable business result with an owner — or are some of ours "agent washing" we will quietly shut down next year?**

### 🏦 Financial Services
- Automation ROI here is real but concentrated in redesigned processes (underwriting, reconciliation, servicing). **Have we re-cut the gearing — rebuilt the workflow around the agent — for one high-volume process, or bolted a copilot onto a process still shaped for human handoffs?**
- Cost discipline is a board reflex. **Can we show, per agent program, the flour — cycle-time cut, loss avoided, revenue added — against the spend, or only the number of pilots underway?**

### 🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
- Clinical and research workflows are dense with handoffs — exactly where rewiring, not overlay, produces value. **Have we redesigned one clinical or R&D process end to end around agents, or are we measuring "AI adoption" by seats and pilots rather than by outcomes and throughput?**
- The safety bar makes half-built projects expensive. **Do we kill an agent pilot that cannot show a validated result, or let it run because it looks modern while grinding no flour?**

### 🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
- The mill metaphor is literal here: motion at the wheel is not output at the stone. **Do we measure agent programs by throughput and yield at the process, or by activity — tokens, tasks, pilots — that never reaches the P&L?**
- Standardization across sites is where redesign compounds. **Have we rebuilt one workflow around agents and rolled the *redesigned process* (not just the tool) across plants, or does each site bolt agents onto its own old works?**

### 🛒 Retail / Consumer
- Customer-facing agents multiply fastest and show ROI last without redesign. **Have we rebuilt the service or merchandising process around the agent — or added a chatbot to a journey still designed for a call center?**
- Build-vs-buy is now live: 32% of firms skip software to build with agentic coding tools. **Where we build, are we redesigning the workflow it serves — or just re-creating the old tool with a faster wheel?**

### 🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
- Value must be demonstrable and auditable, not asserted. **For each citizen-facing agent, can we show the redesigned process and the outcome it improved — or only that we "deployed AI"?**
- Cancelled projects are public failures. **Do we gate agent programs on a redesigned process and a measurable result before scaling — or discover at audit that the wheel turned and the flour never came?**

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — Rebuilding the Works, Not the Wheel

Read the whole stack one more time, but from the seat of the treasurer counting sacks at harvest. The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the lingua franca lets it speak; the conductor coordinates it; the ear hears its errors; the repertoire is the data it performs; the census counts it; the inspector asks you to produce the count; the break-in is what the missing entries cost. **The two mills are what all of it is *for*: turning the motion of a deployed agent into flour on the P&L — and this week the data showed that for all but ~6% of firms, the motion is not yet reaching the stone.** The engineering point is blunt: **an agent laid over a process designed for humans produces expensive motion, not milling; the value comes from re-cutting the gearing — redesigning the workflow end to end so the agent's output actually lands as a business result — which is the hard, quiet, un-demoable part almost everyone skips.**

- **The race is two-speed, and the gap is process redesign.** **McKinsey *State of AI 2026* (1,719 respondents, 97 countries; fielded May–June; published 25 Aug; re-circulated 2 Sep as "a two-speed race"): 40% of $1B+ firms scaling agents (from 27%); 32% skipping software purchases to build with agentic coding tools; only ~6% AI high performers (≥5% EBIT from AI, significant impact — unchanged YoY); ~37% any EBIT impact ("about the same" as 2025); high performers ~3× more likely to fundamentally redesign workflows end to end (as reported).** Read architecturally: value is not correlated with agent count or spend; it is correlated with whether the surrounding process was rebuilt. An agent that automates a step in a workflow designed for human handoffs inherits every inefficiency of that workflow — you have made the wheel spin faster over unchanged gearing.
- **The cancellations are the empty mills being shut.** **Gartner: >40% of agentic AI projects canceled by end 2027 (escalating cost, unclear business value, inadequate risk controls); only ~130 of thousands of agentic vendors "real" ("agent washing" — rebranding RPA and chatbots without substantial agentic capability) (as reported).** The design failure is measurable up front: a pilot with no baseline metric, no redesigned process and no P&L owner is motion instrumented as motion — tokens consumed, tasks run — never as flour. If your agent program reports activity rather than outcome, it is already a candidate for the 40%.
- **Rewiring is the works; the model is only the wheel.** The refrain holds: the model is a rented, now-metered utility; portability (MCP, A2A under neutral governance) keeps it swappable; the moat is your proprietary data and — this week's addition — your *redesigned process*. **Standing context: MCP now ~97M monthly SDK downloads with ~41% of software orgs running MCP servers in production (Stacklok), and platforms are shipping the gearing (Genesys's Xperience 2026 debut of an Agentic Virtual Agent on the Scaled Cognition APT-2 large-action model, 500+ integrations and 25,000+ MCP-compatible tools) (as reported).** The tooling to build the works is now abundant; the scarce input is the organizational will to rebuild the process rather than overlay it.

### Why "the two mills" is the layer that matters now

For two years the reflex was to treat AI value as a function of deployment — more agents, more seats, more pilots, more spend. This week the data reframed it as a function of *process redesign*: **the flour is not in the wheel; it is in the works between the wheel and the stone.** What separates the 6% from everyone else is not a better model or a bigger budget but the unglamorous labor of rebuilding the workflow so the agent's motion actually lands as a result. The most valuable move this quarter is not adding the forty-first agent; it is rebuilding one process end to end around the ten you already have — and being able to point to the EBIT line it moved.

### How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — **be able to account for, and act on, what you run** — now aimed at value, not just risk. On a legacy estate the temptation is to bolt agents onto existing processes because it demos fast and ships faster; this week's data made that the path to the 40% that get cancelled. The retrofit is **redesign, tie, measure, prune. Redesign:** pick one high-volume process and rebuild it end to end around agents — fewer steps, different handoffs, humans reviewing exceptions — instead of overlaying the old one. **Tie:** attach every agent in production to a named P&L line and a human owner, so its output is measured as flour, not motion. **Measure:** report outcomes (EBIT, cycle-time, yield, loss avoided), never activity (tokens, tasks, pilots). **Prune:** kill the pilots that show no baseline and no result, before Gartner's 40% does it for you. ## 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. **Rebuild one process end to end — stop adding paddles to the wheel.** Value tracks workflow redesign, not agent count: high performers are ~3× more likely to rebuild the works. **This month, pick your highest-volume process and redesign it around agents — fewer steps, different handoffs, humans reviewing exceptions instead of pushing paper — rather than laying an agent over the process you already have.** Report one number to the board: the EBIT, cycle-time or yield line that one redesigned process moved, versus the count of agents deployed.

3. **Prune the loud, empty mills before the market does.** With >40% of agentic projects headed for cancellation on unclear value, an unmeasured pilot is a cost line waiting to be cut — so cut it deliberately, and redirect the spend to rebuilding a process. **Run one review this quarter: for every agent program, show the redesigned process and the result, or shut it down and free the budget for one that grinds flour.** Report two numbers next quarter: the count of agent programs killed for no measurable result, and the count rebuilt around a redesigned process. **The model is rented, the knowledge is the moat, the census is the roll, the inspector asks for it, the break-in is what the missing entries cost — and the two mills are the reminder that none of it matters until the wheel's motion becomes flour on the P&L.**
