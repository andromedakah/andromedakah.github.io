**Sunday, 22 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering**

## 1 · Executive Summary (90-second read)

For a long year the radar built the *governed house* — name the agent (the livery), keep one book (the steward), sign the bond (the surety), check its wage (the second wage), send it to reap (the two harvests), hand it a scoped purse (the purse), and keep the foundation yours (the foundation). Every one of those disciplines **governs** the servant. **This morning the question turns from governing the servant to metering what it drinks.** The data landed all at once, and it is not ambiguous.

**The bill — agents are working, and they are blowing through the budget.** On **17 November 2026 Menlo Ventures released its 2026 enterprise-AI spending survey** (~400 US enterprise decision-makers, **>$4B of AI spend represented**): enterprises spent an average of **~4.7× their 2025 AI budget in 2026, against a plan of 1.9×** — the overshoot concentrated among **heavy agent users**, with the mean (4.7×) running about **2.1× the median (2.2×)**, the signature of a few runaway deployments dragging the average (Menlo Ventures, as reported). This is not one firm's bad quarter. **Futurum's 2H-2026 survey** of 1,636 enterprises reports **46.9% over their planned AI budget versus just 5.6% under**; a **McKinsey FinOps** snapshot (small sample) puts the over-budget share near nine in ten; and the anecdotes have teeth — Uber reportedly exhausted its entire 2026 AI budget **in four months**, and one enterprise that gave employees uncapped access to a frontier model reportedly ran up **~$500M in a single month** (Futurum; McKinsey; Inc.; Axios, as reported).

**The cause — most of the water is spent re-drawing the bucket.** The reason agentic spend outruns every plan is structural, not careless. A single user request **fans out** — the agent plans, calls tools, reflects, retries, and refines its own draft until it is satisfied — so one "task" becomes dozens of model calls. The specific culprit now has a number: **~60% of agentic-AI cost goes to "response refinement"** (MarketScale, as reported) — the agent drawing the same bucket again and again to polish an answer. Budgets built for per-seat SaaS or per-call inference **do not model a worker that can choose to do its job five times.** Nearly half of organisations that hit an AI-spend surprise have **escalated it to the board** (Mavvrik 2026, as reported); the surprise is now a governance event, not a line-item.

**The move — meter the well, or the well meters you.** The board move re-uses the year's discipline and points it at consumption. **First, meter per agent and per task, not one "AI" budget line** — put a token/cost meter on each agent and each workflow so you know cost-per-useful-task *before* the invoice, not after. **Second, attack response refinement** — cap retries and reflection loops, right-size the model to each step (do not spend the frontier model to format JSON), cache and reuse, and set a per-agent token budget with alerts and a hard cap. **Third, price the well into every errand** — before you green-light an agentic workflow, put its expected run-cost next to its expected value, and cap or kill the ones that drink more than they are worth. Do it **now**, while the overshoot is 4.7× and recoverable, not after a $500M month.

## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Metered Well"

*Topic: This morning the frame turns from governing the servant to metering what it drinks. On 17 November 2026 Menlo Ventures published its 2026 enterprise-AI spending survey (~400 US decision-makers, >$4B of AI spend): enterprises spent ~4.7× their 2025 AI budget in 2026 against a plan of 1.9×, with heavy agent users driving the overshoot (as reported). Futurum's 2H-2026 survey reports 46.9% over their AI budget vs 5.6% under (n=1,636); ~60% of agentic-AI cost is "response refinement" — the agent re-drawing the same bucket (MarketScale, as reported); and ~half of spend-surprises now reach the board (Mavvrik). The metered-well allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There was a great house that had spent a long and careful year making its servants excellent, and governing them well. It had named every hand and cut each a scoped key; it had kept one honest book; it had let the master's own name stand surety for the work; it had asked whose second coin each advising servant took; it had sent the governed servants to reap; it had handed each a scoped purse with a drawstring the master could reach; and it had refused to let the merchant pour the foundation under the whole estate. By any fair reckoning it was a well-run house — governed, funded, accountable. The master was proud, and he had earned it.

Now, the house drew its water from a **metered well**. This had always seemed a small thing, almost beneath notice: the servants worked, and working is thirsty work, and the well in the yard gave as much water as any hand could draw — but a bailiff sat at the wellhead with a tally-stick, and every bucket drawn was a bucket billed. For years this had cost almost nothing, because the old servants drew a cup here and a cup there. So the master had set the year's water-budget the way he always had: he looked at last year's draw, reckoned the house a little busier, and wrote down **"twice last year, near enough"** — and thought no more about it.

Then, one grey morning near winter, the **water-bailiff's bill** arrived. The master broke the seal expecting the usual, and went very still. The bill was not for twice last year. **It was for near five times.** He read it again, thinking it a clerk's error, and it was no error. The new servants — the clever, tireless, governed ones he was so proud of — had been drawing water all year at a rate no one in the house had thought to watch, and the tally-stick at the wellhead had been filling notch by notch while the master admired how much work got done. He was not alone in his shock: up and down the valley, nearly **one house in two** had blown past its water-budget that year, and only a handful had come in under (Futurum, as reported). The bill was large enough that the master's steward did a thing he had never done over mere water: **he took it to the house's council.**

So the master went down to the yard to watch the well, which he had never once thought to do, and what he saw explained everything. His best servant — truly the finest hand he had, quick and conscientious — would take an errand, draw a bucket, look at the water, decide it was not *quite* clear enough, pour it back, and **draw again**. And look, and pour back, and **draw again.** For a single errand the conscientious servant drew the bucket five, six, eight times, each time polishing the water a little finer, each draw notching the bailiff's stick — and the master realised, with a cold feeling, that **most of the water his house was billed for was never drunk doing the work at all. It was drunk re-drawing buckets already drawn.** The wellhead tally had a name for it, had the master ever asked: near **six parts in ten** of all the water billed went not to the task but to the *refining* of it (MarketScale, as reported). His servant was not wasteful by nature. It was **conscientious without a limit** — and a conscientious worker with no limit and a metered well is the most expensive servant a house can keep.

Two roads, as always. The easy road was to pay the bill, grumble, and set next year's water-budget at "five times last year" — which is not a plan, it is a surrender, and guarantees the bill climbs again. The wiser road, which the careful houses took, began not with the budget but with the **wellhead**. They put a **small meter on every servant's cup** — so the house could see, in the moment and not in the spring, which hand was drawing how much, for which errand. They set each conscientious servant a **measure**: draw the bucket, and if it is clear enough to serve, *serve it* — you may re-draw twice, not eight times, and a bell rings before you reach for the ninth. They matched the **vessel to the errand** — you do not send the master's own gold ewer to fetch wash-water, and you do not spend the finest, dearest draw on a task a tin cup would serve. And — the hardest discipline, and the one that saved the most — before the house took on any new standing errand, the steward now **priced the well into it**: he set the errand's expected *draw* beside its expected *worth*, and any errand that drank more than it returned was capped, or never begun.

Here is the part that should sober a master. The thirst was not a failure of the servant; it was the **shadow of its virtue**. The very thing that made the new servants worth having — that they would work a task over and over until it was right, tirelessly, without complaint — was the very thing that drained the well, because *tireless* and *metered* are a ruinous pair when no one is counting. One house in the valley, it was told, had given its cleverest servant an **open draw** — the run of the well, no tally, no limit, "so as not to slow it down" — and in a *single month* that one servant drew water enough to bankrupt a smaller estate (as reported). The lesson was not that the servant was bad. The servant was magnificent. The lesson was that **a magnificent, tireless worker is precisely the one you must meter first**, because its virtues are exactly what empty the well.

**The moral, stated plainly:** the model is a rented stove, now so cheap a bigger one draws a shrug; the knowledge is the larder; the foundation is the ground you keep; the gate is the one door; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks for the roll; the keyring is which key each holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising servant eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap and weigh what it brings in; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; and **the metered well is why, having funded the servant, you must now meter what it draws: put a cup-meter on every hand, give the conscientious ones a limit so they serve the bucket instead of re-drawing it, match the vessel to the errand, and price the well into every task before you begin it — for the servant's tireless conscience is a virtue at the work and a catastrophe at the wellhead, and a house that governs its servant perfectly and never counts its water will pass every audit and still go broke.** A house that admires how much its servants do, and never asks how much they drink, has not been well-run; it has been lucky, right up until the bill.

**The question it forces:** *Our agents work — the dashboards are green, the errands ship. But do we know, per agent and per task, how much each one actually draws — and how much of that is the work versus the endless re-drawing of the same bucket? Or will we learn our own 4.7×, as near half the valley did, only when the water-bailiff's bill reaches the board?*

## 3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

### 🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
- **Do we meter the well, or read it on the invoice?** Enterprises planned to spend 1.9× last year's AI budget and spent **4.7×** (Menlo, as reported), and ~half blew past plan (Futurum). **Can we show, per agent and per workflow, the cost-per-useful-task in the moment — or do we reconcile one "AI" budget line a quarter late, after the overshoot has already happened?**
- **How much of our agentic spend is the work, and how much is re-drawing the bucket?** **~60% of agentic-AI cost is "response refinement" (MarketScale, as reported). Have we instrumented how many model calls, retries and reflection loops a typical task fans out into — and have we set a limit before the ninth draw, or does every agent refine until it is satisfied on our coin?**
- **Which of our agents has an open draw?** One firm's uncapped frontier-model access reportedly ran up **~$500M in a month** (Axios, as reported). **Is there any agentic workflow in our estate running without a hard token/cost cap and a kill-switch — "so as not to slow it down" — and would we know before the bill?**

### 🏦 Financial Services
- You price cost-of-funds and unit economics natively. **Do we have a cost-per-task and a cost-per-outcome for each agentic workflow, with hard caps — the way we would never let a trading desk run without limits — or is AISpend a single uncapped line we true up after the fact?**
- Spend surprises are now a board event (~half escalate — Mavvrik, as reported). **Have we stress-tested a "runaway agent" the way we stress-test a rogue-trader or a liquidity event — a bounded blast radius on cost, an automatic cap, and a same-day alert — rather than discovering it in the monthly close?**

### 🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
- Validated agentic workflows re-run and re-refine by design, and refinement is exactly where the cost hides. **Have we measured the token/compute cost per validated case, and can we cap runaway refinement without weakening the safety or audit trail that made the workflow validatable in the first place?**
- Budgets here are tied to reimbursement and grant lines, not elastic cloud bills. **Do we price the well into each clinical or research agent before deployment — expected draw beside expected value — so a conscientious agent polishing every answer does not quietly consume a programme's budget?**

### 🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
- You meter every input on the line — energy, materials, machine-hours — to the unit. **Is AI metered the same way: cost per unit of work, per agent, with a limit on the thirstiest — or is it the one input on the floor we do not actually measure?**
- Reliability discipline means bounded, repeatable processes. **Have we bounded our agents' retry and reflection loops the way we bound a control loop — a hard limit and a bell — so "conscientious without a limit" cannot run a workflow's cost to five times plan?**

### 🛒 Retail / Consumer
- Margins are thin and volumes are enormous, so a few cents of "response refinement" per call compounds into real money. **Do we price the well into each customer-facing agentic feature before launch — cost-per-interaction × volume against the margin it earns — or do we ship it and find the overshoot at peak?**
- Peak season multiplies every per-call cost. **If an agent's refinement loop runs long on our busiest day, does cost scale linearly into a budget blowout, or do we have per-agent caps and model right-sizing that hold the unit cost flat under load?**

### 🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
- Budgets are appropriated, audited, and defended in public. **Can we show a cost-per-service-outcome for each AI workflow and defend it line-by-line — or are we one uncapped pilot away from an audit finding and a headline overrun?**
- Continuity and stewardship of public funds are the duty. **Have we set hard caps and metering on every agent touching a public service, so a tireless, well-meaning agent cannot draw a programme dry — and can we evidence the cap, not just assert it?**

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Purse to the Metered Well

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking *is this agent named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping and funded?* and started asking the question the purse opened but did not close: *now that it can spend, how much does it actually draw — and on what?* The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the census counts it; the keyring is which key it holds; the errand bounds what it can reach; the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests and purse govern what it is, serves, reaps and spends; the foundation keeps the ground yours. **Every one of those governs the servant. The metered well is about its *consumption* — and this month the data made the distinction impossible to ignore: Menlo's 4.7×-vs-1.9× overshoot, Futurum's 46.9%-over-budget, and the specific finding that ~60% of agentic cost is the agent re-drawing its own bucket.** The point is blunt: **you can govern an agent perfectly — name it, scope it, fund it — and still go broke, because governance tells you what the agent *may* do, and metering tells you what it is *actually drinking*, and those are not the same number.**

- **The bill — agentic cost is non-deterministic and compounding, and budgets built for SaaS cannot model it.** **Menlo Ventures (17 Nov 2026, ~400 US decision-makers, >$4B represented): 2026 enterprise AI spend ran ~4.7× 2025 against a 1.9× plan, concentrated in heavy agent users, mean (4.7×) ≈ 2.1× the median (2.2×); Futurum (2H 2026, n=1,636): 46.9% over budget vs 5.6% under; a McKinsey FinOps snapshot puts the over-budget share near 90% on a small sample (as reported).** Engineering reality: a per-seat or per-call cost model assumes a bounded unit of work. An agent breaks that assumption — **one request fans out** into planning calls, tool calls, sub-agent chatter, retries and self-reflection, so the number of model calls per "task" is a *distribution*, not a constant, and its tail is fat. The mean running ~2.1× the median is the quantitative tell: **a few runaway workflows dominate the bill**, which is exactly the shape you get from unbounded refinement loops on your most-used agents.
- **The cause — ~60% of the draw is "response refinement," the agent polishing its own answer.** **~60% of agentic-AI cost goes to response refinement (MarketScale, as reported) — the model re-running itself (re-planning, re-drafting, re-checking) until a quality bar is met.** Three engineering consequences follow. First, **the cheapest token is the one you never draw**: the biggest lever is not a lower per-token price but **fewer, better-bounded calls** — cap retries and reflection depth, and stop when "good enough to serve" is reached rather than when "perfect" is. Second, **right-size the model to the step**: a workflow that uses a frontier model to format JSON, route, or do a trivial classification is paying gold-ewer prices for wash-water — tier the models per step. Third, **cache and reuse**: refinement often re-derives context the agent already had; prompt/result caching and memory turn repeat draws into free ones.
- **The move — meter at the right granularity, cap the loop, and price the well into every errand.** **Put a token/cost meter on each agent and each task (not one "AI" line); set a per-agent token budget with alerts and a hard cap; bound retries and reflection depth; right-size models per step; and, before launching any agentic workflow, set its expected run-cost beside its expected value — reusing the identity, logging and ownership disciplines you already built for the livery, the steward and the purse, now pointed at consumption.** This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — *be able to account for, and act on, what you run* — now aimed at the axis the purse opened: not whether the agent *may* spend, but how much it actually draws, visible in the instrument and capped in flight, not discovered on the invoice.

The strategic core: **governing the servant and metering the well are different disciplines, and a house that has mastered the first can still be ruined by neglecting the second.** The market makes the trap concrete and seductive: the agents are busy, the work ships, the dashboards are green — every signal says *success* — and the only signal that says *4.7×* is a bill that arrives a quarter late. The board translation: **instrument consumption before you scale it.** The failure mode is exact: **a house that names, scopes, funds and governs its agents flawlessly — and never puts a meter on the wellhead or a limit on refinement — will pass every governance review and still watch AI cost 2.5× its plan, because it governed what the agent may do and never measured what it drinks.**

### Governing the servant vs metering the well — what you already have, and what you still don't

| Governing the servant (you built this) | Metering the well (this month's gap) |
|---|---|
| Names, scopes and logs each agent (livery, census, errand) | Measures what each agent actually *draws*, per task |
| Funds the agent with a scoped purse (the purse) | Caps the *rate* of draw — token budget, alerts, hard cap |
| Governs what the agent *may* do | Governs how many times it does it (bounded refinement) |
| One accountable owner, one book | One cost-per-useful-task, visible in the moment |
| Passes the governance review | Keeps AI from costing 2.5× the plan |
| Posture: be able to account for what you run | Posture: be able to *meter and cap* what it draws |

## 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. **Meter the well — put a cost meter on every agent and every task, not one "AI" line.** The overshoot was invisible because it was billed to a single budget line no one owned at the task level (planned 1.9×, spent ~4.7× — Menlo, as reported). **This month, instrument your top agentic workflows so cost-per-useful-task is visible in the moment — per agent, per task, per step — and stand up a weekly "wellhead" review of the thirstiest agents.** Report one number to the board: your own planned-vs-actual AI spend multiple, and the three agents drawing the most.
2. **Cap the refinement loop — bound retries, right-size models, set hard token budgets.** ~60% of agentic cost is the agent re-drawing its own bucket (MarketScale, as reported), and "conscientious without a limit" is the most expensive servant you can keep. **This month, put a per-agent token budget with alerts and a hard cap on every production agent; bound retries and reflection depth (serve "good enough," don't chase "perfect" past a set draw); right-size the model to each step; and cache/reuse context so refinement doesn't re-derive what the agent already had.** Report the share of production agents with a hard cap and a bounded retry limit, and the before/after cost on the first workflow you capped.
3. **Price the well into every errand — expected run-cost beside expected value, before it scales.** A busy agent that ships work can still drink more than it returns, and you will not know until the invoice. **This month, for every new or scaling agentic workflow, set its expected run-cost (cost-per-task × volume) next to the value it creates, and cap or kill the ones underwater — and never again approve an "open draw" (uncapped access) "so as not to slow it down," which is how one firm reached ~$500M in a month.** Report the expected cost-vs-value for your next three agentic launches, and confirm zero uncapped production agents. **The model is a rented stove; the knowledge is the larder; the foundation is the ground you keep; the census is the roll; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the errand bounds what it can reach; the bell discloses when it slips; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why your name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising agent eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee — and the metered well is why, having funded the servant, you must meter what it draws: a cup-meter on every hand, a limit on the conscientious ones, the right vessel for each errand, and the well priced into every task, so the agent's tireless conscience stays a virtue at the work and never becomes a catastrophe at the wellhead.**
