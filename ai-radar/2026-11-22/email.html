The Metered Well

Sunday, 22 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

The agents work — and they are blowing through the budget. Enterprises planned to spend 1.9× last year's AI budget and spent ~4.7× (Menlo, 17 Nov), with ~60% of agentic cost going to "response refinement" — the agent re-drawing the same bucket. Meter per agent and per task, cap the refinement loop, and price the well into every errand.

1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from governing the servant to metering what it drinks — because the agents are excellent, and governed, and thirsty, and the well is metered.

1. The bill — the agent works, and it is drinking 4.7× the budget. Menlo Ventures (17 Nov 2026, ~400 US decision-makers, >$4B represented): 2026 enterprise AI spend averaged ~4.7× 2025 against a plan of 1.9×, the overshoot concentrated in heavy agent users; the mean (4.7×) ≈ 2.1× the median (2.2×). Futurum (n=1,636): 46.9% over budget vs 5.6% under; Uber reportedly spent its 2026 AI budget in four months (Menlo; Futurum; Inc., as reported).

2. The cause — most of the water is spent re-drawing the bucket. A single request fans out into dozens of model calls — plan, tool, reflect, retry, refine — and ~60% of agentic cost is "response refinement," the agent re-running itself to polish an answer (MarketScale, as reported). Budgets built for per-seat SaaS cannot model a worker that can do its job five times. ~Half of spend surprises now reach the board (Mavvrik).

3. The move — meter per agent and per task, cap refinement, price the well into every errand. Put a cost meter on each agent and workflow (cost-per-useful-task before the invoice); cap retries and reflection, right-size models per step, set a hard token budget; and set each agentic feature's run-cost beside its value before launch.

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each govern the servant — the metered well is about what it drinks. An excellent, well-governed agent is also a thirsty one, and most of the draw is the agent re-drawing the same bucket. You funded the servant like an employee; now read the bill, and put a meter on the well before the well puts a meter on you.

2 · Allegory — The Metered Well

A great house had spent a careful year making its servants excellent and governing them well — named, booked, bonded, waged, sent to reap, funded with a scoped purse. It drew its water from a metered well: a bailiff at the wellhead with a tally-stick, every bucket drawn a bucket billed. The master set the year's water-budget as he always had — "twice last year, near enough" — and thought no more about it.

Then the water-bailiff's bill arrived, and it was not for twice last year. It was for near five times. The clever, tireless, governed servants had been drawing water all year at a rate no one thought to watch — and up the valley nearly one house in two had blown past its water-budget. Watching the well, the master saw why: his finest servant would draw a bucket, decide it not quite clear enough, pour it back, and draw again — five, six, eight times for one errand. Most of the water was never drunk doing the work; it was drunk re-drawing buckets already drawn — near six parts in ten going to the refining, not the task.

So the wise houses began at the wellhead, not the budget: a meter on every cup (see which hand draws how much, for which errand); a limit for the conscientious (serve the bucket if it's clear enough — re-draw twice, not eight, a bell before the ninth); the right vessel for the errand (no gold ewer for wash-water); and the well priced into every task (expected draw beside expected worth). The thirst was the shadow of the servant's virtue — tireless and metered are a ruinous pair when no one counts, and one house that gave its cleverest servant an open draw watched it drink a smaller estate dry in a single month.

The question it forces: Our agents work — the dashboards are green, the errands ship. But do we know, per agent and per task, how much each one draws — and how much of that is the work versus the endless re-drawing of the same bucket? Or will we learn our own 4.7×, as near half the valley did, only when the bill reaches the board?

3 · Technical — From the Purse to the Metered Well

Governing the servant and metering the well are different disciplines: governance tells you what the agent may do; metering tells you what it is actually drinking — and those are not the same number. The bill: a per-seat/per-call cost model assumes a bounded unit of work, but an agent fans one request into planning, tools, sub-agent chatter, retries and reflection, so calls-per-task is a distribution with a fat tail — the mean running ~2.1× the median means a few runaway workflows dominate the bill. The cause: ~60% of agentic cost is response refinement, so the biggest lever is fewer, better-bounded calls — cap retries and reflection depth, stop at "good enough," right-size the model to each step (don't spend the frontier model to format JSON), and cache/reuse context. The move: meter per agent and per task, set a per-agent token budget with alerts and a hard cap, and price the well into every errand before it scales.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Meter the well. The overshoot was invisible because it was billed to one "AI" line no one owned at the task level (planned 1.9×, spent ~4.7×). Instrument your top agentic workflows so cost-per-useful-task is visible in the moment — per agent, per task, per step — and stand up a weekly "wellhead" review of the thirstiest agents. Report your own planned-vs-actual AI spend multiple and the three agents drawing the most.

2. Cap the refinement loop. ~60% of agentic cost is the agent re-drawing its own bucket, and "conscientious without a limit" is the most expensive servant you can keep. Put a per-agent token budget with alerts and a hard cap on every production agent; bound retries and reflection depth; right-size the model to each step; and cache/reuse context. Report the share of production agents with a hard cap and a bounded retry limit, and the before/after cost on the first workflow you capped.

3. Price the well into every errand. A busy agent can still drink more than it returns, and you won't know until the invoice. For every new or scaling agentic workflow, set its expected run-cost (cost-per-task × volume) beside the value it creates, and cap or kill the ones underwater — and never approve an "open draw" "so as not to slow it down," which is how one firm reached ~$500M in a month. Report expected cost-vs-value for your next three launches, and confirm zero uncapped production agents.
