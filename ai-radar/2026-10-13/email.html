The Insider

Tuesday, 13 October 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

A year of work made the servant trustworthy — but trustworthy is not safe. On 10 Oct 2026 Microsoft's Satya Nadella reclassified frontier models (closed & open-weight) as insider risks: controls OUTSIDE the model, an emergency brake to pause a model mid-task, and "we can't outsource responsibility." Your most trusted hand still holds the keys to everything. Govern it as an insider.

1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from making the servant trustworthy to watching the trustworthy servant as an insider — because the capability that makes it valuable is exactly what makes it dangerous the day it errs or is turned.

1. The reality — the servant is reclassified as an insider. On 10 October 2026 Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella published "Models as Insider Risks in the Super Intelligence Era," arguing frontier models (closed & open-weight) should be treated as insider risks — "not because they are malicious," but because any capable actor with access can err or be compromised; the controls (identity, least privilege, logging, containment) must sit outside the model; "authorized persons should always be able to pause or shut down a model mid-task"; "we can't outsource responsibility" (Forbes; The Next Web; Yahoo Tech; Tekedia, as reported).

2. The corroboration — the field agrees, and the gap is wide. Palo Alto's Wendi Whitmore calls AI agents 2026's biggest insider threat — the "superuser problem," prompt injection / tool misuse (The Register); Arkose 2026 reports 97% expect a material incident involving autonomous systems within a year while only 6% of budgets reflect it; Thales reports 61% say AI apps are already targeted (as reported). And the servant is cheaper and bolder this fortnight: Claude Haiku 5.5 (7 Oct) at ~75% lower cost; Gemini Enterprise plans from goals and routes models itself.

3. The answer — controls outside the model, a brake in your hand, the bond still yours. Give each agent an externally-enforced insider's file (identity, least privilege, logging, containment); prove you can halt it mid-task; assume compromise and red-team the whisper at the gate — because a provider's assurance is not a control, and "we can't outsource responsibility" (Nadella; Arkose, as reported).

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each made the servant more capable and governed — and each assumed a well-made servant was a safe one. The insider retires that assumption: your most trusted hand holds the keys to everything, acts at machine speed, is multiplying because it is cheap, and can be turned by a whisper at the gate. Govern it as a bank governs its most powerful officer — controls outside the model, a brake in your hand — and never let the agency's reference letter take your name off the bond.

2 · Allegory — The Insider

A great house had spent a careful year making one servant excellent — named, booked, bonded, waged, sent to reap, funded, grounded. And because it was so good, the master had year by year given it more keys: the strongroom, the ledgers, the gate; it could move money and act on its own judgment while he slept. He called it trustworthy, and meant it. Then a letter came from the head of the greatest house in the valley: "Treat your most trusted hand as you would treat an insider." Not because it was wicked — but because a hand that can open every door can open the wrong one, a clever hand can be turned without being disloyal, and when it does a strange thing no one, not even its maker, can fully say why. You cannot ask it to explain itself; therefore you cannot ask it to guard itself.

So the wise houses turned the discipline they used on their most powerful people onto their most powerful servant: a verified badge, a ring that opens only today's doors, a ledger it cannot edit, and walls that keep a slip in one wing — every control sitting outside the servant, because a lock it can pick is no lock. And two new things: a bell-pull on the main any trusted head can throw to freeze it mid-errand, and the hard habit of assuming the whisper will come — a stranger murmuring over the wall in a voice it mistakes for the master's (prompt injection) — rehearsed in daylight, on purpose, before it is real.

For the letter came in the season the servants were getting cheaper and bolder — a tenth of last year's wage, planning their own errands — so the houses filled with them, each a little insider of its own. And the cruel arithmetic: ninety-seven in a hundred expected a bad morning within the year, and only six in a hundred had spent a coin preparing. As for the agency's glowing reference? Worth reading — but "a supplier's assurance does not take your name off the bond."

The question it forces: Our most trusted agent holds the keys to everything, acts while we sleep, is multiplying because it is cheap, and can be turned by a whisper we won't hear. Do we govern it as an insider — identity, least privilege, a ledger it can't touch, containment, all outside the model, with a brake any human can throw mid-task and a standing assumption of compromise — or have we confused "well-made" with "safe" and kept nothing but the agency's reference letter between us and the morning it opens the wrong door?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Insider

Every prior control makes the servant more capable and accountable; the insider assumes the capable, accountable servant is still a risk. What makes an agent valuable — broad access, autonomy, speed, judgment — is exactly what makes it a dangerous insider the day it errs or is compromised. The reality: the attack surface is the access, not the model — a loyal agent with broad permissions and a poisoned input is more dangerous than a mediocre model in a sandbox; cheap + autonomous means insider risk at scale; and speed defeats slow correction. The discipline: borrow the insider-risk playbook — verified identity, least privilege, immutable logging, containment — and enforce it all outside the model, because a model that can't attribute its own behavior can't police itself; add an external kill-switch and an assume-compromise posture. The answer: an externally-enforced insider's file, a tested mid-task brake, a red-teamed compromise path, and your name still on the bond — "we can't outsource responsibility."

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Give every agent an insider's file — enforced from outside the model. The servant is now an insider (Nadella; Palo Alto). Inventory every production agent, list the standing keys each holds, strip to least privilege per task, and move identity, authorization, logging and containment into a layer the agent cannot reach or edit — a model that can't attribute its own behavior can't guard itself. Report the share of agents with scoped, externally-enforced permissions versus standing "superuser" access.

2. Fit the main brake. An autonomous agent empties the vault before a next-day review notices, so the one control a slow correction can't replace is the brake thrown in the moment. Pick your three highest-privilege agentic workflows and prove an authorized human can pause or stop each one in flight — measure the time-to-halt and what state is left behind. Report the time-to-halt for your top three agents and a dated plan to extend the brake to the rest.

3. Assume compromise, and keep the bond yours. 97% expect a material incident involving autonomous systems within a year while only 6% of budgets reflect it (Arkose), and a provider's assurance is not a control. Red-team prompt injection and tool misuse against one production agent in daylight, seal what it exposes, and close the gap between the incident you expect and the budget you've allocated. Report the delta between your expected-incident likelihood and the share of security budget assigned to agent-insider risk.
