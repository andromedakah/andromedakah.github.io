The Woodpile

Thursday, 12 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

The stove-seller did what the September radar foretold — cleverer stove, price of fuel cut a fifth. And the catch: the cleverer the stove, the more wood it burns. The log got cheaper; the woodpile per dinner can still rise. Price the finished job, not the token.

1 · Executive Summary

In September the radar made its peace with the stove: the model had commoditized, so shop stoves ruthlessly. This morning the stove-seller proved the point and complicated it in the same release.

1. The reality — the stove got cleverer and cheaper per log. On 9 November 2026 Anthropic shipped Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 — record benchmarks (FrontierCode 2.0: 49.1% Xhigh vs 5.1's 32.4%) and a 20% price cut to $8/$40 (down from $10/$50), output up to 30% faster, one model with two safeguard sets, pre-tested by UK AISI and METR (VentureBeat; Computerworld, as reported). The September "cheap stove" call held.

2. The catch — the clever stove burns more wood. Independent testing found Fable 5.2 at Medium effort wrote ~2× the tokens of 5.1 and cost ~56% more per task; reasoning models broadly can be half the per-token price and spend 2–3× the tokens and time per finished job (Decrypt, "Impressive and Expensive"; token-economics reviews, as reported) — Altman having already called token costs "a huge issue." Price-per-token fell; cost-per-task can rise.

3. The subplot — the inspector knocks. On 14 October 2026 the EU AI Office sent its first formal Requests for Information under the AI Act (security, external evaluation, post-market monitoring; OpenAI and Anthropic reported among recipients; fines up to €15M / 3% of turnover) (Reed Smith; CSA, as reported). And a Zapier survey (updated 9 Nov) puts 88% of leaders using/testing agents — more agents, more tokens.

Bottom line: the cheaper, cleverer stove is a real gift — buy it. But the same clever fire mutters to itself while it works, and the muttering is wood: up to twice the tokens, more than half again the cost, for the same meal. Change your unit of account — price the dinner (cost per completed task), not the log (price per token) — match effort to the dish, and keep producible books, because the inspector has stopped writing and started knocking.

2 · Allegory — The Woodpile

A great house had, at last, learned to be wise about its stove — shop stoves ruthlessly, keep the hearth your own. And this week the stove-seller arrived with a gift that seemed to vindicate every word: a cleverer stove, and a price for firewood cut by a fifth. The kitchen cheered; the master signed before the cart had stopped rolling. "This," he said, "is what winning looks like."

But the cook watched the clever stove cook the same plain dinner. It was marvellous and it was busy — it muttered, considered, reconsidered, turned the roast over three times in its mind before the pan. And when the dinner was done, she measured the woodshed: the clever stove had burned very nearly twice the wood to cook the very same meal. "Master, the wood is cheaper and our wood bill is higher. You read the price on the log; I weigh the pile by the dinner. The cart brought you a cheaper log and a hungrier fire, and the hungrier fire won."

So the wise houses changed their unit of account: they stopped pricing the log and started weighing the woodpile per dinner. They found most dinners did not need a stove set to ponder — toast does not require a philosopher — so they turned the clever fire down for plain work and saved its hungry high setting for the dishes that earned it. And that week a second knock came: the inspector who for a year had only sent letters asked, this time, to see the books. The houses with producible records opened a drawer; the rest looked, too late, for a pen.

The question it forces: The log is cheaper and the stove cleverer — the radar's own call come true. But are we cheering the price on the token while the bill per finished job climbs? Do we price the dinner or only the log? Have we matched effort to the dish — or is it deliberating over toast on our coin? And can we open the drawer, now the inspector knocks?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Woodpile

The thing that fell (price per token) is not the thing you pay (cost per finished job) — confusing the two is how a house that feels thrifty on every invoice runs its AI budget dry. The reality: Fable 5.2 is cleverer and 20% cheaper per token, and "effort" is now a first-class cost lever — the unit that matters is cost-per-completed-task at a chosen effort, not the headline rate. The catch: thinking is wood, and output ($40/M) dominates the bill — a 20% price cut is erased by a 25% rise in tokens-per-task, and reasoning models can run 2–3× the tokens per job. The answer: instrument cost-per-completed-task, route each task to the smallest model and lowest effort that clears its bar, cap and alert on tokens-per-task, and keep model/effort/tokens/cost in a producible record.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Price the dinner, not the log. The stove got 20% cheaper per token and ~2× hungrier per job, so the per-token price is no longer a reliable proxy for what you pay. Pick your top three AI workloads and measure cost-per-completed-task (model × effort × tokens × price), then compare it against last quarter's — and find out whether your true AI unit cost fell when the sticker did, or rose. Report fully-loaded cost-per-completed-task, trended.

2. Match the effort to the dish. The same model spans a wide band of tokens-per-task by effort, and the benchmark-winning score is the costliest way to run it. Audit which workloads run on your most capable, highest-effort model, and down-route the routine long tail to the smallest model and lowest setting that clears the bar; cap tokens-per-task and alert on drift, especially after a model upgrade. Report the share of tasks running above the effort their bar requires.

3. Keep the books producible. The EU AI Office issued its first formal RFIs on 14 October 2026, with fines up to €15M / 3% of turnover. Confirm that for every production AI system you capture model version, effort, inputs, outputs, token counts and cost in a producible record, and run a dry-run "RFI drill": could you answer, in a week, what a system did and what it cost? Report pass/fail per system and a dated plan for the gaps.
