And in the same breath revealed the catch: the cleverer the stove, the more wood it burns. The price of the log fell; the woodpile per dinner can still rise. Thursday, 12 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering

On 9 November 2026 Anthropic shipped Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 — record-high benchmarks and a 20% cut in the API price, to $8/$40 per million tokens (down from $10/$50 on 5.1), with output up to 30% faster (VentureBeat; Computerworld; Decrypt, as reported). The stove got both cleverer and cheaper per log — exactly the commoditization the radar predicted. Then the catch, in the same coverage: the cleverer the stove, the more wood it burns. Independent testing found Fable 5.2 at a middling effort setting wrote roughly twice the tokens of 5.1 and cost ~56% more per task on real DevOps work, and the reasoning-model pattern is now familiar — a model can cost less per token and two-to-three times more per finished job (Decrypt, "Impressive and Expensive"; token-economics reviews, as reported). The board question is no longer is the stove cheap? — it is: what does a finished meal cost, once we count the wood this clever new stove actually burns? And a second bell: on 14 October 2026 the EU AI Office sent its first formal Requests for Information under the AI Act to leading providers — the inspector who for a year only sent letters has started knocking on doors.

1 · Executive Summary 90-second read

For a long year the radar worked on the servant — name it (the livery), keep one book (the steward), put your name on the bond (the surety), check its wage (the second wage), send it to reap (the two harvests), hand it a scoped purse (the purse) — and in September it turned to the ground the house stands on (the foundation), ruling that the model had become a cheap, swappable stove. This morning the stove-seller proved the point and complicated it in the same release.

The reality — the stove got cleverer and cheaper per log; the radar's September call held. On 9 November 2026 Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2, one underlying model with two safeguard sets (Fable open to all; Mythos limited to vetted security and life-sciences organizations), available through AWS, Azure and Google Cloud, and — per Anthropic — pre-tested before launch by the UK AI Security Institute and METR (as reported). It pairs record-high benchmarks with a 20% price cut: $8/$40 per million input/output tokens, down from $10/$50 on 5.1, with an optional fast mode at $16/$80, Batch API at $4/$20, and output as much as 30% faster (VentureBeat; Computerworld, as reported). On FrontierCode 2.0, Fable 5.2 at Xhigh effort reportedly scored 49.1% against 5.1's 32.4%, and at High effort roughly matched Opus 5.5's best score (45.3% vs 45.5%) for "roughly half the cost per task," beating GPT-6 Astra on every listed benchmark and trailing Gemini 4 Carbon only on AutomationBench (53.9% vs 51.4%). This is the stove the radar told you to shop for: cleverer, and cheaper per log.

The catch — the clever stove burns more wood, so the sticker price is not the bill. The same coverage carries the warning the radar has flagged since Grok 4.7 in September: a lower price per token does not mean a lower cost per finished job. Independent testing found Fable 5.2 at Medium effort wrote about twice the tokens of Fable 5.1 and cost ~56% more per task on a set of DevOps tasks; the broader reasoning-model pattern is that a model can be half the per-token price and spend two-to-three times the tokens and time to complete the same work (token-economics reviews, as reported), which is why Decrypt's verdict on 5.2 was blunt — "Impressive and Expensive." It is the fear Sam Altman already named when he admitted AI token costs are becoming "a huge issue" (Tom's Hardware, owner-verified). The 20% price cut is real and welcome; it is also the headline that hides the meter. The log got cheaper; the woodpile per dinner can still rise — because the clever stove deliberates, and deliberation is wood.

The subplot — the inspector stopped writing and started knocking. On 14 October 2026, at the Dublin AI summit, the EU AI Office (via Henna Virkkunen) confirmed its first formal Requests for Information under the AI Act to leading GPAI providers — covering security practices, external evaluation of models, and post-market monitoring — with OpenAI and Anthropic reported among the recipients (the Commission has not published the full list), traced to summer-2026 incidents in which lab models reached outside systems, and made with fines of up to €15M or 3% of global turnover for non-compliance (Reed Smith; Cloud Security Alliance; Lawfare, as reported). A separate copyright-related request reportedly reached 30+ companies. For a year the radar said keep the books the inspector will one day ask for; the inspector is now at the door. And the backdrop that makes the fuel bill matter: a Zapier survey updated 9 November 2026 reports 88% of enterprise leaders now use or test AI agents (up from 72% in 2025), yet only a minority run them in production — Camunda puts just 11% of agentic use cases in production in the past year (as reported). More agents, more tokens, a live inspector, and a bill measured in wood.

2 · Allegory of the Day

🖋️ "The Woodpile"

Topic: This morning the stove-seller did exactly what the September radar foretold — made the stove cleverer and cut the price of its fuel — and in the same breath revealed the catch. On 9 November 2026 Anthropic shipped Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2: record benchmarks with a 20% price cut to $8/$40 per million tokens, output ~30% faster (VentureBeat; Computerworld; Decrypt, as reported). But independent testing found the new model writes roughly twice the tokens and costs ~56% more per task at a middling effort setting, and reasoning models broadly can be half the per-token price yet 2–3× the tokens and time per finished job (Decrypt, "Impressive and Expensive"; token-economics reviews, as reported) — Altman having already called AI token costs "a huge issue." In the background, on 14 October 2026 the EU AI Office sent its first formal Requests for Information to leading providers (Reed Smith; CSA, as reported). The woodpile allegory is the radar's own illustration.

There was a great house that had, at last, learned to be wise about its stove. For a season it had fretted over which fire to rent, until the radar taught it the plain truth: fire is a commodity, so shop stoves ruthlessly, keep the hearth your own, and never let the stove-seller pour your foundation. The house took the lesson to heart, and was proud of it — as it should have been.

And this very week the stove-seller arrived with a gift that seemed to vindicate every word. A cleverer stove — one that could cook dishes last season's fire could not manage at all — and, stamped on its side, a price for firewood cut by a fifth. The steward read the new price aloud and the kitchen cheered: a smarter fire and cheaper wood, in the same cart, on the same morning. The master signed for it before the cart had stopped rolling. "This," he said, "is what winning looks like."

But the cook, who had kept her own tally since the year the house nearly ruined itself on a runaway fire, did not cheer. She lit the clever stove and set it to cook the same plain dinner she cooked every night — and she watched. The new stove was marvellous and it was busy: it muttered to itself, it considered, it reconsidered, it turned the roast over in its mind three times before it turned it over in the pan. And when the dinner was done — a good dinner, better than last year's — she went out to the woodshed and measured what it had taken. The clever stove had burned very nearly twice the wood of the old one to cook the very same meal.

So she did the sum the master had not. The price of each log had fallen by a fifth, true. But the stove had burned near double the logs. "Master," she said, "the wood is cheaper and our wood bill is higher. You have been reading the price on the log and I have been weighing the pile by the dinner, and we have been reading two different accounts of the same kitchen. The cart did not bring you a cheaper supper. It brought you a cheaper log and a hungrier fire, and the hungrier fire won."

Two roads, as always. The easy road was the master's: read the sticker, cheer the cut, and discover the true bill only at quarter's end, when the woodshed was bare three weeks early and no single invoice showed a higher price anywhere. The wiser road, which the careful houses took, began with a change of unit. They stopped pricing the log and started weighing the woodpile per dinner. They asked not what does a token cost? but what does a finished job cost, once we count every token this clever stove mutters on the way? And when they measured honestly, they found something freeing: most dinners did not need a stove set to ponder. Toast does not require a philosopher. They turned the clever stove down to a plain flame for plain work, and saved its deliberating, wood-hungry high setting for the few dishes that truly earned it — and their woodpile lasted, and their suppers were still better than last year's.

Here is the part that should steady a master's hand. The cheaper log is not a trick and the cleverer stove is not a villain; both are real gifts, and the radar still says buy them. The error is only in the unit of account. A house that measures its fire by the price of a log will always feel thrifty and always run short, because the one number that fell is the one number printed on the invoice, and the one number that rose — the tokens burned per task — is the one number nobody sends you. The vendor cut the sticker because the market made him; the stove burns more because thinking is expensive; and the gap between those two truths is precisely the margin a careless house loses while congratulating itself on a discount.

And there was a second knock at the door that week, quieter than the cart but heavier. For a year an inspector had sent the house letters — keep your books, record your fires, be ready to show your workings — and the house had filed them, half-believing the day would never come. This week the inspector did not send a letter. He knocked, and asked to see the books. The houses that had kept producible records opened a drawer; the houses that had kept a slide about record-keeping began, too late, to look for a pen. The lesson braided into the first: the fire you can afford and the books you can produce are the same discipline — knowing, in a form you can show, exactly what your kitchen did and what it cost.

The moral, stated plainly: the model is a rented stove, now cleverer and cheaper per log; the fuel is the tokens it burns, and the woodpile is what a finished job actually costs; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector — who this week stopped writing and started knocking — asks you to produce the roll; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising servant eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; the foundation is why you own the ground the stove plugs into — and the woodpile is why, having bought the cleverer, cheaper stove the radar told you to buy, you must change your unit of account from the price of a log to the weight of the pile per dinner: price the finished job, not the token; turn the clever fire down for plain work and save its hungry high setting for the dishes that earn it; and keep your books producible, because the inspector is no longer writing. A house that cheers a cheaper log while a hungrier fire doubles its woodpile has not struck a bargain; it has bought a discount on the one number it reads and a premium on the one number it does not — and called the arrangement a saving.

The question it forces: The log is cheaper and the stove is cleverer — the radar's own call come true. But are we cheering the price on the token while the bill per finished job quietly climbs? Do we price the dinner (cost per completed task) or only the log (cost per token)? Have we matched the stove's effort to the dish — a plain flame for plain work — or is it deliberating over toast on our coin? And now that the inspector has stopped writing letters and knocked on the door, can we open the drawer and produce the books?

3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
  • Do we price the dinner or the log? The new stove is cheaper per token (Fable 5.2 at $8/$40, down 20%) and hungrier per job (reportedly ~2× the tokens, ~56% more per task at Medium effort). For our top AI workloads, do we track cost-per-completed-task — not just price-per-token — and did our true AI unit cost actually fall when the sticker did, or did it quietly rise?
  • Have we matched the stove's effort to the dish? Reasoning models burn far more tokens at higher "effort," and most tasks do not need the top setting. Do we route each workload to the smallest model and lowest effort that clears the quality bar, or is a premium, deliberating model doing work a cheaper flame would finish — deliberating over toast on our coin?
  • Can we produce the books now that the inspector knocks? The EU AI Office sent its first formal RFIs on 14 October 2026 (security, external evaluation, post-market monitoring). If a regulator — or our own board — asked today for the records of what our AI systems did and what it cost, could we open the drawer, or do we only have a slide about record-keeping?
🏦 Financial Services
  • Cost-of-serve is a supervised number, and token consumption now sits inside it. Can we show the fully-loaded cost-per-completed-task for each AI-driven process (not the per-token rate the vendor quotes), and have we modelled what a 2–3× token-per-task swing on a model upgrade does to the P&L of a high-volume workflow?
  • The EU AI Office's first information requests are live and backed by fines up to €15M / 3% of turnover. Under the high-risk duties we already owe, can we produce — on demand — the logs, evaluations and post-market monitoring records for our customer-facing AI, the way we produce a trade blotter for a supervisor?
🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
  • Mythos 5.2 is gated to vetted security and life-sciences organizations — capability is arriving with safeguards attached. When we adopt a more capable, more token-hungry model for clinical or research work, have we budgeted the real fuel cost per validated task, and does a model swap re-open validation we thought was settled?
  • Record-keeping is both a safety and a compliance asset. Are our AI records — inputs, model version, effort setting, outputs, cost — captured in a producible form, so that the inspector's knock is a drawer we open, not a scramble?
🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
  • You already measure cost-per-unit ruthlessly; AI inference is now a variable input. Have we put a tokens-per-task meter on our AI the way we meter energy per unit — so a cheaper per-token price that doubles tokens-per-job shows up as the higher bill it is, not the saving it pretends to be?
  • Effort tiering is the AI equivalent of right-sizing a machine to a job. Are we running our highest-reasoning (and most expensive-per-task) model only where the work earns it, and a plain, cheap model on the long tail of routine tasks?
🛒 Retail / Consumer
  • Your margins live and die on cost-of-serve at volume. At our query volumes, does the 20% per-token price cut actually lower our cost per resolved customer interaction, or does a more deliberative model erase the cut by burning more tokens per resolution — and do we measure which?
  • Peak season multiplies every per-task cost. Have we load-tested the fully-loaded token cost of our AI-driven search / support / checkout at peak volume on the new model, with effort tuned down for routine traffic, before the bill arrives?
🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
  • Public money demands cost you can defend and records you can produce. Can we justify our AI spend in cost-per-completed-service terms (not per-token), and produce the decision and cost records a regulator or auditor would now expect, given the EU AI Office has begun formal information requests?
  • Continuity and accountability outlast any one model. Are we procuring AI with the fuel bill and the record-keeping duty priced in from the start — effort matched to the task, tokens metered, books producible — rather than chasing the lowest sticker price on a token?

4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Foundation to the Woodpile

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking which stove is cheapest? and started asking the question the sticker cannot answer: what does a finished job actually cost, once I count the wood this clever stove burns thinking about it? The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count (and this week came to the door); the keyring is which key it holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what it can reach; the bell discloses when it slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests and purse govern the servant; the foundation is the ground it stands on. This morning the axis is the fuel. The model the September radar called a cheap, swappable stove just got cleverer and 20% cheaper per token — and, in the same release notes, measurably hungrier per task. The thing that fell (price per token) is not the thing you pay (cost per finished job), and confusing the two is how a house that feels thrifty on every invoice runs its AI budget dry.

  • The reality — the stove is cleverer and cheaper per log; the September call held. On 9 November 2026 Anthropic shipped Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 — one model, two safeguard sets (Fable open; Mythos vetted security/life-sciences) — on AWS, Azure and Google Cloud, pre-tested by the UK AI Security Institute and METR (as reported), pairing record benchmarks (FrontierCode 2.0: 49.1% Xhigh vs 5.1's 32.4%; High effort 45.3% ≈ Opus 5.5's 45.5% at ~half the cost per task; beats GPT-6 Astra on every listed benchmark; trails Gemini 4 Carbon only on AutomationBench 53.9% vs 51.4%) with a 20% price cut to $8/$40 (fast mode $16/$80; Batch $4/$20) and output up to 30% faster (VentureBeat; Computerworld; officechai, as reported). Architecturally, three things follow. First, commoditization is continuing exactly as called — capability up, per-token price down, models interchangeable behind an interface you own. Second, "effort" is now a first-class cost lever — the same model spans a wide band of tokens-per-task depending on its reasoning setting, so the unit that matters is cost-per-completed-task at a chosen effort, not the headline rate. Third, the benchmark that sells the model (peak score at Xhigh effort) is the most expensive way to run it — the launch number and the production number are rarely the same setting.
  • The catch — thinking is wood, and the meter is on the output side. Independent testing found Fable 5.2 at Medium effort wrote ~2× the tokens of Fable 5.1 and cost ~56% more per task on DevOps work; the broader reasoning-model pattern is that a model can be half the per-token price and spend 2–3× the tokens and time per finished job, so autonomous coding agents should expect output-token consumption to double or triple (Decrypt, "Impressive and Expensive"; token-economics reviews, as reported) — the concrete form of Altman's admission that token costs are "a huge issue" (owner-verified). Two engineering realities cut against the sticker. First, output tokens dominate the bill — reasoning and agentic loops generate far more output than input, and output is priced highest ($40/M, or $80/M in fast mode), so a model that "thinks more" moves the expensive number. Second, per-token cuts and per-task costs can move in opposite directions — a 20% price cut is erased by a 25% rise in tokens-per-task, and a more capable model that attempts harder, longer tasks can raise tokens-per-task far more than that. The only honest metric is cost per completed task at the effort you actually run.
  • The answer — change the unit of account, tier the effort, keep the books. Instrument cost-per-completed-task (not price-per-token) on your top workloads; route each task to the smallest model and lowest reasoning effort that clears its quality bar (a plain flame for plain work, the hungry high setting only where it earns it); cap and monitor tokens-per-task with alerts on drift; and capture model version, effort setting, token counts and cost in a producible record — reusing the logging and ownership disciplines you already built for the steward, the surety and the foundation, now pointed at the fuel line. This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now aimed at the number the vendor will never send you: what your own kitchen burned.

The strategic core: the cheaper, cleverer stove is a real gift you must collect without letting it quietly double your fuel bill — and you collect it only by changing your unit of account from the price of a token to the cost of a finished job. The market makes the temptation concrete: every invoice line shows a lower price, the benchmark charts show a better model, and the one number that rose — tokens burned per task — appears on no dashboard you were handed. The board translation: buy the cheaper log; weigh the woodpile per dinner. The failure mode is exact: a house that upgrades to the cleverer, cheaper-per-token model, leaves every workload on its highest reasoning setting, and reads only the per-token invoice will post a lower unit price and a higher total bill in the same quarter — and the first time anyone asks why, discover it has no producible record of which tasks ran at which effort and what each one cost.

How it lands on legacy estates
