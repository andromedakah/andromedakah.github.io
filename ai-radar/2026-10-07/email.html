The Lodger

Wednesday, 7 October 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

You were told to keep the larder — your data in a form you own. The catch was that using the frontier servant meant shipping your grain out every day. This morning the vendor answers it: its agent now runs inside your own house, and the data never leaves. Real progress — but a worker who lives in your walls and holds your keys is worse than a stranger at the gate the moment you stop watching. Welcome the cook; don't hand him every key.

1 · Executive Summary

For a year, using the frontier servant meant shipping your data out to the vendor's cloud. This morning the pattern inverts: instead of your grain going to the servant, the servant comes to your grain — which changes the shape of the risk, for the better in one way and for the worse in another.

1. The reality — the vendor's agent runs inside your own house; the data never leaves it. On 5 October 2026, at OpenAI's DevDay, AWS and OpenAI launched "Bedrock Managed Agents, powered by OpenAI" (public preview) — OpenAI agents run entirely inside your own AWS account, so that "data never leaves AWS"; the managed runtime handles inference, memory and skills inside your environment, and each agent has its own AWS identity, scoped permissions and full audit logs (The Next Web; AWS Weekly Roundup, 5 Oct; OpenAI, as reported). It rides GPT-6.1 Sol (29 Sep), near-flagship quality at ~1/5 the cost. The larder stays home.

2. The collision — a lodger is a different risk from a visitor. Safer on data: 72% of IT leaders now rank data sovereignty their top AI challenge, up from 49% a year ago (Info-Tech, as reported) — "data never leaves" is the direct answer. More dangerous on access if you relax: a resident holding your keys with the run of the house beats a stranger at the gate, and only ~11–25% of agentic pilots reach production, stalling on identity, audit and access-control gaps. The controls don't vanish; they move inside your walls.

3. The answer — bring the work to the data, keep the lodger in livery, don't rent your ground. Run the agent inside your boundary; give every resident agent its own identity, scoped keys and a complete log; and keep the abstraction, portability and a second hearth — because "runs in my cloud" is not "I own the ground."

Bottom line: the livery, steward, keyring, purse and foundation each governed a servant that worked at arm's length. The lodger is the morning the vendor stops asking for your grain and sends its cook to your kitchen instead. For the sovereignty worry 72% rank first, this is real progress — the grain stays home. But a worker who lives in your walls and holds your keys is the most dangerous servant you have the moment you decide that, because he lodges with you, he can be trusted with the run of the house. Welcome the cook; don't hand him every key; and don't mistake your landlord's floor for your own.

2 · Allegory — The Lodger

A great house had, at last, learned to keep its own larder — its grain and recipes the estate itself. But the finest cook in the valley did not work in its kitchen, so for a year it had carted its grain out through its own gates to his distant kitchen each morning, and hoped the bread came home with nothing left behind. This week the cook's master came with a different offer: "Keep every sack behind your own walls. I'll send my cook to live and work in your kitchen — not an ear of your grain will ever leave your land." And the house was glad, and rightly: three in four now lay awake more over where their grain slept than over almost anything. The larder kept with the great cook, not in spite of him.

But a wise steward felt a second thing stir: a man who lodges in your house is not the risk of a man you meet at your gate. The visitor you search, watch and send home at dusk. The lodger you stop searching, stop watching — and one evening you hand him the ring with every key, because surely a man who sleeps under your roof can be trusted with the strongroom. A stranger at the gate with no key can do little harm; a resident with the master key, whom you stopped watching because he is family now, can empty the house. So the careful houses took the cook in — gladly — and then treated him exactly as strictly as the stranger, and more: his own badge, cut in the house's livery; only the keys his baking needed, never the deed-box; and the steward's book open on him most of all. The master had, to his credit, handed back the livery, the keyring and the book — and asked them, politely, to actually use them.

And the wisest saw one last thing, watching the lodger knead their grain: the kitchen he cooked in was not, in fact, theirs. The walls, the hearth, the very floor were rented from a single great landlord. Keeping the grain home was real; but the house it never left was still another man's, and the rent could rise, the lease could change, the landlord could rearrange every tenant's kitchen at once. The foundation question had not gone away — it had only moved underfoot.

The question it forces: The vendor's agent now lives in our house and our data never leaves it — genuine progress, take it. But have we kept treating that resident as strictly as the stranger at the gate — its own identity, only the keys its task needs, every action in a log we own — or handed a lodger the run of the estate for sleeping under our roof? And have we remembered that the kitchen it cooks in is still rented?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Lodger

Every prior control governed a servant working at arm's length; the lodger is the morning it moves in — and the controls don't vanish, they relocate inside your house, where using them is now your job. "Data never leaves AWS" is a real answer to the larder worry that converts an external trust problem into an internal governance one. The reality: the data-gravity problem flips — you move the (now cheap) model to the data, and GPT-6.1 Sol's near-flagship quality at ~1/5 the cost makes running a strong agent inside your own account ordinary; each agent is a first-class principal with its own identity, scoped permissions and a log — the livery, keyring and steward, implemented. The collision: proximity erodes vigilance — a resident inherits your internal network, service accounts and trust, and a lodger with the master key is the worst principal you have; and the floor is still rented, deepening dependence on one cloud landlord (the foundation, underfoot). The answer: bring the work to the data (in-boundary), give every resident agent its own identity, least-privilege keys and a complete owned log, and keep the abstraction, portability and a second hearth.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Bring the work to the data. The servant now comes to the grain: OpenAI agents run inside your own AWS account, "data never leaves," and the cheap engine (GPT-6.1 Sol at ~1/5 the cost) makes it ordinary. Name your top few high-sensitivity AI workflows (regulated, PHI, payment, IP) and pilot running their agents inside your own boundary — confirming inference, memory and logs all stay in your account, not just the storage. Report the share of sensitive AI workloads where data provably never leaves your boundary.

2. Give the lodger a badge, scoped keys, and a place in the book. A resident agent is a first-class principal, and the in-boundary design hands you back the livery, keyring and steward. Require every in-house agent to have its own distinct identity, a least-privilege permission set scoped to its task (not broad standing access because it "runs inside"), and a complete audit log you own — and run an access review that treats resident agents as strictly as privileged humans. Report the count of any with broad standing access, as a defect list.

3. Don't mistake your landlord's floor for your own. Keeping the grain home is real progress, but the kitchen the lodger cooks in is rented from one cloud landlord — the foundation question, underfoot. Confirm that moving agents in-boundary hasn't single-sourced you: keep the abstraction between your systems and any one provider, keep your data, context and evals portable, and keep a second hearth you've actually lit. Report the measured time-to-switch for one in-boundary workflow to a second environment.
