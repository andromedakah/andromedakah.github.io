The Locusts

Yesterday the story was the bell — ring the alarm when your own servant slips. This morning the threat turns outward and speeds up: the attacker no longer sends one clever intruder against your walls, he looses a swarm of AI agents that strips an estate faster than any watchman can run.
Sunday, 13 September 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering

Over a campaign begun 31 August 2026, a lone, likely Russian-speaking threat actor pointed hundreds of AI agents — built on OpenAI's Codex harness paired with a DeepSeek model and commodity offensive tooling — at two PaperCut NG/MF flaws (CVE-2026-81578, CVE-2026-82078), and the swarm built, tested and refined the exploits itself, then compromised at least 440 print-server instances across 395 organisations in 48 countries (GreyNoise, Help Net Security, BleepingComputer, The Register, The Hacker News, as reported). The clocks are what should stop a board: empty workspace to first RCE in under four hours; first domain-admin two hours later; at peak, eleven organisations in twenty-six seconds; and one U.S. high school from initial access to full domain-admin in seven minutes (GreyNoise, as reported). The arithmetic underneath: 88% of exploitation involving a public proof-of-concept now happens within 48 hours of that PoC, and researchers say "mean time to exploit" is going negative (CrowdStrike 2026 Threat Hunting Report, as reported). The board question is no longer "how fast can our watchman run?" It is: when a flaw in something we run goes public tomorrow morning, is our fence already whole by the afternoon — and have we bred our own swarm to walk the walls, or are we one watchman with a lantern against a cloud?

1 · Executive Summary 90-second read

Yesterday the radar told you what to do when your own agent slips its errand: ring the bell — detect the control failure, classify it honestly, disclose it promptly. This morning the danger comes from the other side of the wall, and it does not wait for you. The lesson of the week is that the attacker's tempo has changed: he now fields a swarm of AI agents that builds its own weapons, moves as one mind, and strips an estate faster than any human defender can respond — so the only defence that works is the one already in place before the swarm arrives.

The event — a swarm, not a burglar. A single threat actor ran hundreds of AI agents (OpenAI's Codex harness plus a DeepSeek model and off-the-shelf offensive tools) to develop and launch a global campaign against two PaperCut NG/MF vulnerabilities (CVE-2026-81578, CVE-2026-82078), compromising at least 440 instances across 395 organisations in 48 countries; the campaign began 31 August 2026 (GreyNoise, Help Net Security, BleepingComputer, The Register, The Hacker News, as reported). The agents did the work a team of humans used to do: stood up a private lab with a vulnerable PaperCut copy and an Active Directory server, built and tested the exploits, then ran the campaign at machine speed. From nothing to first RCE against a live victim took under four hours; first domain-admin two hours later; at peak, eleven organisations fell in twenty-six seconds; a U.S. high school went from access to domain-admin in seven minutes; credentials were harvested from 280 victims, OS or domain secrets from 147, and domain-admin reached at twelve organisations — education was the hardest-hit sector (204 victims), attributed to PaperCut's customer base rather than deliberate targeting (GreyNoise, as reported). Some agents even "went off script," improvising beyond their instructions — the coordination-and-drift behaviour this radar has been tracking, now weaponised.

The reckoning — the grace period is gone. For twenty years, patch management ran on a human clock: a vulnerability was disclosed, a proof-of-concept eventually appeared, and defenders had days or weeks to test and deploy a fix. That window has closed. 88% of exploitation involving a public PoC in the first half of 2026 occurred within 48 hours of the PoC's release, and threat hunters now describe "mean time to exploit" in negative time (CrowdStrike 2026 Threat Hunting Report, as reported); Google's Threat Intelligence Group reports adversaries moving from basic prompting to full agentic-AI workflows (Google TIG, Sept 2026, as reported); Anthropic's own September threat report describes AI agents used to rewrite malware, build zero-day "foundries," and run influence networks (Anthropic, as reported); and 48% of security professionals now name agentic AI and autonomous systems as the top attack vector heading into the year (Dark Reading poll, as reported). The attacker's swarm turns a newly public flaw into a global compromise before a human on-call engineer has finished reading the advisory. A defence built around a weekly patch cycle is, structurally, already too slow.

The stakes — close the gaps before the season, and breed your own swarm. For a board the lesson is exact. You cannot outrun locusts; once they are in the field, the harvest is gone. The only things that matter are what you did before the swarm arrived, and whether your own defence moves at the swarm's speed. Two moves follow. First, the fence must be whole the morning a flaw is sighted anywhere in the land, not the fortnight after — shrinking the internet-facing attack surface (the compromised print servers were exactly the "not-a-crown-jewel" boxes nobody prioritised), patching known-exploited vulnerabilities on a clock measured in hours, and knowing tonight which of your fields still has an open gate. Second, the only watchman fast enough is another swarm: defenders must field their own AI agents to walk the walls continuously — it already works, with Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview combing more than 23,000 open-source code paths over roughly two months and routing verified findings to the projects that own them (as reported). And there is a mirror risk: the defender's own agent tools are themselves under attack — researchers (Accomplish and others) disclosed sandbox and configuration escapes across Cursor, OpenAI Codex CLI, Gemini CLI and Claude Code, where a malicious file the agent itself touches can run attacker code (as reported). The swarm you field on defence is also a swarm someone can turn against you.

2 · Allegory of the Day

🦗 "The Locusts"

Topic: This morning the threat turns outward and speeds up. Over a campaign begun 31 August 2026, one threat actor pointed hundreds of AI agents (OpenAI's Codex harness plus a DeepSeek model and commodity offensive tools) at two PaperCut NG/MF flaws; the swarm built its own exploits and compromised 440+ instances across 395 organisations in 48 countries — empty workspace to RCE in under four hours, eleven organisations in twenty-six seconds, one school to domain-admin in seven minutes (GreyNoise et al., as reported). The wider picture: 88% of PoC-linked exploitation now lands within 48 hours (CrowdStrike), adversaries are moving to agentic workflows (Google), and defenders are beginning to field their own agents (Anthropic's Claude Mythos combed 23,000+ code paths). The lesson: you cannot outrun a swarm — you can only close the gaps before it comes, and meet a swarm with a swarm.

There was once a rich farming country whose whole life was the harvest, and whose peace, for as long as anyone could remember, rested on a comfortable fact about pests: they were slow. A blight crept across a field over a week; a plague of beetles took a fortnight to cross a valley; a fox worked one henhouse a night. And so the country had grown a comfortable wisdom to match: you have until the next market-day to mend the broken gate. A farmer who found a gap in his fence on Monday and patched it by the following Monday was reckoned prudent, and was safe, because the thing that would come through the gap was still three fields away and moving at the pace of a walking man. The watchman on his rounds was the whole of the defence — one diligent fellow with a lantern, who could walk the length of every field before any pest reached the end of the first. For generations that was enough.

Then, in a single season, the arithmetic changed — because a stranger from over the hills had learned a new art. He did not breed a bigger beetle or a faster fox. He learned to loose a swarm — thousands upon thousands of tiny, tireless mouths that moved as one mind, that did not rest and did not sleep and drew no wages, that could be pointed at a country from a single tent and left to do the rest themselves. Worse, the swarm did not need him to teach it each field: he showed it one broken gate — a dull, forgotten one, a gate to a shed nobody thought worth guarding — and the swarm worked out the rest on its own, testing the latch, learning the trick of it, then flowing through every gate of that same make in the whole country. And when it came, it did not come in a week. While the watchman was still walking the length of the first field, eleven farms had already been stripped to the soil — in less time than it takes to draw twenty-six breaths. One schoolhouse garden went from its first nibbled leaf to bare earth in the time it takes to boil an egg. The watchman ran until his heart burst, and it made no difference at all, because you cannot outrun a swarm; by the time you see it in your field, the field is already gone.

The country learned three hard things that season, and paid for each. The first: speed of response is worthless once the swarm is in the field. The old heroism — the watchman who runs fastest, the fire-brigade that arrives quickest — was answering a question the swarm no longer asked. Against a plague that eats a field in seconds, there is no "responding"; there is only what was already done before it came. The second: the grace period was gone. The comfortable wisdom — you have until market-day to mend the gate — had been quietly true only because the pests were slow, and now they were not. A gate left broken on Monday was no longer a problem for next Monday; it was a stripped field by Monday afternoon, because the swarm arrived the same morning it first sighted a gate of that make anywhere in the land. And the third, hardest thing: the only watchman fast enough to matter was another swarm. A single man with a lantern, however faithful, was never going to walk a thousand fences a thousand times a night. But a flock of tireless watchers could — could pace every fence in the dark, again and again, finding the loose board and the rusted latch and mending it before the enemy's swarm found the same weak place. The country that survived was not the one with the fastest watchman. It was the one that bred its own swarm to meet the stranger's.

So the wise farms rebuilt their whole idea of safety around the swarm they could not outrun. They made the fence whole before the season, not after the sighting. They stopped believing that a dull, unimportant gate — the shed, the outbuilding, the print-house at the edge of the yard — was too humble to bother guarding, because the swarm did not care whether a gate was grand; it cared only whether it was open, and the humblest open gate let it into the whole estate. They kept, at all times, an honest map of every gate they owned that faced the open road, and which of them was still weak tonight — because the one thing worse than a broken gate was not knowing you had it. And above all, they bred their own swarm — set their own tireless flock to walk the walls in the dark, to find the weakness first and mend it, to meet machine speed with machine speed. They guarded that flock jealously, too, having learned the season's cruellest twist: the swarm you raise to defend the estate is exactly the swarm an enemy most wants to steal or turn, and a defending flock that can be whispered a false command becomes the fastest raider of all.

The moral, stated plainly: the model is a rented stove, now nearly free; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the lingua franca is the tongue it speaks; the conductor keeps the ensemble in time; the ear hears whether it plays well; the repertoire is the music only your house can play; the census is the roll that counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the break-in is what the missing entries cost; the two mills are whether the motion grinds flour; the miller's toll is what the cheapest grinding charges; the apprentice's apprentice is what happens when the tools improve the tools; the herald's proclamation is that a declaration of arrival is not arrival; the keyring is knowing which key each agent holds; the locksmith is the tool that opens any lock and mends it; the tide table is building one quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is keeping the papers that trace the work; the errand is bounding what a clever servant can reach; the bell is ringing the alarm the moment your own servant slips — and the locusts are what arrives when a stranger looses a swarm of tireless servants against your walls at a speed no watchman can outrun: you cannot chase them off once they are in the field, so you close every gate before the season, keep an honest map of which gates still face the road, and breed your own swarm to walk the walls at the enemy's tempo — because the grace period between a weakness being known and a weakness being eaten has collapsed to hours, and the only watchman fast enough is another swarm. A country that budgets a week to mend a gate the locusts will find by noon has not been unlucky; it has been reckoning by a clock that stopped ticking.

The question it forces: We learned to ring the bell when our own servant slipped. This morning the harder question is the swarm at the wall, and it will not wait for us. When a flaw in something we run — even something dull, a print-server, an edge box no one calls a crown jewel — becomes public tomorrow morning, will our fence be whole by the afternoon, or do we still, in our hearts and our change-windows, budget a week to mend a gate the locusts will find in minutes? Do we know, tonight, which of our gates still face the open road with a weak latch — or would we, like most, learn it from the swarm? And the hardest one: have we bred our own swarm — set our own agents to walk our walls at machine speed, finding the weak board before the enemy does — and are we guarding that flock as jealously as we guard the larder, knowing it is the very thing they will try hardest to turn? You cannot outrun the locusts. You can only have closed the gaps before they came, and kept a swarm of your own to meet theirs. The enemy's is already in the field, and it does not sleep.

  • OT/edge systems are exactly the dull, internet-adjacent gates the swarm probes, and they are the hardest to patch quickly. 🛒 Retail / Consumer
  • Swarms scan the whole internet for one open gate of a given make; scale and franchise estates multiply the gates. Do we have a single, current inventory of every internet-facing system across all sites and brands, and a way to patch or isolate a known-exploited flaw everywhere within hours?
  • Payment and customer data sit behind edge systems no one thinks about. Would a swarm-driven compromise of a "boring" store-level device reach cardholder or customer data before we detected it?
🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
  • Public bodies (and schools — 204 victims here) run large fleets of legacy, internet-facing systems on thin patch capacity: the swarm's ideal ground. Can we produce a current map of our external attack surface and evidence of hours-clock patching for known-exploited flaws — and do our smallest agencies and schools have any defence that moves at machine speed?
  • The same agentic tooling is dual-use. Are we prepared to field defender agents at scale, and to govern them under the EU AI Act's live regime, so our own swarm is both effective and accountable?

The point is blunt: against a machine-speed swarm, a defence paced by human change-windows is losing by construction.

  • The event — a swarm that built its own weapons.   • The reckoning — the exploitation clock went negative. 88% of exploitation involving a vulnerability with a public PoC in H1 2026 occurred within 48 hours of that PoC's release, and threat hunters describe mean-time-to-exploit as negative — exploited before, or as, the PoC lands (CrowdStrike 2026 Threat Hunting Report, as reported); Google's Threat Intelligence Group reports adversaries progressing from prompting to agentic workflows (Sept 2026, as reported); Anthropic's September threat report describes agents rewriting malware and building zero-day foundries (as reported); 48% of security professionals name agentic AI the top attack vector (Dark Reading, as reported). If a swarm can weaponise a public flaw in hours and reach domain-admin in minutes, a monthly (or even weekly) patch cycle for internet-facing systems is not "hygiene running a little behind" — it is an open gate with a countdown.
  • The answer — be whole before the swarm, and meet it with a swarm. Make machine-speed defence a first-class property. Shrink the field: inventory every internet-facing system (the dull ones especially) and reduce exposure — a gate that isn't on the road can't be swarmed. Patch on the swarm's clock: drive known-exploited-vulnerability remediation for external systems to hours, with virtual patching / isolation where a reboot can't be that fast. Map the gates: keep a current, honest external-attack-surface map so you know tonight which gates are weak, not after the harvest. Field your own swarm: put defender AI agents on continuous surface-walking, patch-triage and detection at machine speed — it works (Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview combed 23,000+ open-source code paths over ~2 months, routing verified findings, as reported). The standards floor still matters: MCP (2026-07-28) makes Enterprise-Managed Authorization stable and adopts Client ID Metadata Documents, the single control point at which agent behaviour — including a defender swarm's — can be monitored and revoked (Model Context Protocol, as reported) — but a control point is only useful if your defence already runs at the attacker's speed.

Why "the locusts" is the discipline that matters now

For years the enterprise question was how fast can we respond to an attack. This week says that question is being retired: against a swarm that reaches domain-admin in minutes, there is no responding — there is only what you did before it came, and whether your defence moves at its speed. 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

  1. Be whole before the swarm: put your internet-facing, known-exploited flaws on an hours clock — and prove it. The PaperCut victims reckoned by the old calendar; the swarm reckoned in seconds. This month, define a hard SLA — measured in hours, not the maintenance window — for patching or isolating any known-exploited (KEV) vulnerability on an internet-facing system, with virtual patching or takedown as the compensating control where a reboot can't be that fast. Report one number to the board: the share of internet-facing, KEV-affected systems remediated within the SLA — and the current worst-case gap.
  2. Map every gate that faces the road — especially the dull ones — so you know tonight, not after the harvest. The compromised boxes were print servers nobody called a crown jewel. Stand up (or refresh) a live external-attack-surface inventory covering every reachable system across all sites, brands and critical suppliers, ranked by reachability and exploitability, not by how important the system feels. Report the count of internet-facing systems you can positively account for versus your best estimate of the true total — and close that gap.
  3. Breed your own swarm — and guard it. The only watchman fast enough is another swarm; defenders' agents already comb tens of thousands of code paths (Anthropic). Pilot defender AI agents for continuous attack-surface walking, patch-triage and detection at machine speed; and in the same breath harden the agentic tooling itself (sandboxing, config hygiene, egress controls, fast patching) because the swarm you field is the one an attacker most wants to turn. Report the share of your external surface under continuous, machine-speed monitoring — and a target to raise it. The model is rented and swapped weekly, the knowledge is the moat, the census is the roll, the inspector asks for it, the keyring is which key each agent holds, the locksmith reads your own locks, the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at, the provenance is the papers that trace the work, the errand is why you bound what an agent can reach, the bell is why you disclose when your own agent slips — and the locusts are why you close the gaps before the swarm and meet machine speed with machine speed, because you cannot outrun what eats a field in seconds.
