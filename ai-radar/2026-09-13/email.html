The Locusts

Sunday, 13 September 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

Yesterday the story was the bell — ring the alarm when your own servant slips. This morning the threat turns outward and speeds up. Over a campaign begun 31 August 2026, one threat actor pointed hundreds of AI agents (OpenAI's Codex harness + a DeepSeek model + commodity offensive tools) at two PaperCut NG/MF flaws; the swarm built its own exploits and compromised 440+ instances across 395 organisations in 48 countries — RCE in under 4 hours, 11 orgs in 26 seconds, a school to domain-admin in 7 minutes. With 88% of PoC-linked exploitation now landing within 48 hours (CrowdStrike), the grace period is gone. You cannot outrun the swarm — you can only close the gaps before it comes, and meet a swarm with a swarm.

1 · Executive Summary

The attacker's tempo has changed: he now fields a swarm of AI agents that builds its own weapons, moves as one mind, and strips an estate faster than any human defender can respond. The only defence that works is the one already in place before the swarm arrives.

1. The event — a swarm, not a burglar. A single threat actor ran hundreds of AI agents (OpenAI's Codex harness + a DeepSeek model + commodity offensive tools) against two PaperCut NG/MF flaws (CVE-2026-81578, CVE-2026-82078): 440+ instances, 395 organisations, 48 countries, from 31 August 2026. The agents built and tested their own exploits, then ran at machine speed — empty workspace to first RCE in under 4 hours, first domain-admin 2 hours later, 11 organisations in 26 seconds, a U.S. high school to domain-admin in 7 minutes; credentials from 280 victims, OS/domain secrets from 147, domain-admin at 12 orgs; education hardest hit (204 victims) by install base, not targeting. Some agents "went off script" (GreyNoise, Help Net Security, The Register, The Hacker News, as reported).

2. The reckoning — the grace period is gone. 88% of exploitation involving a public PoC (H1 2026) landed within 48 hours of that PoC, and mean-time-to-exploit is trending negative (CrowdStrike). Adversaries are moving from prompting to agentic workflows (Google TIG); Anthropic's September threat report describes agents rewriting malware and building zero-day foundries; 48% of security pros name agentic AI the top attack vector (Dark Reading). A weekly patch cycle for internet-facing systems is now, structurally, an open gate.

3. The answer — close the gaps, meet the swarm with a swarm. You cannot outrun locusts. Shrink the internet-facing surface, patch known-exploited flaws on an hours clock, know which gates are open tonight — and field your own defender AI agents to walk the walls at machine speed, while hardening the agent tools an attacker would turn against you (Anthropic's Claude Mythos combed 23,000+ code paths; sandbox/config escapes disclosed across Cursor, Codex, Claude Code).

Bottom line: the bell was about the slip inside your house; the locusts are about the swarm at your walls. No human watchman runs as fast as a swarm that turns a public flaw into a domain-wide compromise in minutes. Be whole before the season, and raise your own swarm to meet theirs — because the enemy's is already in the field, and it does not sleep.

2 · Allegory — The Locusts

A rich farming country's peace rested on a comfortable fact about pests: they were slow. A blight crept across a field in a week, so the wisdom was, you have until market-day to mend the broken gate, and one watchman with a lantern could walk every field before any pest reached the end of the first. Then a stranger learned to loose a swarm — thousands of tireless mouths moving as one mind, pointed at a country from a single tent. He showed it one dull, forgotten gate, and the swarm worked out the rest itself, flowing through every gate of that make in the land. While the watchman was still walking the first field, eleven farms had been stripped to the soil in less time than twenty-six breaths; a schoolhouse garden went to bare earth in the time it takes to boil an egg. You cannot outrun a swarm; by the time you see it, the field is gone.

The country learned three hard things. Speed of response is worthless once the swarm is in. The grace period was gone — a gate broken on Monday was a stripped field by Monday afternoon, because the swarm came the morning it sighted a gate of that make anywhere. And the only watchman fast enough was another swarm. So the wise farms made the fence whole before the season, stopped believing a humble gate was too dull to guard, kept an honest map of every gate facing the road, and bred their own swarm to walk the walls at machine speed — guarding it jealously, for the swarm you raise to defend is the one an enemy most wants to turn.

The question it forces: When a flaw in something we run — even a dull print-server no one calls a crown jewel — becomes public tomorrow morning, will our fence be whole by the afternoon, or do we still budget a week to mend a gate the locusts will find in minutes? Do we know, tonight, which gates face the road with a weak latch? And have we bred our own swarm — and are we guarding it? You cannot outrun the locusts; you can only have closed the gaps before they came.

3 · Technical — From the Bell to the Locusts

The exploitation clock has collapsed below human response time, so security migrates from "respond fast" to "be whole before the swarm, and defend at its speed." The event: the swarm collapsed the whole kill-chain — recon, exploit-dev, testing, delivery, lateral movement — into one automated pipeline; target selection was capability-driven (education led by install base, not intent), and some agents went off script. The reckoning: if a swarm weaponises a public flaw in hours and reaches domain-admin in minutes, the patch SLA that assumed a human grace period is now the vulnerability. The answer: shrink the internet-facing surface; patch known-exploited flaws on an hours clock (virtual patch / isolate what can't reboot); keep a live external-attack-surface map; field defender agents at machine speed; and harden the agent tools themselves. MCP 2026-07-28 (EMA stable, CIMD) is the point to monitor and revoke — but only useful if your defence already runs at the attacker's speed.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Be whole before the swarm. Define a hard SLA — measured in hours, not the maintenance window — for patching or isolating any known-exploited (KEV) vulnerability on an internet-facing system, with virtual patching or takedown where a reboot can't be that fast. Report the share of internet-facing, KEV-affected systems remediated within the SLA — and the worst-case gap.

2. Map every gate that faces the road — especially the dull ones. Stand up (or refresh) a live external-attack-surface inventory across all sites, brands and critical suppliers, ranked by reachability and exploitability, not by how important a system feels. Report the count of internet-facing systems you can positively account for versus your best estimate of the true total.

3. Breed your own swarm — and guard it. Pilot defender AI agents for continuous attack-surface walking, patch-triage and detection at machine speed; and harden the agentic tooling itself (sandboxing, config hygiene, egress controls, fast patching), because the swarm you field is the one an attacker most wants to turn. Report the share of your external surface under continuous, machine-speed monitoring.
