The Threshold

A year of work made the servant excellent in the courtyard. This morning the anniversary asks the one question none of that answers: has any of it crossed the threshold into production? A year on from the EU's Apply AI Strategy, the research houses agree — adoption is near-universal, production is rare. Stop counting pilots. Count crossings.
Tuesday, 10 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering

On 17 November 2026 the European Commission convenes the Apply AI Summit in Brussels — a hybrid gathering (~900 in person, up to ~2,500 online) marking the first anniversary of the Apply AI Strategy and closing European AI Innovation Month (European Commission; BDVA, as reported). An anniversary is an audit whether you ask for one or not, and the one-year stock-take is brutally consistent. Deloitte's State of AI in the Enterprise 2026 (3,235 leaders, 24 countries) reports only ~25% of organizations have moved 40%+ of their AI pilots into production; Gartner still projects over 40% of agentic-AI projects canceled by end-2027 and only ~28% of use cases fully meeting ROI; McKinsey finds ~44% say AI is scaling yet only ~40% report measurable EBIT impact (as reported). Deloitte's Jim Rowan: "If 2025 was defined by 'pilots,' 2026 will be defined by 'workflows.'" The board question is no longer do we have agents? — the courtyard is full of them — it is: how many have crossed the threshold into real production work, and come home having earned their keep?

1 · Executive Summary 90-second read

For a long year the radar worked, step by step, on the servant in the courtyard — name it (the livery), keep one book (the steward), put your name on the bond (the surety), check its wage (the second wage), send it to reap (the two harvests), fund it (the purse), and keep the ground beneath it your own (the foundation). Every one of those disciplines is real, and necessary, and each improves the agent. This morning the calendar turns the question from how well the servant is drilled to whether it has ever actually crossed the threshold into the street. The occasion is an anniversary, and anniversaries audit you.

The occasion — a one-year anniversary that forces a stock-take. On 17 November 2026 the European Commission holds the Apply AI Summit in Brussels (hybrid; ~900 in person, up to ~2,500 online), marking one year since the Apply AI Strategy and closing European AI Innovation Month (European Commission; BDVA, as reported). It is a deliberate taking-stock — healthcare, energy, mobility, manufacturing, agrifood, robotics, media and public administration all on the agenda — and it lands at the exact moment the research houses publish their own year-one reckonings. The anniversary is the bell; the numbers are the count.

The reckoning — adoption is near-universal, production is rare, and the gap is the whole story. The year-one numbers point the same way from three independent directions. Deloitte (State of AI in the Enterprise 2026, 3,235 leaders, 24 countries) finds only ~25% of organizations have moved 40%+ of their AI pilots into production — and, tellingly, that worker access to sanctioned AI tools rose from under 40% to nearly 60% in a year while only ~23% report at least moderate use of agentic AI (with ~74% planning to within two years) (Deloitte; unite.ai; grcreport, as reported). Gartner still forecasts over 40% of agentic-AI projects canceled by end-2027, pegs the "real" agentic vendors at ~130 of thousands, and finds only ~28% of AI use cases fully met ROI (Gartner, 25 June 2025; secondary coverage, as reported). McKinsey finds ~44% say AI is scaling across the enterprise, yet only ~40% report measurable EBIT impact (McKinsey, as reported). A courtyard full of drilled servants; a street that is still mostly empty.

The stakes — the threshold is the one step the courtyard cannot take for you. Every control the radar built lives inside the courtyard: it makes the servant safe, named, bonded, funded and portable — but none of it is the same as sending it across the threshold into production, where the work is real, the load is unforgiving, and the ledger is honest. Deloitte's Rowan says the year turns from "pilots" to "workflows"; the data says the turn is mostly still a promise. The board move is to stop counting pilots and start counting crossings. First, name the threshold — a hard, written definition of "in production" (serving real load, owned by a named team, measured on a business metric), so a pilot can no longer masquerade as a deployment. Second, send a cohort across, not a fleet — pick a small number of use cases with a real P&L owner and a redesigned workflow, and drive them all the way over, because the cancellation rate punishes the house that launches fifty errands and finishes none. Third, weigh what comes home — tie each crossing to a measured ROI and EBIT line, so "we have agents" becomes "these three earn their keep, and here is the number." Do it now, on the anniversary, while the Summit's stock-take gives you cover to ask the honest question before the next budget cycle asks it for you.

2 · Allegory of the Day

🖋️ "The Threshold"

Topic: The anniversary turns the frame from drilling the servant in the courtyard to whether it has crossed into the street. On 17 November 2026 the European Commission convenes the Apply AI Summit in Brussels (hybrid; ~900 in person, up to ~2,500 online), marking one year of the Apply AI Strategy and closing European AI Innovation Month (European Commission, as reported) — a deliberate stock-take that lands alongside the research houses' year-one reckonings: Deloitte reports only ~25% of organizations have moved 40%+ of AI pilots into production (State of AI in the Enterprise 2026, 3,235 leaders, 24 countries); Gartner still projects over 40% of agentic-AI projects canceled by end-2027 and only ~28% of use cases fully meeting ROI; McKinsey finds ~44% scaling AI yet only ~40% reporting EBIT impact (all as reported). Deloitte's Jim Rowan: "If 2025 was defined by 'pilots,' 2026 will be defined by 'workflows.'" The threshold allegory is the radar's own illustration.

There was a great house that had spent a long and careful year making its servants excellent. It had named every hand and cut it a scoped key, hired a steward to keep one honest book, accepted that the master's own name stood surety for the work, learned to ask whose second coin each advising servant took, sent the governed servants into the fields to reap, handed each a scoped purse, and refused to let the merchant who rented it the cheap stove pour the foundation the whole house stood on. By any fair reckoning the courtyard was the best-run courtyard in the valley, and the master, walking its rows of drilled and liveried servants each morning, was proud — as he had every right to be.

And then the year turned, and with it came the day — a year to the morning since the master had stood in that same courtyard and declared that this would be the year the servants went out and did the real work of the town. On the anniversary of a vow, a man counts. So the master called for the auditor, expecting to be praised for his courtyard. But the auditor did not count the servants in the yard, nor test their drill, nor admire their livery. He asked one question, and only one: "How many of these have crossed the threshold — gone out the door, into the street, and come home with the day's work actually done?"

The master gestured proudly at the yard. "All of them are ready," he said. "Named, bonded, waged, funded. Look how they stand." The auditor nodded, and wrote nothing down for that, because readiness was not the question. He walked to the great door, set his back to the courtyard, and looked out at the street — and the street was nearly empty. Of all the splendid servants in the yard, only about one house in four had sent even a fair portion of its trained hands across the threshold to work for real. Of the errands that had been begun in the town, more than two in five would be called back and abandoned before they were done. And of the servants that had gone out, barely a quarter came home at dusk able to lay real coin on the table and say this is what I earned. The courtyard was magnificent. The street told the truth.

The steward, flustered, offered the house's motto for the year: "If last year was rehearsals, this year is work." It was a fine saying, and the master had paid to have it carved over the door. But the auditor only pointed at the carving, and then at the empty street, and said the quiet thing: "A rehearsal you repeat is still a rehearsal. The carving is a promise. The street is the proof. And the two do not yet agree." For the house had fallen, without meaning to, into the most comfortable error of a well-run courtyard: it had mistaken the perfecting of the servant for the sending of it. Every discipline of the long year — the livery, the steward, the surety, the wage, the harvests, the purse, the foundation — was real, and each had made the servant better. But not one of them was the act of crossing the threshold, and the house had spent so long making the servant ready to go that it had never quite made it go.

Two roads, as always. The easy road was to hold another drill, print the motto larger, and add a servant or two to the gleaming yard — to keep demonstrating readiness, because a demonstration is safe, and the street is not. The wiser road, which the careful houses took, began by admitting that readiness is not the work, and then doing three plain things. The first: name the threshold. Write down, in hard words, what it means for a servant to have crossed — serving the real street, under a named hand that answers for it, measured by what it actually brought home — so that no drill in the yard could ever again be counted as a crossing. The second: send a cohort, not a fleet. Do not fling fifty servants at the town and watch two in five errands collapse for want of a master who owns them; pick a few errands that matter, give each a hand that answers for its profit and a route redrawn for the real street, and walk them all the way over. And the third: weigh what comes home. Meet each returning servant at dusk and make it lay its coin on the table — a real number, against a real line in the house's books — so that "we have servants" becomes "these three earned their keep, and here is how much."

Here is the part that should keep a master awake. The courtyard is pleasant, and the drill is genuinely good, and there is always one more discipline to perfect before the servant is sent — one more key to scope, one more book to balance, one more fallback to light. A house can spend forever getting ready to cross, and call the getting-ready progress, because every step of it is defensible and none of it is the street. The anniversary is a mercy precisely because it is merciless: it does not ask how ready you are, it asks what you shipped. And the valley's own auditors had already published the count before the master's auditor arrived — one in four across, two in five errands abandoned, a quarter earning their keep — so there was nowhere to hide. The lesson was not that the courtyard was wasted. The courtyard was the foundation of everything. The lesson was that the courtyard is where the work begins, and the threshold is where it counts — and a house that never crosses has not been careful; it has been rehearsing.

The moral, stated plainly: the model is a rented stove; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising servant eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap and weigh what it brings in; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; the foundation is why you own the ground it stands on — and the threshold is why, having made your servants excellent in the courtyard, you must now walk them across the one line no discipline in the yard can cross for you: out of the rehearsal and into production, where the work is real and the ledger is honest. Name the threshold, send a cohort across, and weigh what comes home — because a servant perfected and never sent is a cost you have dressed up as a capability, and the anniversary will always, eventually, ask you to count the street.

The question it forces: A year on from the day we declared this the year of real work, how many of our agents have actually crossed the threshold into production — serving real load, owned by a named team, measured by what they earned — and how many are still, however beautifully, rehearsing in the courtyard? Have we named what "in production" means, sent a real cohort all the way across with a P&L owner and a redesigned workflow, and weighed what came home in EBIT — or are we among the three houses in four that have perfected the servant and never quite made it cross?

3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
  • How many of our AI use cases have actually crossed the threshold into production — and how do we define "crossed"? Deloitte finds only ~25% of organizations have moved 40%+ of their pilots into production, and Gartner expects over 40% of agentic projects to be canceled by end-2027. Can we produce, today, a written bar for "in production" (real load, named owner, measured on a business metric) and a count of exactly how many of our agents clear it — separating the deployments from the demonstrations?
  • Are we counting pilots or counting crossings? A year after most strategies were declared, ~44% say AI is "scaling" yet only ~40% report measurable EBIT impact (McKinsey), and only ~28% of use cases fully met ROI (Gartner). For our top ten AI initiatives, can each name a P&L owner and a measured number it brought home — or are we reporting activity (pilots launched) as if it were outcome (value earned)?
  • Did we mistake perfecting the servant for sending it? We have spent a year on governance, identity, funding and vendor strategy — all real, all necessary, all inside the courtyard. What is the single use case we have been "almost ready" to put into production for more than two quarters, and what, precisely, is the threshold-crossing step we keep deferring?
🏦 Financial Services
  • Your production bar is already the highest in the building — model risk, controls, auditability — which is exactly why pilots stall at the threshold. Have we pre-defined the production gate for AI (validation, controls, oversight) so a use case is engineered to cross it from day one, rather than built as a pilot and then stranded at the compliance wall?
  • Gartner's cancellation forecast is a capital-allocation signal, not just a tech one. Do we run AI initiatives like a portfolio with a kill/scale decision at a named milestone — concentrating spend on the few that can cross into production and earn EBIT — or are we funding a long tail of pilots that the 40% cancellation rate will eventually claim anyway?
🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
  • Crossing into clinical or regulated production is the hardest threshold there is, and it is where value actually lives. For each AI use case, have we drawn the full path from pilot to validated production — the clinical, safety and regulatory gates — and resourced the crossing, or are we accumulating promising pilots that can never, as built, reach a patient?
  • Deloitte notes agentic adoption is outpacing governance. Where we deploy agents into care or research workflows, is each one measured on a real outcome (time saved, error reduced, throughput) owned by a named clinical leader — so "we piloted an agent" becomes "this agent is in production and here is what it changed"?
🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
  • You already know the difference between a line that runs in the pilot cell and one that runs on the floor at volume. Have we applied that same pilot-to-production discipline to AI — a named "move to the floor" gate with an owner, a redesigned workflow and a measured yield number — or are our AI agents stuck in the equivalent of a permanent pilot cell?
  • The cancellation rate rewards focus. Are we driving a small cohort of AI use cases all the way into production on the plant floor, or spreading thin across many proofs-of-concept that will be abandoned before any reaches real throughput?
🛒 Retail / Consumer
  • The threshold for you is live customer traffic at peak. Which AI use cases are genuinely in production against real shopper load (search, support, merchandising, fraud) with a measured conversion or cost number — and which are demos that have never faced a real Saturday?
  • Workflow redesign is the crossing, not the model. For our highest-value AI use case, have we actually redrawn the end-to-end workflow around the agent and put it into production — or bolted a pilot onto an unchanged process, which is why the ROI has not shown up?
🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
  • The Apply AI Summit (17 Nov) marks a year of the EU strategy and asks, pointedly, what has moved from strategy to service. Can we show, for our own AI commitments, how many have crossed from pilot into a live public service with a named accountable owner — the public-sector version of the production threshold — rather than a portfolio of announcements?
  • Accountability and continuity raise the production bar, and rightly so. Have we defined the threshold a public-facing AI service must clear (oversight, transparency, EU AI Act duties, a fallback) and resourced the crossing — so that a citizen-facing agent is genuinely in production and answerable, not a pilot quietly serving real people without the controls a production service would carry?

The point is blunt: readiness is not production, demonstration is not value, and a governance program that perfects the servant without ever crossing the threshold has built a magnificent cost center and called it an AI strategy.

  • The reckoning — three independent measurements of the same threshold. Deloitte's State of AI in the Enterprise 2026 (3,235 leaders, 24 countries) reports only ~25% of organizations have moved 40%+ of AI pilots into production, that sanctioned-tool access rose from <40% to ~60% in a year, and that ~23% report at least moderate agentic use with ~74% planning to within two years; Gartner projects >40% of agentic-AI projects canceled by end-2027, pegs "real" agentic vendors at ~130 of thousands, and finds only ~28% of use cases fully met ROI; McKinsey finds ~44% scaling AI yet only ~40% reporting measurable EBIT impact (Deloitte; Gartner, 25 June 2025; McKinsey; secondary coverage, as reported). Two engineering realities sit under those numbers. First, the threshold is a systems problem, not a model problem. Pilots stall at production because the hard part is the crossing — real load, integration, workflow redesign, ownership, monitoring, the controls a demo never needed — and none of that is solved by a better model. Second, the cancellation rate is a concentration signal. More than two in five agentic projects dying by 2027 is what happens when a house launches a long tail of unowned proofs-of-concept; the survivors are the few with a P&L owner and a redesigned process. Spreading thin is how you feed the 40%.
  • The answer — name the threshold, send a cohort, weigh what comes home. Write a hard, testable definition of "in production" (serving real load, a named accountable owner, a business metric it is measured on, the production-grade controls it carries); pick a small cohort of high-value use cases and drive each all the way across with a redesigned end-to-end workflow and a real P&L owner; and tie every crossing to a measured ROI/EBIT line — reusing the identity, logging, ownership and portability disciplines you already built for the livery, the steward, the purse and the foundation, now aimed not at making the servant ready but at getting it across. This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now pointed at the sharpest axis of all: not whether the agent is governed, but whether it is actually working, in production, for a number you can show the board.

The strategic core: the courtyard disciplines are necessary and the radar built every one of them on purpose — but they are the cost of being allowed to cross the threshold, not the crossing itself, and a year of research now says most houses have paid the cost and not taken the step. The market makes the trap comfortable: perfecting the servant is safe, defensible and endlessly extensible (there is always one more control to add), while production is exposed, unforgiving and measured. So the well-run house keeps drilling. The board translation: stop counting pilots; start counting crossings. The failure mode is exact: a house that names, governs, funds, perfects and future-proofs its servants — and never drives a cohort across into measured production — will pass every governance review, headline every strategy deck, and discover at the anniversary that it is one of the three in four that has a magnificent courtyard and an empty street.

Why "the threshold" is the discipline that matters now

For a long year the worry was whether the servant was named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and built on your own ground. This morning the anniversary moves the question past all of that: not is the servant ready, but has it crossed. The Apply AI Summit's one-year stock-take and the Deloitte/Gartner/McKinsey reckonings prove the same thing from four directions — adoption is everywhere, production is rare, and the gap between them is where the value either appears or evaporates. Governance that stopped at readiness will headline every deck and move no EBIT. The estates that keep their standing will treat the courtyard as what it is — the necessary cost of admission — and spend this year's effort on the step it was always for: driving a measured cohort across the threshold into production.

How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now sharpened to be able to show what you shipped, and what it earned. On a legacy estate the instinct, after a hard year of governance work, is to keep hardening the courtyard: one more identity control, one more eval, one more vendor-risk review before anything is "allowed" into production. Each is defensible; together they are how a house spends a second year getting ready. The retrofit is name, send, weigh.
