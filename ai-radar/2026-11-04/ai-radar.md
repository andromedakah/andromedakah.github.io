# 🗓️ AI Tech Radar — The Free Well

**Wednesday, 4 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering**

> Six weeks ago the radar said the model had become a **rented stove** — cheap, plentiful, swappable — and told you to shop stoves ruthlessly and never let the stove-seller pour your foundation. This morning the first hard market data of the season grades the valley's homework, and the verdict is uncomfortable. On **3 November 2026 Menlo Ventures published "2026: The State of Enterprise AI"** (a survey of ~400 U.S. enterprise decision-makers, as reported), and the stove got cheaper still: the price of open-weight intelligence **fell ~3.3× to about $0.13 per million tokens**, and open-weight models now draw **19% of enterprise AI tokens, up from 11%** — a genuinely good, genuinely cheap well running right in the public square. And yet, over the same year, enterprises poured **more** of their coin than ever up one hill: **frontier models' share of enterprise AI spend rose from 35% to 63%**, their token share from 18% to 42%, while the **frontier labs' share of deployment spend grew from 50% to 69%**, and open-weight's share of *spend* sits at only **~4%** even at 19% of tokens (Menlo Ventures; FourWeekMBA, as reported). The middle hollowed out — smaller closed "workhorse" models **collapsed from 29% to 13% of tokens** — and one supplier now reportedly holds **~55% of enterprise API share, roughly 6× Google's** (as reported). The free well runs in the square, and the town queues at the paid fountain on the hill. The board question is no longer *is the cheap option real?* — it is, it's $0.13 and 19% of the water. It is: **while a free well runs in our square, are we pouring two-thirds of our AI bill — and climbing — up one hill because that water is genuinely worth it for the work that matters, or because we never once carried a test bucket from the well?**

## 1 · Executive Summary (90-second read)

Six weeks ago, in "The Foundation," the radar argued the model had commoditized into a cheap, swappable stove and told you to **own the floor, keep the larder, and light a second fire** — shop stoves ruthlessly, because the vendors were racing to become the ground you stand on. **This morning the season's first hard market data grades that homework, and it cuts both ways.**

**The stove got cheaper — the free well is real.** Menlo Ventures' **"2026: The State of Enterprise AI,"** published **3 November 2026** (≈400 U.S. enterprise decision-makers, as reported), confirms the commoditization thesis at the token layer: the average **open-weight token fell ~3.3× in price to about $0.13 per million**, and open-weight models' share of enterprise tokens **climbed from 11% to 19%** over the year to Q3 2026 (Menlo; FourWeekMBA, as reported). Capable, cheap, open-weight intelligence — DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi and their kin — is now a genuine public well, and Mistral shipped **Mistral Large 4 on 29 October 2026** into the same current (as reported). By the radar's own refrain, the water commoditized exactly as predicted.

**And yet the valley did not drink from the well — it poured more coin up the hill.** The same report finds that over the year to Q3 2026, **frontier models' share of enterprise AI *spend* rose from 35% to 63%**, their *token* share from 18% to 42%, and the **frontier labs' share of deployment spend grew from 50% to 69%** — while open-weight, at 19% of tokens, captured only about **4% of spend** (Menlo; FourWeekMBA, as reported). The middle path was abandoned: **smaller closed "workhorse" models collapsed from 29% to 13% of tokens.** Concentration sharpened rather than eased — one supplier reportedly holds **~55% of enterprise API share, ~6× Google's** (as reported). This is the "poured foundation" arithmetic of six weeks ago turning from a warning into a balance sheet: the valley found the cheap water and kept sending its money uphill.

**The stakes — make the spend a measured choice, not a habit.** None of this means the hill is wrong. Frontier models genuinely do the high-value, agentic, production-grade work that cheap open-weight often cannot — paying up for the tasks that earn it is good engineering, not weakness. The danger is the **unexamined** climb: pouring two-thirds of a growing bill up one hill, concentrating on a single lab, and never carrying a test bucket from the free well to find out which coins truly needed the hill. The board move re-uses the whole year's discipline, now pointed at the invoice. **First, meter the well against the hill** — know, per workload, which tokens genuinely need frontier quality and which the $0.13 well would serve as well. **Second, draw a real test bucket** — actually run an open-weight model on a live workload and measure the quality gap, because a 10–50× price spread demands you prove the gap is worth it rather than assume it. **Third, watch the concentration** — 63% of spend and 69% of deployment spend up one hill, one lab at ~55% of enterprise API, is a dependency you should be able to name, price, and reverse.

1. **The well is real and cheap.** **Open-weight tokens fell ~3.3× to ~$0.13/M and rose to 19% of enterprise tokens (from 11%); Mistral Large 4 shipped 29 Oct (Menlo Ventures "2026: The State of Enterprise AI," 3 Nov; FourWeekMBA, as reported).** The cheap stove the radar predicted is now a public well.
2. **The valley poured its coin up the hill anyway.** **Frontier models' share of spend rose 35%→63% and token share 18%→42%; frontier labs' deployment-spend share 50%→69%; open-weight is 19% of tokens but only ~4% of spend; mid-tier closed models collapsed 29%→13% of tokens; one lab ~55% of enterprise API, ~6× Google's (Menlo; FourWeekMBA, as reported).** The free well runs; the town queues on the hill.
3. **The answer — meter, test, and watch.** Meter the well against the hill per workload; draw a real test bucket (run open-weight on a live task and measure the gap against a 10–50× price spread); and watch the concentration so two-thirds of a growing bill up one hill is a choice you can defend and reverse, not a default.

**Bottom line:** the radar called the commoditization right — the water is cheap now, $0.13 and a fifth of all tokens — and the market's own scoreboard, published yesterday, shows the valley found the free well and kept its money flowing uphill: 63% of spend, 69% of deployment spend, one lab near 55% of the enterprise API, the mid-tier hollowed out. There is an honest reading of this and a dangerous one, and a board owes itself both. The honest reading: frontier quality is genuinely worth paying for on the work that matters, and the climb is a considered bet on reliability, capability and speed-to-value. The dangerous reading: the same climb is the "poured foundation" of six weeks ago arriving on the invoice — a growing bill concentrated on one hill, a free well no one carries a bucket from, a cheap alternative that lowers the industry's token price without ever lowering *your* bill. The estates that keep their standing will not swear off the hill; they will **meter the well against it** (route the routine, high-volume, low-stakes tokens to the cheap well and reserve the hill for the work that earns it), **draw a real test bucket** (prove the quality gap rather than assume it, against a 10–50× price spread), and **watch the concentration** (name it, price it, and keep a funded, exercised alternate so the climb stays a choice). The water commoditized. Whether that ever reaches your bottom line is entirely a question of whether you drink from the well or only admire it from the hill.

## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Free Well"

*Topic: This morning the season's first hard market data grades the commoditization thesis of six weeks ago. On 3 November 2026 Menlo Ventures published "2026: The State of Enterprise AI" (≈400 U.S. enterprise decision-makers, as reported): open-weight tokens fell ~3.3× to about $0.13 per million and rose to 19% of enterprise tokens (from 11%) — the cheap "well" is real — yet over the same year frontier models' share of enterprise spend rose from 35% to 63%, token share from 18% to 42%, frontier labs' deployment-spend share from 50% to 69%, with open-weight at only ~4% of spend, mid-tier closed models collapsing from 29% to 13% of tokens, and one supplier reportedly at ~55% of enterprise API share, ~6× Google's (Menlo Ventures; FourWeekMBA, as reported). The free-well allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There was a town that had learned, over a hard and clever year, to be careful about its water. It had named every water-carrier and cut each a scoped key, kept one honest ledger of every barrel drawn, put the mayor's own name on the bond for the carriers' work, and — most prized of all — it had stopped marrying a single well. Water, the town had decided, was water; you did not wed a spring, you *rented* your supply, and when a cheaper source appeared you switched to it before supper. The town was proud of this thrift, and it had every right to be.

And this very season the thing the town had bet on came true. A **free well** opened in the public square — good water, clean enough for most of the day's work, and so cheap it was practically a gift: a few coppers where the old sources had charged silver. By autumn the town was drawing nearly a fifth of all its water from that free well, and the carriers reported the water was fine. The old prophecy had come to pass: the stove had become a commodity, the spring had become a well, and the well ran in the square for almost nothing. This was the town winning the game it had set out to win.

But then the season's accounts came in — a careful tallier walked the town and counted not the buckets drawn but the **coin spent** — and the ledger told a story the carriers' happy reports had hidden. For all the free water in the square, the town was sending *more* of its money than ever before up the long road to the **private waterworks on the hill**. A year ago, half the town's water-coin went up that hill; now it was better than two-thirds, and climbing. The free well, which drew a fifth of all the buckets, took barely a twentieth of the coin. And the comfortable middle the town had once relied on — the decent, mid-priced sources between the free well and the grand hill — had all but dried up, abandoned by a town that now drew either the cheapest water or the dearest and almost nothing in between. Worse: of all the silver that climbed the hill, more than half flowed to a single waterworks, whose pipes now ran under half the houses in town.

Now here is the part that requires an honest man to hold two thoughts at once, for this is not a simple fable about foolishness. The hill-water was, for the work that mattered, genuinely better — clearer, more reliable, drawn for the delicate tasks where a bad bucket cost more than the water saved. A wise town *should* pay up the hill for the work that earns it; to drink only the cheapest water for every purpose would be its own kind of folly. So the climb was not, in itself, a mistake. **The mistake was climbing without ever asking which buckets needed the hill.** The town had found the free well, admired it, told itself it was thrifty — and then, out of habit and the carriers' assurance that the hill-water was simply *better*, poured two-thirds of its growing bill up the road without once carrying a test bucket from the well to a real task to see whether the well would have served.

Two roads, as always. The easy road was to keep climbing — the hill-water was excellent, the carriers trusted it, and who had time to test a free well against a trusted spring? The wiser road, which the careful houses took, began with a cold accounting asked not of the buckets but of the coin: *not "is the free well real?" — it plainly is — but "which of our coins truly needed the hill, and which did we send uphill from habit?"* And when the careful houses metered honestly — matching each day's task to the cheapest water that would actually do it — they found that a good share of what they had been paying hill-prices for was ordinary work the free well would have served for a fraction, and that the hill was worth every silver piece on the delicate tasks and a quiet waste on the rest. The climb, rightly sized, cost them far less and served them just as well.

So the wise houses learned three plain things, and they cost less to do early than late. **The first: meter the well against the hill.** For each kind of work, know which water it truly needs — reserve the dear hill-water for the delicate, high-value, production-grade tasks that earn it, and send the ordinary, high-volume, low-stakes drawing to the free well in the square. A town that pours every bucket up the hill is not prudent; it is merely incurious about its own bill. **The second: draw a real test bucket.** Not a rumour about the free well, not the carriers' word that the hill is better — an actual bucket, drawn from the well and carried to a real task, measured against what the hill would have done, because when the hill costs ten to fifty times the well, the burden is on the hill to prove the gap is worth it, task by task. **And the third: watch the hill.** When two-thirds of your coin climbs one road, and more than half of *that* flows to a single waterworks whose pipes run under half your houses, you have a dependency whether you named it or not; map it, price it, and keep a funded, exercised path to the well, so the climb stays a choice you can defend and, if need be, reverse.

Here is the part that should keep a mayor awake. The free well in the square was loud, cheap, and genuinely wonderful — and all that cheerful water was, in part, a flattering distraction. **While the town congratulated itself on drinking a fifth of its water for coppers, it was quietly handing two-thirds of its silver, and rising, to the hill it had never thought to test an alternative to** — and the waterworks on the hill knew it, which is why the free well never troubled their accounts at all. The commodity water had lowered the *market's* price of water without lowering *this town's* bill by a single piece, because a cheap well you admire but do not drink from is not a saving; it is a saving someone else is enjoying while you pay full price up the hill. The lesson was not that the hill was wicked, nor that the well was a trick. The lesson was that **a cheap option you never actually route your work to is not thrift — it is thrift you talked yourself out of, one trusted bucket at a time.**

**The moral, stated plainly:** the free well is the commoditized open-weight model — real, cheap, a fifth of all the water, and running right in your square; the hill is the frontier, genuinely worth its price for the work that earns it and a quiet waste for the work that does not; the coin is your bill, and the bill is what commoditization was supposed to lower. The stove is why the model is swappable; the foundation is why you own the ground; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks for the roll; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings when your own servant slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the mayor's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising servant eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; the foundation is why you own the ground it stands on — and **the free well is why, having been handed cheap water in your own square, you must still meter the well against the hill, draw a real test bucket before you trust the carriers' word, and watch where your coin actually flows, so that the water which commoditized for the whole valley finally reaches *your* bottom line instead of merely widening the toll-keeper's.** A town that drinks a fifth of its water for coppers and congratulates itself while pouring two-thirds of a growing bill up one untested hill has not been thrifty; it has rented its own applause.

**The question it forces:** *The free well is real — a fifth of our tokens at a tenth the price, exactly the commoditization we predicted. So why is two-thirds of our AI bill, and climbing, going up one hill — and can we show, task by task, which of those coins the hill genuinely earned and which we sent uphill from habit, having never once carried a test bucket from the well? Have we metered the well against the hill, drawn a real bucket to prove the quality gap against a 10–50× price spread, and named the concentration that now runs more than half our spend through a single lab — or have we let a cheap well we only admire convince us we were being thrifty while the invoice climbed?*

## 3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

### 🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
- **Is our cheap option a saving we actually bank, or one we only admire?** Open-weight tokens fell ~3.3× to ~$0.13/M and are 19% of enterprise tokens, yet only ~4% of spend, while frontier took 63% of spend (Menlo, 3 Nov, as reported). **Can we show, per major workload, which tokens genuinely need frontier quality and which we route to the hill out of habit — and what our bill would be if the routine, high-volume work ran on the well?**
- **Can we name and price our concentration?** Frontier labs now take 69% of deployment spend and one supplier reportedly holds ~55% of enterprise API share, ~6× the next (as reported). **If that single provider raised prices, deprecated a model, or had an outage, what share of our AI spend and which production workflows are exposed — and do we have a funded, exercised path to a second source, or only a line item that says "we could"?**
- **Have we drawn a real test bucket this quarter?** **For at least one live workload, have we actually run a capable open-weight model (DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi, Mistral Large 4, or similar) against our frontier incumbent and measured the quality gap — or are we assuming the gap from the vendor's word, against a 10–50× price spread we have never tested?**

### 🏦 Financial Services
- The mid-tier hollowed out — smaller closed "workhorse" models fell from 29% to 13% of tokens as spend barbelled to the cheapest and the dearest (Menlo, as reported). **Have we made that barbell a deliberate routing policy — cheap open-weight for high-volume, low-stakes work behind strong controls, frontier for the regulated, high-value decisions — or did our spend drift to the frontier by default, and can we evidence the choice to a supervisor?**
- Concentration risk is a supervised discipline in your world, and AI spend now shows it: 63% of spend and 69% of deployment spend up the frontier, ~55% through one lab (as reported). **Can we produce a concentration-risk map of our AI stack with a tested exit for each single point of failure, the way we would for any critical outsourced dependency?**

### 🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
- Clinical and safety work genuinely earns frontier quality, and paying up for it is prudent, not wasteful. **But have we separated the tasks that truly need the hill (diagnosis support, regulated documentation, patient-facing reasoning) from the ordinary, high-volume drawing (summarization, coding, internal retrieval) that a validated open-weight model would serve for a fraction — and are we paying hill-prices for well-work by default?**
- An open-weight model you can run inside your own compliance boundary is also a data-sovereignty option, not just a cheaper one. **Have we evaluated whether running a capable open-weight model on our own infrastructure would both cut cost and keep protected health data in our own larder, versus routing every token to an external frontier API?**

### 🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
- You already run dual-sourcing and make/buy discipline for physical inputs; AI tokens are now a high-volume input with a 10–50× price spread between sources. **Have we applied the same make/buy and second-source rigor to model spend — a qualified, live open-weight alternate for the routine load — or is our token supply single-sourced to the frontier in a way we would never tolerate for a critical component?**
- High-volume, repetitive inference (inspection notes, maintenance logs, routine classification) is exactly the well-water case. **What share of our token volume is low-stakes and repetitive, and what would it cost on open-weight at ~$0.13/M versus our current frontier bill?**

### 🛒 Retail / Consumer
- Your margins live and die on cost-per-interaction, and the well is now genuinely cheap. **Are we routing high-volume, low-stakes traffic (product Q&A, search, routine support) to the cheap well behind an abstraction we own, and reserving the frontier for the conversions and escalations that earn it — or are we paying frontier prices for every interaction and watching the commoditization dividend accrue to someone else?**
- Peak-season economics and reliability both matter. **If our primary frontier provider had an outage or a price step on our biggest sales day, does traffic degrade gracefully to a live open-weight fallback, or does the whole experience stop — and have we actually run that failover under load?**

### 🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
- Public money and continuity of service make an untested, concentrated AI bill a governance question, not just a cost one. **Can we show that our AI spend is matched to need — cheap open-weight for routine work, frontier only where it is justified — and that no critical public service is single-sourced to one provider with no tested exit, under the duties we already owe (EU AI Act, GPAI enforcement live since 2 August 2026)?**
- Sovereignty and long horizons matter most here, and open-weight models are a sovereignty lever. **Are we evaluating capable open-weight models we can run on our own ground for services citizens will still need in a decade — or are we pouring a growing, concentrated bill up a single external hill by default?**

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Foundation to the Free Well

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking *is this agent named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and standing on ground I own?* and started asking the one question the season's first hard data just forced onto the table: *the cheap water arrived exactly as we predicted — so why has it not lowered our bill?* The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the keyring is which key it holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what it can reach; the bell discloses when it slips; the livery gives it a scoped identity; the steward keeps one office; the surety puts your name on the bond; the second wage asks whose interest it serves; the two harvests sends it to reap; the purse funds it; the foundation keeps the ground yours. **Every one of those is a control. The free well is a *spending* discipline — and this week the market produced the first hard evidence that the commoditization the radar kept promising is real at the token layer and absent at the invoice.** The point is blunt: **the price of intelligence fell for the whole industry, and most enterprises did not capture a cent of it, because a cheap option you admire but never route your work to is a saving someone else banks while you pay full price up the hill.**

- **The well is real — open-weight is cheap and widely drawn.** **Menlo Ventures' "2026: The State of Enterprise AI" (3 November 2026, ≈400 U.S. enterprise decision-makers) reports the average open-weight token fell ~3.3× in price to about $0.13 per million and open-weight's share of enterprise tokens rose from 11% to 19% over the year to Q3 2026, with Chinese open-weight models (DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi) leading the pack and Mistral Large 4 shipping 29 October 2026 (Menlo; FourWeekMBA; as reported).** Architecturally, three things follow. First, **capable open-weight is now a genuine production option for a large class of work**, not a hobbyist's toy — at $0.13/M the price of being wrong on a routine token is near zero. Second, **capturing that requires a routing layer you own** — the dividend is real only if your systems can send each task to the cheapest model that will actually do it, which is impossible if your work is wired directly to one frontier API. Third, **"cheaper for the industry" is not "cheaper for you"** — the token price fell everywhere, but the saving lands only in the bills of enterprises that actually route work to the well.
- **And yet the invoice climbed — spend concentrated on the frontier.** **The same report finds frontier models' share of enterprise AI *spend* rose from 35% to 63%, their *token* share from 18% to 42%, and frontier labs' share of deployment spend grew from 50% to 69%, while open-weight captured only ~4% of spend at 19% of tokens; smaller closed "workhorse" models collapsed from 29% to 13% of tokens, and one supplier reportedly holds ~55% of enterprise API share, ~6× Google's (Menlo; FourWeekMBA; as reported).** Two engineering realities cut against comfort. **First, the spend barbelled and the middle died.** Work split to the cheapest water (open-weight, high-volume, low-stakes) and the dearest (frontier, high-value, agentic), and the mid-priced closed models in between were abandoned — which means the real decision is now binary and explicit (well or hill), and a default of "frontier for everything" is a decision you are making whether you meant to or not. **Second, the frontier premium is often genuinely earned — but only sometimes.** Frontier models do the production-grade, long-horizon, agentic work that cheap open-weight still fumbles; paying up there is correct. The waste is paying the same premium for the ordinary, high-volume drawing the well would serve, and the only way to tell the two apart is to measure, not assume.
- **The answer — meter, test, and watch.** **Put a routing layer you own between your work and the models (route each task to the cheapest model that passes its quality bar); draw a real test bucket (run a capable open-weight model on a live workload and measure the quality gap against the 10–50× price spread before you accept the frontier premium); and watch the concentration (name and price your exposure to the 63%/69% frontier spend and the ~55% single-lab API share, and keep a funded, exercised alternate) — reusing the abstraction, portability and second-source disciplines you already built for the foundation, now pointed at the invoice.** This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — *be able to account for, and act on, what you run* — now aimed at the sharpest line on the balance sheet: not whether the model is cheap in the market, but whether its cheapness ever reaches your own bottom line.

The strategic core: **the commoditization the radar predicted is real — the water is $0.13 and a fifth of all tokens — and the market's own scoreboard shows most enterprises captured none of it, pouring two-thirds of a growing bill up one hill while a free well ran in the square.** The temptation is subtle and respectable: frontier quality is genuinely better, the carriers vouch for it, and testing a free well against a trusted source feels like make-work — so the spend drifts to the hill by default and the dividend accrues to someone else. The board translation: **pay up the hill for the work that earns it, drink from the well for the work that doesn't, and prove which is which.** The failure mode is exact: **a house that governs, funds and perfects its agents and owns its foundation — and then routes every token to one frontier API because the quality is "obviously" better, without ever metering the well against the hill — will pass every governance review and discover, when the season's accounts come in, that the cheapest intelligence in history never once lowered its bill.**

### Why "the free well" is the discipline that matters now

For a long year the worry was whether the servant was named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and on ground you own. **This morning the market produced the first hard number on whether any of that lowered the bill — and the answer, for most, is no.** The open-weight token fell to $0.13 and took a fifth of all volume (the well is real), yet frontier spend climbed to 63% and one lab to ~55% of the enterprise API (the coin went up the hill). Commoditization that never reaches your invoice is not a saving you earned; it is a saving you watched someone else take. The estates that keep their standing will not swear off the frontier — it earns its price on the work that matters — but they will refuse to pour a growing, concentrated bill up one hill without ever metering the well against it.

### How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — **be able to account for, and act on, what you run** — now sharpened to *be able to account for, and act on, what you spend.* On a legacy estate the instinct, after a hard year of governance and a trusted frontier relationship, is to send every new workload to the same incumbent API — it is easier, it is "obviously" the best quality, and the carriers vouch for it. Under the free well that is exactly the reflex to check: default-to-frontier is how a 10–50× price spread quietly becomes your largest and most concentrated AI line item.
