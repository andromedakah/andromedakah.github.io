## 1 · Executive Summary (90-second read)

All week the radar built the governed house: name every agent (the livery), keep one book (the steward), put your own name on the bond (the surety), ask whose wage each draws (the second wage). Necessary, all of it. But this morning the freshest enterprise data lands a plainer verdict from underneath: **a governed hall is not a full barn.** The divide now separating winners from the rest is not who *has* agents, nor whose agents are best-governed — it is **who has actually put them to work executing real tasks, and that gap is widening fast.**

**The reality — the divide is crystallizing, and it is about execution, not adoption.** OpenAI's enterprise telemetry ("Enterprise Signals" / "From assistance to execution") reports that **frontier firms — the top 10% of AI users each month — now generate 8.3× as many output tokens per active user as typical firms, up from 2.6× in January**: the gap has roughly tripled in about eight months. And the character of the usage has changed — **as of June, 64% of combined Codex-plus-ChatGPT output tokens among enterprise customers are agentic (Codex), not chat** — the shift the company names **"from assistance to execution."** The advanced work is spreading beyond engineering: since February, weekly active enterprise Codex users grew **108× in legal, 41× in sales, 41× in recruiting and 26× in marketing, against 5× in engineering**, and **21% of frontier-firm users touch Plugins each week versus 9% at typical firms** (OpenAI, as reported). The leaders are not merely chatting more; they are delegating more work.

**The collision — most houses are still keeping a tidier hall, not reaping.** McKinsey's 2026 State of AI (1,719 leaders across 97 countries) describes **a two-speed enterprise-AI race**: among firms above **$1B in revenue, 40% are now scaling AI agents, up from 27% a year earlier**, while at **smaller firms the figure is 22% — and did not move**. Only about **6% of enterprises are "AI high performers" attributing more than 5% of EBIT to AI** (McKinsey, as reported). Independent surveys agree the middle is stuck: **79% of organizations report challenges scaling AI despite heavy investment** and **only ~18% say they have fully integrated it** (Writer, 2026, as reported). The picture is not one of universal progress; it is a small cohort pulling away while the majority buys tools, governs them well, and leaves them in the hall.

**The stakes — measure the grain, move from assistance to execution, and close the gap before it compounds.** For a board the move is exact. **The barn, not the hall, is the scoreboard: output executed, not agents adopted or governed.** Three things follow. First, **measure the harvest** — track work actually completed by agents (tasks closed, cycle time, tokens spent on execution versus chat), not licenses bought or a governance checklist ticked. Second, **move from assistance to execution** — pick two or three high-volume workflows and delegate the whole task to a governed agent, not just the drafting; the frontier firms' lead comes from execution tokens, not chat tokens. Third, **close the gap now, because it compounds** — a 2.6×-to-8.3× move in eight months is not a plateau you can catch at leisure; the working house learns the fields while you tidy the hall, so a lead you ignore this quarter is dearer to close next quarter.

## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Two Harvests"

*Topic: This morning the frame turns from how well the house is governed to how much it actually reaps. OpenAI's enterprise telemetry reports frontier firms (top 10% of AI users) now generate 8.3× as many output tokens per active user as typical firms, up from 2.6× in January, with 64% of enterprise output tokens now agentic (Codex) rather than chat — "from assistance to execution" (OpenAI Enterprise Signals, as reported). McKinsey's 2026 State of AI (1,719 leaders) calls it a two-speed race: 40% of >$1B firms scaling agents (up from 27%) versus 22% and flat at smaller firms (McKinsey, as reported). The two-harvests allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There were, in the same valley, two great houses that had done everything right. Each had, over a long and careful year, given every hand a name and a scoped key, hired a steward to keep one honest book, accepted from the crown that the master's own name stood surety for all the servants did, and even learned to follow a clever counsellor to the market to see whose coin he took. If you had walked either hall you would have called it well run. The servants were named, the keys were cut to their errands, the book was whole, the bond was signed. And the two masters, who were neighbours and rivals, each believed himself as ready for the age as a man could be.

But at reaping the two barns did not match, and the gap was not small. For the first master, having governed his hall so beautifully, had come to treat the governing as the work. His named, scoped, loyal servants stood about the spotless hall all day — fetching a paper when asked, offering a word of advice when addressed, tidying what was already tidy — and he took great satisfaction in how orderly they were. The second master had done every governing thing his neighbour had, and then, at dawn, done the one thing his neighbour had not: **he sent the same governed servants out into the fields to actually bring in the crop.** Not to advise on the harvest. To harvest. And when the wagons came in, the second barn held eight loads to the first barn's one — though the servants, the wages, the keys and the book were, hand for hand, the same. **The difference was not the quality of the servants nor the tidiness of the hall. It was that one house had set its servants to reap and the other had set them to stand.**

Here is the part that should keep a master awake. When the two houses had started, one spring, the working barn held only two-and-a-half loads to the idle house's one — a lead, but a lead a diligent man tells himself he can close next season. By this autumn it was eight-to-one, and still widening. **For the working house was learning the fields.** Every day its servants reaped, the house learned which corners ripened first, which hands were best at which rows, how to send a dozen into the far acre at once and bring them home fed and counted; the harvest taught the house to harvest better, and next year it would reap more still. The idle house, meanwhile, had learned only to keep an ever-tidier hall. The gap between them was not a fixed distance the laggard could stroll across at leisure; **it was a compounding interest, paid to the house that worked and charged to the house that admired its own good order** — and the longer the idle master waited, telling himself his hall was so well governed that the harvest would surely follow, the dearer the crossing became.

So the wise houses of the valley, once they had seen the two barns side by side, stopped congratulating themselves on the tidiness of the hall and started asking the only question a barn can answer. **The first thing they learned: a governed hall is not a full barn.** Naming the servant, keeping the book, signing the bond and checking the wage are the price of admission, not the harvest; a house can do all four flawlessly and still starve beside a neighbour who did the same four and then went out to reap. **The second: measure the grain, not the servants.** They stopped counting how many hands they had hired and how orderly the hall looked, and started weighing what actually came into the barn — for a hall full of idle, well-governed servants weighs the same at reaping as an empty one. **And the third: the gap compounds, so cross it now.** They knew that a lead built on learning the fields does not wait politely for the laggard to catch up; it widens every season, so the cheapest morning to send your servants into the fields is always this one, and the most expensive is the one after you finally admit the neighbour's barn is full and yours is not.

**The moral, stated plainly:** the model is a rented stove, now nearly free; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent at the door; the census is the roll that counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand is bounding what a clever servant can reach; the bell is ringing the alarm the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you give every hand its own name and key; the steward is why you keep one governed office and its book your own; the surety is why the master's own name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising servant eats — and **the two harvests is why, having done all of that, you must still walk out to the barn and weigh what you actually brought in, because a governed hall of idle servants weighs nothing at reaping, the estates pulling away are simply the ones that set the same named, scoped, loyal servants to real work instead of to standing, and the gap between the working house and the admiring one is not a fixed distance but a compounding interest that has already tripled in a single season and grows dearer to cross with every harvest you spend keeping the hall tidy.** A house that governs its servants immaculately and never sends them to reap has not prepared for the age; it has built a beautiful, orderly, well-audited way to fall behind.

**The question it forces:** *We named every agent, kept one book, signed the bond and checked the wage — and all of it was right. But this morning the barns are being weighed. Have we actually sent our governed servants into the fields to execute whole tasks, or are they standing about a very tidy hall fetching and advising? Do we grade ourselves on grain in the barn — work our agents actually completed — or on servants hired and a governance checklist ticked? And do we understand that the lead the working houses hold over us is not a fixed distance we can close at leisure, but a compounding interest that tripled in eight months while we admired our own good order — so that the cheapest morning to walk out to the fields is this one?*

## 3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

### 🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
- **Are we reaping or just standing?** OpenAI's telemetry shows frontier firms generate 8.3× the output tokens of typical firms (up from 2.6× in January), and 64% of enterprise tokens are now agentic execution, not chat. **For our top three workflows, have we delegated the whole task to a governed agent, or are we still using AI only to assist a human who does the work — and can we point to grain in the barn (tasks executed end-to-end) rather than seats bought?**
- **What is our scoreboard?** Most estates grade themselves on adoption and governance maturity. **Do we measure work actually executed by agents — tasks closed, cycle time cut, execution tokens vs chat tokens — or do we report license counts and a compliance checklist and call it progress?**
- **Do we understand the gap compounds?** The divide tripled (2.6× → 8.3×) in about eight months because leaders learn execution while others tidy the hall. **If we are behind, do we treat closing the gap as urgent capital-allocation now, or as something a well-governed house can catch up on later — and have we priced how much dearer the crossing gets each quarter?**

### 🏦 Financial Services
- Codex-style agentic usage is spreading fastest into legal, sales and operations — exactly the high-volume, rules-bound work banks and insurers run at scale. **Which of our reconciliation, underwriting-support, KYC/AML review and client-servicing workflows have we actually delegated to a governed agent end-to-end, and what measurable throughput did we gain — not "we deployed a copilot"?**
- The two-speed race maps onto scale: >$1B firms are scaling agents at 40%, smaller ones at 22% and flat. **Are we using our scale advantage to build a compounding execution lead, or spending it on the best-governed hall of idle assistants in the sector?**

### 🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
- Prior-authorization, coding, documentation and evidence-review are enormous, repeatable workloads where execution — not assistance — is the prize. **Have we moved any of these from "AI drafts, human does everything else" to "governed agent executes, human approves," and can we show the harvest (throughput, turnaround) with the same governance we already built?**
- Adoption gaps here are safety-and-trust bound, not just ROI-bound. **Where we are deliberately keeping agents in an assisting role for safety, is that a considered clinical choice we can defend — or is it just an ungoverned hall we never dared send to the fields?**

### 🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
- Planning, procurement, quality documentation and maintenance workflows are ripe for delegated execution across a long OT/IT chain. **Which whole workflows have we handed to a governed agent to run, not just advise on — and are we measuring units of work executed, or counting pilots?**
- The compounding-lead dynamic is brutal in operations, where learning-by-doing is everything. **Are our agents accumulating operational learning by actually executing, or are competitors' agents out-reaping ours while ours stay in the control room offering suggestions?**

### 🛒 Retail / Consumer
- Merchandising, customer service, returns, catalog and marketing operations are high-volume execution targets — and marketing Codex usage grew 26× since February. **Have we delegated end-to-end campaign, service-resolution or catalog workflows to governed agents, and can we show output executed rather than "we adopted an assistant"?**
- Speed compounds in consumer markets. **If a rival is three seasons into an execution lead, do we grasp that our well-governed assistant estate is not a substitute — and that the crossing gets dearer every quarter we wait?**

### 🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
- Casework, benefits processing, records and correspondence are exactly the high-volume, rules-bound work agentic execution accelerates — under the governance duties (EU AI Act high-risk regime, live since 2 August 2026) you already owe. **Have we moved any citizen-facing or back-office workflow from assistance to governed execution, and can we show the throughput gain and the audit trail together?**
- Public bodies risk being the archetype of the well-governed idle hall. **Are we measuring service actually delivered by agents, or are we mistaking a mature governance posture for a working one — while a compounding capability gap opens against the private sector we regulate and rely on?**

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Second Wage to the Two Harvests

Read the whole stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking *is this agent named, scoped, logged, owned and loyal?* and started asking the only question a barn can answer: *did it actually do the work, and how much came in?* The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the keyring is which key it holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what it can reach; the bell discloses when it slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you give it a scoped, revocable identity; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why your own name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose interest it serves. **The two harvests sits at the next turn, because every one of those controls governs what the agent *is* and *may do* — and none of them measures what it *actually did*. And the newest telemetry has made "what it actually did" the axis the market now separates on: the leaders' advantage is not that their agents are better governed but that their agents are executing more real work, and the control that matters migrates out of "is this agent named, scoped, logged and loyal?" and into "is this agent reaping — completing whole tasks — and is our barn filling faster than our rivals'?"** The point is blunt: **an estate can name every agent, keep one book, sign the bond and audit every wage, and still bring in a fraction of a rival's harvest — because governance is the price of admission to the field, not the act of reaping it, and the gap between the house that reaps and the house that merely governs is compounding while you read this.**

- **The reality — the divide is an execution divide, and it is tripling.** **OpenAI's enterprise telemetry reports frontier firms (top 10% of AI users each month) now generate 8.3× as many output tokens per active user as typical firms, up from 2.6× in January; as of June, 64% of combined Codex-plus-ChatGPT enterprise output tokens are agentic (Codex), not chat — "from assistance to execution." Advanced use is spreading beyond engineering: weekly active enterprise Codex users grew 108× in legal, 41× in sales, 41× in recruiting and 26× in marketing since February (vs 5× in engineering), and 21% of frontier-firm users use Plugins weekly vs 9% at typical firms (OpenAI Enterprise Signals, as reported).** Read architecturally, three things matter. First, the leaders' lead is measured in *execution* tokens — work delegated and completed — not in chat volume; the same model, differently deployed, produces radically different output. Second, the advantage is *compounding*: a 2.6×-to-8.3× move in eight months is a widening, not a plateau, because execution generates the data, workflows and organizational muscle that make the next execution cheaper. Third, it is *spreading horizontally* — legal, sales, recruiting, marketing — so treating agentic execution as an engineering-only phenomenon under-reads the divide.
- **The collision — most estates optimized governance and forgot the harvest.** The temptation, after a week of building the governed house, is to treat the governance as the achievement. **But McKinsey's 2026 State of AI (1,719 leaders, 97 countries) finds a two-speed race — 40% of >$1B firms scaling agents (up from 27%) versus 22% and flat at smaller firms — with only ~6% of enterprises "AI high performers" attributing >5% of EBIT to AI, while independent surveys find 79% of organizations struggling to scale AI despite investment and only ~18% fully integrated (McKinsey; Writer, as reported).** Two engineering realities cut against confusing governance with output. **First, adoption is not execution:** a seat bought, an agent named and a policy written produce no grain; only a whole task delegated and completed does — and most dashboards count the former. **Second, the counterfactual is invisible without measuring output:** an estate that governs beautifully has no signal that its rival's identically-governed agents are executing eight times the work, because it is measuring the hall (agents deployed, controls in place), not the barn (tasks executed), and the two look equally impressive on a maturity chart.
- **The answer — measure the barn, move from assistance to execution, and close the compounding gap now.** **Instrument work actually executed by agents — tasks closed end-to-end, cycle time, and the ratio of execution tokens to chat tokens — and make that, not seat count or a governance checklist, the board scoreboard. Pick two or three high-volume workflows and delegate the whole task (not just the draft) to a governed agent, reusing the identity, scope, logging and ownership you already built. And treat the gap as a compounding debt: model how much dearer it is to close next year than this quarter, and allocate accordingly.** This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — *be able to account for, and act on, what you run* — now pointed at the last axis: not what the agent is, nor whom it serves, but how much it actually harvested.

The strategic core: **once the same governed agent can either stand in the hall assisting or go to the fields executing, an estate's fate stops being set by how well it governs and starts being set by how much it reaps — so survival migrates out of "is this agent named, scoped, logged and loyal?" and into "is our barn filling faster than our rivals', and do we grasp that the gap compounds every season we spend admiring the hall?"** The data makes the temptation concrete — mistake a mature, well-governed AI estate for a productive one — and hides the cost in the counterfactual you never measure. The board translation: **grade the barn, not the hall; move from assistance to execution; and close the gap now, because it is cheapest today.** The failure mode is exact: **an estate that names every agent, keeps one book, signs the bond and audits every wage — and then leaves those immaculate servants standing in the hall — will pass every governance review, top every maturity chart, and lose, quietly and compoundingly, to a rival who did the same four things and then walked its servants out to reap.**

### Why "the two harvests" is the discipline that matters now

For a week the worry was whether the agent was named, scoped, logged, owned and loyal — all properties of an agent *at rest*. **This morning's data adds the property that actually decides the outcome: whether the agent is *at work*.** OpenAI's telemetry shows the leaders' edge is execution — 64% of tokens are agentic, and the frontier-vs-typical gap tripled to 8.3× in eight months — while McKinsey's two-speed race shows most estates still assisting, not executing. Governance will not close that gap, because both houses are equally governed; only sending the servants to the fields will. The estates that keep their standing will be the ones that grade themselves on grain in the barn, delegate whole tasks rather than first drafts, and act on the gap now — because a lead built by learning the fields compounds against everyone who waits.

### How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — **be able to account for, and act on, what you run** — now sharpened to *be able to account for what your agents actually harvested.* On a legacy estate the instinct, after a hard year of governance work, is to declare victory when the hall is orderly: agents named, one control plane, name on the bond, wages checked. Under the two harvests that orderly hall is a beautiful way to fall behind, because the rival with the identical hall is out in the fields reaping. The retrofit is **weigh, execute, close. Weigh:** instrument work actually executed by agents and put it on the board's scoreboard in place of seat counts and checklists. **Execute:** take two or three high-volume workflows and delegate the whole task to the governed agents you already built, not just the drafting. **Close:** treat the tripling gap as a compounding debt and allocate to close it this quarter, because it is dearer next. And keep the whole refrain: the model is a rented stove, now cheap; the knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door; the passport names each agent; the census is the roll; the inspector asks you to produce it; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what an agent can reach; the bell discloses when your own agent slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you give every agent its own name; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why your own name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising agent eats — and *the two harvests is why, having governed the servants perfectly, you must still send them to the fields and weigh what they bring in, because the divide that now decides your fate is not who governs best but who reaps most, and it is compounding.*

## 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. **Grade the barn, not the hall — put work-executed on the board scoreboard.** The divide is an execution divide (frontier firms at 8.3× output, up from 2.6×; 64% of tokens now agentic), and adoption metrics hide it. **This month, replace "agents deployed" and "governance maturity" as your headline AI metric with work actually executed by agents: tasks closed end-to-end, cycle time cut, and the ratio of execution tokens to chat tokens across your top workflows.** Report one number to the board: the share of a chosen high-volume workflow now completed by a governed agent rather than merely assisted.
2. **Move from assistance to execution — delegate whole workflows, not first drafts.** Frontier firms lead on delegated work, and it is spreading beyond engineering (legal 108×, sales 41×, marketing 26× since February). **This month, pick two or three high-volume, rules-bound workflows and hand the whole task to a governed agent — reusing the identity, scope, logging and ownership you already built — with a human on approval, not on every step.** Report the throughput gained and the cycle time cut, side by side with the audit trail, to prove execution and governance are not a trade-off.
3. **Close the gap now — treat the tripling lead as a compounding debt.** A 2.6×-to-8.3× move in eight months is not a plateau you catch at leisure; the working house learns the fields while you tidy the hall. **This month, model how much dearer it is to close the execution gap next year than this quarter, and allocate capital and attention accordingly — the cheapest morning to send your servants to the fields is always this one.** Report a dated plan to move a second and third workflow from assistance to execution, with owners and target harvests. **The model is a rented stove, now cheap; the knowledge is the larder; the census is the roll; the inspector asks for it; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what an agent can reach; the bell discloses when your own agent slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you give every agent its own name; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why your own name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising agent eats — and the two harvests is why, having governed the servants perfectly, you must still send them to the fields and weigh what they bring in, because a governed hall of idle servants weighs nothing at reaping, the estates pulling away are the ones that set the same named, loyal servants to real work, and the gap between the house that reaps and the house that admires its own good order has already tripled in a season and compounds with every harvest you spend keeping the hall tidy.**
