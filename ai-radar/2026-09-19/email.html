The Two Harvests

Saturday, 19 September 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

All week you built the governed house — named every agent, kept one book, signed the bond, checked the wage. This morning the data lands a colder question underneath: a governed hall is not a full barn, and the divide that now decides your fate is not who governs best but who reaps most. The move: grade the barn (work executed), move from assistance to execution, and close the gap now — because it compounds.

1 · Executive Summary

The discipline moves from how well the house is governed to how much it actually reaps — because a governed hall is not a full barn, and the newest data says the divide that separates winners is execution, not adoption or governance.

1. The reality — the divide is about execution, and it's tripling. OpenAI's enterprise telemetry reports frontier firms (top 10% of AI users) now generate 8.3× as many output tokens per active user as typical firms, up from 2.6× in January; as of June, 64% of enterprise output tokens are agentic (Codex), not chat — the shift "from assistance to execution." Advanced use is spreading: weekly active enterprise Codex users grew 108× in legal, 41× in sales, 41× in recruiting and 26× in marketing since February (vs 5× in engineering) (OpenAI Enterprise Signals, as reported).

2. The collision — most estates govern well and reap little. McKinsey's 2026 State of AI (1,719 leaders, 97 countries) finds a two-speed race — 40% of >$1B firms scaling agents (up from 27%) vs 22% and flat at smaller firms; only ~6% are AI high performers attributing >5% of EBIT to AI — while 79% of organizations report scaling challenges and only ~18% have fully integrated AI (McKinsey; Writer, as reported).

3. The answer — measure the barn, execute, close the gap. Track work executed (not seats or a checklist), delegate whole high-volume workflows to your governed agents, and treat the tripling gap as a compounding debt: it widens every season because the working house learns the fields while you tidy the hall.

Bottom line: the livery, the steward, the surety and the second wage are all necessary — but none of them puts grain in the barn. The estates pulling away simply took the same governed, loyal servants and sent them to reap, and the gap tripled in eight months and compounds. Walk out to the barn and ask the oldest question a farmer ever asked: how much did we actually bring in?

2 · Allegory — The Two Harvests

Two neighbouring houses had done everything right — every hand named and scoped, one honest book, the master's name on the bond, the counsellor's wage checked. Walk either hall and you would call it well run. But at reaping the barns did not match. The first master, having governed so beautifully, had come to treat the governing as the work; his named, scoped, loyal servants stood about the spotless hall all day, fetching and advising. The second master had done every governing thing his neighbour had — and then, at dawn, sent the same servants out into the fields to actually bring in the crop.

When the wagons came in, the second barn held eight loads to the first's one — though the servants, wages, keys and book were, hand for hand, the same. The difference was not the quality of the servants nor the tidiness of the hall. It was that one house set its servants to reap and the other set them to stand. And the gap was not fixed: at spring it had been two-and-a-half to one; by autumn, eight to one and widening — because the working house was learning the fields. It was a compounding interest, paid to the house that worked and charged to the house that admired its own good order.

So the wise houses learned three things. A governed hall is not a full barn — naming, booking, bonding and wage-checking are the price of admission, not the harvest. Measure the grain, not the servants — a hall of idle, well-governed servants weighs the same at reaping as an empty one. The gap compounds, so cross it now — a lead built on learning the fields does not wait for the laggard; the cheapest morning to send your servants to the fields is always this one.

The question it forces: We named every agent, kept one book, signed the bond and checked the wage — and it was right. But the barns are being weighed. Have we sent our governed servants to the fields to execute whole tasks, or are they standing about a tidy hall fetching and advising? Do we grade ourselves on grain in the barn, or on servants hired and a checklist ticked? And do we grasp that the lead the working houses hold tripled in eight months while we admired our own good order?

3 · Technical — From the Second Wage to the Two Harvests

Every governance control governs what the agent is and may do — none measures what it actually did. The newest telemetry has made "what it actually did" the axis the market separates on: the leaders' edge is not better governance but more executed work, and the control that matters migrates from "is it named, scoped, logged and loyal?" to "is our barn filling faster than our rivals'?" The reality: the divide is an execution divide — frontier firms at 8.3× output (up from 2.6×), 64% of tokens now agentic — and it is compounding, because execution generates the data and muscle that make the next execution cheaper. The collision: adoption is not execution (a seat, a name and a policy produce no grain; only a whole task delegated and completed does), and the counterfactual is invisible unless you measure output — a beautifully governed estate has no signal its rival executes eight times the work. The answer: instrument work executed and make it the board scoreboard; delegate whole workflows on the governed rails you already built; treat the gap as a compounding debt and close it now.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Grade the barn, not the hall. Replace "agents deployed" and "governance maturity" as your headline AI metric with work actually executed by agents: tasks closed end-to-end, cycle time cut, and execution tokens vs chat tokens. Report the share of a chosen high-volume workflow now completed by a governed agent rather than merely assisted.

2. Move from assistance to execution. Pick two or three high-volume, rules-bound workflows and hand the whole task to a governed agent — reusing the identity, scope, logging and ownership you already built — with a human on approval, not on every step. Report throughput gained and cycle time cut, side by side with the audit trail.

3. Close the gap now — it's a compounding debt. A 2.6×-to-8.3× move in eight months is not a plateau you catch at leisure. Model how much dearer the gap is to close next year than this quarter, and allocate accordingly. Report a dated plan to move a second and third workflow from assistance to execution, with owners and target harvests.
