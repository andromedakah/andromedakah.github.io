The Surety

Yesterday you built one governed office over every agent and kept its book your own; this morning the harder question lands, and the law has just put it on the table — when the reckoning comes, whose name stands surety for what your agents do: yours, or your hired steward's?
Thursday, 17 September 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering

The EU AI Act's obligations for high-risk AI and its penalty regime are now live — the main body of duties applied from 2 August 2026, and the fines run to €35M or 7% of global annual turnover for prohibited uses, €15M or 3% for breaches of the high-risk and governance obligations, and €7.5M or 1% for supplying incorrect information to a regulator (EU AI Act, as reported). And the Act binds the deployer and the provider by name: "our platform vendor is compliant" and "our steward saw to it" are not, in law, an answer. You can rent the steward's office; you cannot rent the surety. The board question is no longer who keeps our book? — it is: whose name stands surety for what our agents do, can that person produce the record on their own this week without asking a vendor's leave, and have we mistaken a supplier's certificate for our own bond?

1 · Executive Summary 90-second read

Yesterday the radar told you to put one governed office over every agent — one book that registers, identifies, scopes, observes, costs and can dismiss it — and to keep that book your own rather than rent it from the grandest vendor in the valley. This morning the discipline moves up one final, uncomfortable level, from who keeps the book to whose neck is on the line for what the book records. And here the market's temptation and the law's demand pull in opposite directions: the vendors are selling you a ready-made office and the comfort of delegating governance to it, while the law has just made governance a personal, non-delegable bond.

The reality that is now live — the accountability regime, not a press release. The EU AI Act is no longer a future compliance project; its central obligations are in force. The Act entered into force on 1 August 2024 and phased in — prohibited practices and AI-literacy duties from 2 February 2025, general-purpose-AI-model obligations and the governance-and-penalty machinery from 2 August 2025, and the broad body of high-risk-system obligations from 2 August 2026 (with AI embedded in regulated products following in August 2027) (EU AI Act, as reported). That means that as of this week, an enterprise deploying high-risk AI in the EU owes, by law, a running risk-management process, automatic logging and record-keeping (Article 12), effective human oversight, data governance, and accuracy/robustness — and owes them as the deployer, distinct from and in addition to the provider's duties. The penalties are not theoretical: up to €35M or 7% of global annual turnover for prohibited practices, up to €15M or 3% for breaching the high-risk and other obligations, and up to €7.5M or 1% for giving a regulator incorrect, incomplete or misleading information (EU AI Act, as reported).

The collision — you can buy the office, but the bond stays with you. This lands the morning after the platforms offered to be your governance. At Dreamforce 2026 Salesforce formalised the whole agent-governance stack into a single "Trusted Enterprise AI Harness" and an AI Control Plane to register, identify, govern, observe, cost and revoke every agent — its own and third parties' — with broad availability not expected until early 2027 (Salesforce, as reported). The offer is real and the shape is right. But the Act's architecture is blunt about who answers: a provider's conformity does not discharge a deployer's duties, and a deployer who materially modifies a system, or puts its name on it, can become a provider in law. So the accountability cannot travel with the coat of arms on the invoice. Meanwhile the scale that makes this acute keeps climbing — Gartner projects 40% of enterprise applications will embed task-specific AI agents by year-end 2026, up from under 5% in 2025 — and the same analyst warns that applying one uniform governance across all agents is itself the root cause of failure, and that by 2027 40% of enterprises will demote or decommission autonomous agents over governance gaps found only after a production incident (Gartner, as reported). A law that fines you 3% of turnover for a missing record meets an estate where most cannot yet produce the record.

The stakes — name the surety, hold the evidence, and make the contract carry the liability without discharging your duty. For a board the move is exact. The office can be rented; the bond cannot. Three things follow. First, name a surety inside your own house — a single accountable owner, a person or role of yours (not a vendor), for every high-risk AI system and material agent, because "the platform handles governance" is not a defence the Act recognises. Second, hold the evidence in your own hand and be able to produce it — the logs, the risk assessments, the human-oversight design, the data-governance records — portable and yours, so you can answer a regulator on your own initiative within days, whichever vendor's office you happen to rent. Third, make the contract carry the liability back-to-back, but never mistake a supplier's certificate for your own bond — audit rights, evidence access, indemnity and exit/portability in every AI vendor agreement, treated as necessary and never sufficient, because on the bad morning the assize asks for your name, not your steward's.

2 · Allegory of the Day

🖋️ "The Surety"

Topic: This morning the frame turns from who keeps the book to whose name stands surety for what it records. The EU AI Act's central high-risk obligations (risk management, Article 12 logging and record-keeping, human oversight, data governance, accuracy and robustness) applied from 2 August 2026 and bind the deployer as well as the provider; penalties reach €35M or 7% of global turnover (prohibited practices), €15M or 3% (high-risk and other obligations) and €7.5M or 1% (incorrect information to a regulator) (EU AI Act, as reported). It lands the morning after the platforms offered to be your governance (Salesforce's Trusted Enterprise AI Harness and AI Control Plane, GA ~early 2027), and against Gartner's forecast that 40% of enterprise apps embed task-specific agents by 2026 and its warning that uniform governance is the root cause of failure (Gartner, as reported). The surety allegory is the radar's own illustration.

There was once a great house that, having at last learned to name every hand in its crowded servants' hall and to keep one honest book over them all, breathed easier — and hired, at no small cost, a fine steward to keep that book, and even began to weigh the ready-made office the grandest house in the valley had come up the drive to sell. Then one grey morning a rider came from the county town in the crown's own colours, and what he carried was not an offer but a law. From this term forward, he read aloud, the conduct of every house in the realm is the master's personal bond. The master's own name and seal now stand surety for what his servants do; when the assize sits, the house must lay its book open, whole and true, and show that a hand of judgement stood over every grave act. And should the book be false, or missing, or the oversight a fiction, the fine is not a few coins from the housekeeping — it is a share of the whole estate's yearly income, taken from the master, by name.

The master did the only sensible thing and sent at once for his fine new steward, and for the man from the grandest house who had been so eager to sell him the ready-made office. "You keep my book," he said to the one; "and you sold me the very office of governance," he said to the other; "so this bond is yours to carry, surely, between you." And here the morning turned cold. For the steward bowed and said he would keep the book as faithfully as any man alive — but the bond was not his to carry; he was a servant of the house, and it was the master's name the crown had written down. And the man from the grandest house spread his hands and said, more gently, that he sold a splendid office and a beautifully ruled ledger and would stand behind his goods — but he could not stand behind the master at the assize; he could sell the office, he said, but not the surety. The master understood, too late to be comfortable, that he had spent a fortune buying someone to keep his book and someone to point to, and had bought no one at all to answer in his place.

So the wise houses of the valley, watching that grey rider go up one drive after another, learned three hard things and set them down before the assize could sit. The first: name a surety of your own blood. Not the hired steward, not the great house that sold the office, but a person of your own household — known, named, and answerable — over every grave matter the servants touched, because a bond with no name of the house upon it is a bond the law reads as the master's anyway, only now with no one inside the house who was ever charged to make it good. The second: keep the evidence in your own hand. The book that answers at the assize is worthless if it lives only in the rented office, in a ledger you cannot open without the great house's leave, in a hand you cannot read and could not carry out the door the day you part ways; so the wise kept their own true copy — every judgement recorded, every oversight real, every record theirs to lay open within the week, whichever office they happened to rent. And the third: make the contract carry what a contract can, and never a grain more. They made the great house sign, back to back, to answer for its own goods, to open its workings to inspection, and to let them leave with their book intact — but they never once mistook the great house's fine certificate for their own seal on the bond, because a supplier's warranty is a thing you can sue upon later and the assize is a thing that asks for your name today.

So the wise houses took the good of the offer and refused its comfort. They rented the office where it served, and kept the surety their own. They set a named hand of their own household over every grave act; they held their own true copy of the book, portable and whole and ready to lay open; they bound the great house by contract to answer for its goods and to let them walk away with their record; and they swallowed the truth their tempted neighbour learned too late: that you can hire a man to keep your book and buy an office to house it, but the one thing no fortune can purchase is someone to stand in your place when the crown reads out your name — that a certificate from a supplier is not a seal upon your bond — and that the house made safe is not the one with the finest rented office but the one whose master can walk into the assize, lay his own book open, and say, in his own name, here is what we did, and here is the hand that answered for it.

The moral, stated plainly: the model is a rented stove, now nearly free; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent at the door; the lingua franca is the tongue it speaks; the conductor keeps the ensemble in time; the ear hears whether it plays well; the repertoire is the music only your house can play; the census is the roll that counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the break-in is what the missing entries cost; the two mills are whether the motion grinds flour; the miller's toll is what the cheapest grinding charges; the apprentice's apprentice is what happens when the tools improve the tools; the herald's proclamation is that a declaration of arrival is not arrival; the keyring is knowing which key each agent holds; the locksmith is the tool that opens any lock and mends it; the tide table is building one quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is keeping the papers that trace the work; the errand is bounding what a clever servant can reach; the bell is ringing the alarm the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you close the gaps before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you give every hand its own name and key; the steward is why you put one governed office over the whole staff and keep its book your own — and the surety is why the master's own name must be on the bond: because the law now makes you, the deployer, personally answerable for what your agents do, a duty it will not let you delegate to the steward you hired or the great house that sold you the office; because a house that rents a fine office and assumes the accountability went with it has bought someone to point at and no one to answer; and because on the morning the assize sits, it does not ask for your vendor's certificate — it asks for your book, your oversight, and your name. A house that buys the finest governance in the valley and keeps the surety in someone else's hand has not been made safe by its purchase; it has only arranged, at great expense, to be the one still standing in the dock when the great house has gone home.

The question it forces: We learned to keep one honest book over every agent, and to keep it our own. This morning the harder question is the one the law has just written down: when the assize sits, whose name is on the bond? Is there a hand of our own household — named, known, answerable — over every high-risk system, or have we quietly assumed the platform we bought carries the accountability it never could? Could that person walk into the room this week and lay our own book open — the logs, the risk files, the record that a human judgement stood over every grave act — without first asking a vendor's leave, in a form we could carry out the door the day we change offices? And have we bound our suppliers to answer for their goods and to let us leave with our record intact — while never once mistaking their fine certificate for our own seal on the bond, since the fine for a false or missing book is now a share of everything we earn in a year, taken from us, by name?

4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Steward to the Surety

Read the whole stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking who keeps our book? and started asking the question the law has just forced: whose name answers for what the book records, and could that person produce it without a vendor's leave? The gate admits the agent; the passport names it at the door; the lingua franca lets it speak; the conductor coordinates it; the ear hears its errors; the repertoire is the data it performs; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the break-in is what the missing entries cost; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand is bounding what an agent can reach; the bell is disclosing when your own agent slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you give every agent a first-class, scoped, revocable identity; the steward is why you put one governed office over all of them and keep its book your own. The surety sits at the next turn, because a governed office is necessary but not sufficient once the law attaches a personal, non-delegable liability to what your agents do — and it turns on one hard fact: accountability does not travel with a purchase order, so the control that matters migrates out of "do we have a governed control plane?" and into "is there a named owner of our own who can produce the whole record on demand, and does the liability stay ours no matter whose office we rent?" The point is blunt: a beautifully governed estate run entirely inside a vendor's platform, with no named internal owner and no record you can produce yourself, is not a compliant estate — it is an estate that has arranged who will be standing in the dock, and it is you.

  • The reality — the duty is live, and it is personal. The EU AI Act's central high-risk obligations applied from 2 August 2026 (the Act entered into force 1 August 2024; prohibited practices and AI-literacy duties from 2 February 2025; GPAI-model obligations and the governance-and-penalty machinery from 2 August 2025; AI embedded in regulated products following in August 2027). For a high-risk system the deployer owes a running risk-management process, automatic logging and record-keeping (Article 12), effective human oversight, data governance, accuracy and robustness — as duties distinct from the provider's — enforced by penalties up to €35M/7% (prohibited), €15M/3% (high-risk and other obligations) and €7.5M/1% (incorrect information to a regulator) (EU AI Act, as reported). Read architecturally, three things matter. First, the Act separates provider from deployer and does not let one discharge the other; a deployer who materially modifies a system or markets it under its own name can itself become a provider. Second, the duties are evidentiary — you must be able to produce logs, assessments and oversight design — which is an engineering property of your estate, not a clause in a policy. Third, the liability is personal to the enterprise, not the tooling: the fine is levied on you.
  • The collision — the platforms sell the office, but the bond stays home. The temptation is to answer a personal, non-delegable duty with a purchase. At Dreamforce 2026 Salesforce formalised agent governance into one platform — a Trusted Enterprise AI Harness and an AI Control Plane to register, identify, govern, observe, cost and revoke every agent, its own and third parties', with broad availability ~early 2027 (Salesforce, as reported). The office is genuinely useful and its shape is right. But two engineering realities cut against delegation. First, scale and heterogeneity: Gartner projects 40% of enterprise applications embed task-specific agents by year-end 2026 (up from under 5% in 2025), and warns that uniform governance is itself the root cause of failure — that conflating an agent's ability to act with the scope of its access, and ruling all agents alike, will drive 40% of enterprises to demote or decommission autonomous agents by 2027 over gaps found only after incidents (Gartner, as reported). Second, the containment floor is still low: 60% of organisations cannot terminate a misbehaving agent quickly and 55% cannot isolate AI from sensitive networks (Kiteworks 2026, as reported) — so the very record the law demands (what the agent did, whether a human could stop it) is exactly what most estates cannot yet produce. A platform can help you generate that record; it cannot become the party that answers for it.
  • The answer — name the surety, hold the evidence, contract the liability. Assign a single named accountable owner of your own — a role in your house, not a vendor — to every high-risk AI system and material agent, and make "who answers for this?" a field with a person in it, not a platform. Hold the evidence yourself and make it producible: automatic, tamper-evident logs (Article 12), the risk-management file, the human-oversight design and the data-governance record, kept portable and exportable so you can lay them open within days regardless of which office you rent. And make every AI vendor contract carry the liability back-to-back — audit rights, evidence access, indemnity, and exit/portability of your record — while treating a supplier's conformity as necessary and never sufficient. This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now sharpened to its legal edge: the account must be one you can produce, and the answer must carry your name.

The strategic core: once the law attaches a personal, non-delegable liability to agent behaviour, survival migrates out of "do we have a governed office?" and into "is there a named surety of our own, holding a record we can produce ourselves, with the liability contracted back-to-back but never assumed to have left with the vendor?" The market has made the temptation concrete — buy the office, delegate the worry — and the law has made the temptation a trap. The board translation: rent the office where it serves, but keep the surety your own — a named owner, a record you hold, a contract that carries liability without discharging your duty. 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

  1. Name a surety of your own — a person, not a platform — for every high-risk system. The Act binds the deployer by name and does not accept "our vendor is compliant" as a defence. This month, assign a single accountable owner inside your own house to every high-risk AI system and material agent, make "who answers for this?" a field with a named person in it, and confirm they know they hold the bond. Report one number to the board: the share of your high-risk AI systems and material agents that have a named, internal accountable owner — versus those where accountability is assumed to sit with a vendor.
  2. Hold the evidence yourself and prove you can produce it. Article 12 logging, the risk-management file, the human-oversight design and the data-governance record must be producible on demand — and useless if they live only in a rented console. Run a fire drill this month: pick two high-risk systems and require the owner to produce the complete record within a week, in a form you hold yourself and could export the day you change vendors. Report the share of high-risk deployments that passed the drill — and a target to raise it.
  3. Make the contract carry the liability back-to-back — and never mistake a certificate for your bond. A supplier's conformity is a thing you can sue on later; the assize asks for your name today. Put audit rights, evidence access, indemnity and record-portability/exit into every AI vendor contract, treat a vendor's certificate as necessary but never sufficient, and price the 3%-of-turnover exposure of a record you cannot produce. Report the share of AI vendor contracts carrying back-to-back liability, audit and exit rights.
