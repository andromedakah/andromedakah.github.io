The Break-In

Wednesday, 2 September 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

Yesterday the inspector asked you to produce the roll; this morning we learn what the missing roll costs — not a fine, but a thief. IBM's 2026 Cost of a Data Breach finds shadow AI now implicated in 43% of breaches (up from 20%), a shadow-AI breach running ~$5.39M — roughly $670K above average — with 68% of breached firms carrying no AI governance policy and ~97% lacking AI access controls. Kiteworks: 80% hit by a security or AI incident, 65% an AI-agent incident, and 60% who cannot even terminate a misbehaving agent. The inspector grades your books; the burglar doesn't wait for the assizes.

1 · Executive Summary

For a week this radar built an agentic workforce, then learned to count it (the census) and prove the count to an authority (the inspector). This morning the frame turns from audit to loss: the same ungoverned agent that fails an inspection is the one an attacker walks through — and this week the industry attached a number to it.

1. The break-in has a price now. IBM's 2026 Cost of a Data Breach finds shadow AI implicated in 43% of breaches (up from 20%); a shadow-AI breach ~$5.39M and adding as much as ~$670K to the bill; 68% of breached organizations with no AI governance policy; ~97% lacking proper AI access controls; a record global average breach of ~$4.99M (+12%); and 1 in 4 malicious breaches AI-enabled (~+$1M each). Governance failure — not the technology — drives the cost (as reported; IBM report released mid-2026 and re-circulated this week).

2. You cannot terminate what you never enrolled. Kiteworks' 2026 survey: 80% of organizations hit by a security/AI incident in the past year, 65% specifically an AI-agent incident; 63% cannot enforce purpose limits on agents; 60% cannot terminate a misbehaving one; 65% found staff using unapproved AI with sensitive data. The census (enroll it), the inspector (prove it), the kill-switch (stop it) are the same discipline from three sides.

3. The attack surface is non-human and unwatched. CSA: non-human identities outnumber humans ~45:1 (to ~144:1 in cloud-native), yet only ~10% of firms are mature on them and ~16% do not track AI-identity creation. Every unrostered agent is a live credential the thief can wear.

Bottom line: a workforce you cannot see is a workforce you cannot defend — and this week the industry priced exactly what that costs: a record breach, a shadow-AI premium, and a majority of firms that could not stop an agent gone wrong even if they saw it. Enroll every agent, scope it to least privilege, and hold the kill-switch — because the break-in does not send a letter first, and the door it uses is the one you never wrote down.

2 · Allegory — The Break-In

The city had taken its census and faced the inspector — every worker named, every owner known, every key logged. And most of the roll was honest. But in the back streets, quietly, some households had gone on hiring the old way: a worker conjured on a whim, given a key to do its errand, and never entered on any roll. Not out of malice — out of hurry. Then one night there was a break-in at the granary. Not a battering ram at the watched main gate — the thief did something simpler: he found the one worker no household had entered on the roll, holding a key that opened a door nobody was minding, and walked in behind it as if he belonged there.

By morning the grain was gone. And when the city tried to answer the only questions that mattered — which worker let him in? who owned it? can we revoke its key before he returns? — it could do none of them, because the worker was never on the roll. The danger of the unrostered worker was never that it might do wrong on its own; it was that it was a standing, open door — a live key in an unwatched lock — and a door does not have to be malicious to be walked through. The loss was weighed, as always, against the treasury, and it came in at a record, because the households that had hired fastest and counted least had the most open doors. The ones that kept their footing had done the quiet thing all along: let no worker exist the roll did not know, gave each the smallest key, watched every lock, and kept a hand on a lever that could recall any key in an instant. The front gate you guard is not where the break-in happens. It happens at the door you forgot you had — the one held open by a worker you never wrote down.

The question it forces: the inspector asks whether we can produce the record; the burglar asks whether we can shut the door. If we learned tonight that one agent was moving data it should not touch, could we, before morning, name it, name its human owner, revoke its credential and stop it — or would we be the city in the dark, counting a record loss, unable even to say how many doors we left open? Do we have a roll, a scope and a kill-switch for every non-human worker with a key — or only a front gate we guard while the back streets hire in the dark?

3 · Technical — Shutting the Door Before the Break-In

The break-in is priced: IBM 2026 — shadow AI in 43% of breaches (from 20%); a shadow-AI breach ~$5.39M, +~$670K; 68% no AI governance policy; ~97% no AI access controls; record avg breach ~$4.99M (+12%); 1 in 4 malicious breaches AI-enabled. The cost is not the model's behavior but the absence of enrollment, scope and control around it. You cannot stop what you never enrolled: Kiteworks — 80% had a security/AI incident, 65% an AI-agent incident; 63% cannot enforce purpose limits; 60% cannot terminate a misbehaving agent. No agent should run without an enrolled identity mapped to a human owner and a least-privilege, short-lived credential, and one lever should suspend it and recall its keys in seconds. If "stop that agent" is a support ticket, you have a hope, not a control. The surface is non-human: CSA — non-human identities outnumber humans ~45:1 (to ~144:1 in cloud); ~10% mature on them; ~16% do not track AI-identity creation.

On legacy: the temptation is to bolt agents onto existing service credentials — long-lived, over-scoped, shared — because it ships faster. This week made that the expensive path. Enroll: no agent without a first-class, attributable identity mapped to a human owner. Scope: least-privilege, short-lived, per-task keys, so a stolen key opens one small door. Watch: monitor non-human credentials as closely as human logins. Revoke: a real kill-switch that recalls keys in seconds, because 60% of firms cannot. The census is the roll; the inspector asks you to produce it; the break-in is what walks through the door you left off it.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Enroll and scope every agent. Require that no agent runs without a first-class, attributable identity mapped to a human owner and a least-privilege, short-lived credential — and hunt down the shadow ones (65% of firms have staff using unapproved AI on sensitive data). Report one number: the share of production agents on enrolled, least-privilege, revocable credentials versus standing, over-scoped ones.

2. Build a real kill-switch. 60% of firms cannot terminate a misbehaving agent; be in the 40%. Wire a single lever that suspends any agent and recalls its credentials in seconds, and rehearse it: pick one production agent and time how long it takes to name it, name its owner, revoke its access and confirm it is stopped. If that drill takes longer than an incident would, the gap is the finding. Report the mean-time-to-revoke.

3. Price your shadow-AI exposure before an incident does. With shadow-AI breaches at ~$5.39M and a record average of ~$4.99M, an ungoverned agent is a measurable liability. Quantify your non-human identity surface (they outnumber humans ~45:1), find where unapproved AI touches sensitive data, and treat closing that gap as breach-cost avoidance the board can read on the P&L. Report two numbers: non-human identities without an owner or expiry, and the share of sensitive-data systems reachable by an unenrolled agent.
