The Conductor

Friday, 28 August 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

Yesterday the radar settled the language — the two open tongues an agent must speak now live in a neutral library no vendor can lock. But a hundred thousand named agents that can all speak is not a workforce; it is a crowd. Today it asks the question the lingua franca leaves open: once every agent can speak, who makes them play together — in time, toward one piece of work? Deloitte's new survey (501 senior-manager-to-C-suite leaders, five US industries, April–June 2026) finds only 15% have scaled, orchestrated, cross-functional multi-agent adoption — mostly in low-risk, low-ROI corners — even as 74% expect nearly half their processes redesigned around agents within four years. Gartner warns 40%+ of agentic-AI projects will be canceled by end of 2027. We hired virtuoso soloists all year; did we hire the conductor?

1 · Executive Summary

For a week this radar walked the value up the stack; today it names the layer that turns all of it into work: the conductor. The shift is no longer "can an agent act?", "who is the agent?" or "what language does it speak?" but "who coordinates the hundred thousand of them so they produce one outcome instead of a hundred thousand collisions?" The market's answer this week: orchestration — and the sober evidence that it, not the model or the agent count, is where value now lives or dies.

1. The gap — few have hired the conductor. Deloitte's research, "AI agents are only the beginning: the path to agentic transformation" (501 senior-manager-to-C-suite leaders, five US industries, fielded April–June 2026), finds only 15% of organizations have scaled, orchestrated, cross-functional multi-agent adoption — mostly in low-risk, near-term-ROI use cases (customer service, IT, engineering) — even as 74% expect nearly half their business processes redesigned around agents within four years. Deloitte's Laura Shact: layered-on quick fixes "may create the sense of getting ahead," but "thriving in the agentic future will require more" — work and organization redesign.

2. The reckoning — un-orchestrated sprawl gets cut. Gartner: more than 40% of agentic-AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027, on escalating costs, unclear value and inadequate controls; a comparable share of enterprises may demote or decommission autonomous agents after production governance failures. Appetite is real (Gartner logged a 1,445% rise in multi-agent inquiries), but appetite without a conductor is exactly what gets cut.

3. The prize — orchestration is the multiplier. Deloitte's math: the agentic-AI market could grow from ~US$8.5B in 2026 to ~US$35B by 2030 — and up to ~30% higher (≈US$45B) for enterprises that orchestrate well. And yesterday's open tongues (A2A + MCP under the Linux Foundation's AAIF) are the shared score every agent can finally read.

Bottom line: the lingua franca let every named agent speak; the conductor is what makes them play one piece of music instead of a hundred thousand solos. When an enterprise is heading for six figures of agents that can all talk, the control point is not another model, agent or pilot — it is the orchestration layer that sequences them, cues the handoffs, rests the idle, meters the cost, and keeps the whole in time toward a business outcome. Stop hiring soloists and start hiring the conductor: pick the high-value processes worth redesigning around coordinated agents, put orchestration, governance and cost-metering on one podium, and measure the music. When the model is rented, the knowledge is the moat, the agent is named and the language is open, the thing that turns all of it into work is who keeps time.

2 · Allegory — The Conductor

A city fell in love with musicians and hired every virtuoso it could find — tireless players who never tired and never slept. It had solved the old problems: every player carried papers that named them, every player passed through the one guarded door, and — as of this season — every player could at last read the same score and tune to the same pitch, for the city kept a common notation in a public library no house could lock. On paper, the greatest assembly of talent it had ever fielded.

And yet the concerts were unlistenable. A hundred brilliant players, each reading the same score, all began at once — strings racing, brass entering four bars early, two soloists in different movements, nobody resting. The audience heard not a symphony but a magnificent, costly roar. The missing figure was not another player — it was the conductor: someone who produced no note yet set the tempo, cued each entrance, rested the idle sections, held the whole toward one piece of music, and kept an eye on the cost. A modest orchestra with a real conductor made better music than a legendary one without. But some hired a conductor and never wrote the piece; some beat time but never redesigned the seating; and some kept hiring soloists faster than any conductor could fold them in, until the house — exhausted by the cost of the roar — canceled the season entirely.

The question it forces: we have named our agents, admitted them through one gate, taught them a common tongue — and we keep hiring more. But have we hired the conductor? Have we chosen the high-value pieces worth redesigning around coordinated agents, seated the sections to hand off cleanly, put orchestration and cost-metering on one podium — or are we still adding virtuoso soloists to an unled pit, mistaking the volume for music, heading for the season that gets canceled?

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Conduct — stand up an orchestration layer, not another agent. You need a podium, not more soloists. Put one orchestration layer over your existing agents — on the open A2A/MCP score — that sequences them, cues handoffs, rests the idle and enforces governance, and prove it on one real multi-agent workflow. Report the outcome, not the agent count. If you can't name who conducts a workflow, it's a candidate for cancellation, not scaling.

2. Redesign — aim the orchestra at a high-value piece. Layered-on agents on unchanged processes are not transformation; only 15% have scaled orchestration, most in safe, low-ROI corners. Pick one high-value, cross-functional process and redesign it around coordinated agents — the seating and the parts, not just the baton — where the ~30% orchestration premium lives.

3. Meter — put cost and value on the conductor's stand. Un-orchestrated sprawl is what gets canceled — 40%+ by end of 2027 — because cost outran value and no one kept time. Wire cost-per-workflow and a named outcome into the orchestration layer, dismiss the agents that add volume but no value, and give every program a cost ceiling and an owner. Report two numbers to the board: the share of agents under real orchestration toward a named outcome, and the cost-per-outcome of your top workflow.
