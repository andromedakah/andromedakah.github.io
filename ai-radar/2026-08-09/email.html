The Waterworks

Sunday, 9 August 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

This week fitted the controls for the layer around the commodity model — the airlock (6 Aug), the keyring (7 Aug), the proving ground (8 Aug). Today the ground shifts under all of them. The industry just crossed a line it will not cross back: 2026 is the first year the world spends more compute running AI than training it. AMD's Lisa Su put the industry at ~35 quadrillion tokens a month (~160× two years ago), inference ~60% of capacity; Deloitte puts inference at ~two-thirds of AI compute; Goldman Sachs sees token demand multiplying ~24× to 120 quadrillion/month by 2030; OpenAI says enterprise is now >40% of revenue. The dam-building era is ending; the waterworks era has begun. The board's question: our bill now comes from the water at our taps, set to multiply twenty-fold — do we own our waterworks, or are we metered to death on someone else's?

1 · Executive Summary

For a month this radar has argued the model is a commodity, so the durable advantage is the layer you own around it. This week made it concrete with controls — the airlock, the keyring, the proving ground. Today the argument gets its macro proof: the AI economy flipped from a buildout to a run-rate. 2026 is the first year global inference compute surpasses training compute — the money stops being about making the model and starts being about running it, and running it is the bill that grows every quarter you succeed.

1. The era changed, not just the chart. Value is migrating from the capital-intensive buildout (training, owned by a few, rented by all) to the operational layer (inference, run by everyone). "Who has the best model" is last era's question; "who runs the flow cheapest and cleanest per unit of value" is this era's.

2. Your AI cost just became opex that grows with success. A pilot is cheap; a successful pilot at 24× throughput is the bill that kills projects. Inference is ~two-thirds of compute and ~55% of cloud AI spend, and demand is forecast to multiply ~24× by 2030 — the token line is now a first-class P&L item.

3. The controls only pay off if the flow is metered and governed. Unit economics and governance are one discipline: an unmetered, ungoverned flow is both a runaway bill and an uncontained risk. Meter every tap, or the era's growth curve becomes your loss curve.

Bottom line: the buildout is over, the run-rate has begun, and the bill has moved from the dam you rent to the water you pump. Rent the reservoir — cheaper every quarter, never your moat. Own the waterworks: routing, caching and small-model splits that make a token cheap; the meter that turns a mystery bill into a managed one; and the airlock, keyring and proving ground that keep the flow clean — because in a year the world crossed into inference-first and token demand is set to rise twenty-fold, the enterprises that thrive are metered by design, not by surprise.

2 · Allegory — The Waterworks

Picture a city that spent a decade raising a great dam. Everyone measured a city's greatness by the height of its wall — and it was not vanity, for a city with no reservoir has no water. But a reservoir waters no one while it merely sits behind the wall; it matters only when the water runs — through the mains, out of ten thousand taps. This is the year the city, for the first time, spent more effort moving water to the taps than raising the wall.

The city waterman read out the meter: the mains now carry on the order of thirty-five thousand-thousand barrels a month, ~160× two years ago, and three barrels in five now go to the taps, not the reservoir; the bankers warn the flow will multiply some twenty-four-fold by the decade's end. Here is the turn: greatness was capital — you raised the wall once, and only a handful of cities could, so everyone else rented their water. Renting the reservoir was never the shame. But a rented reservoir is exactly why the waterworks — pumps, mains, valves, meters, filters — is the part that is yours, and the part your whole fortune now turns on: the city that thrives is not the one with the tallest rented wall but the one whose waterworks wastes nothing and meters every tap. The proud city, still admiring its wall, wakes to a water bill it never metered — and finds success was the thing that drowned it.

The question it forces: Our AI spend has flipped from a thing we build to a thing we run — metered by the token, growing with every agent, forecast to multiply many times over. Do we own our waterworks — cost per token, routing to cheap models, caching, a meter and a governance check on every tap — or are we still staring at whose model is biggest while the water bill runs, unmetered, toward a number our success alone will make ruinous?

3 · Technical — Rent the Reservoir, Own the Waterworks

The reservoir — the rented engine: Opus 5 (#1, $5/$25), Gemini 3.6 Flash, GPT-5.6 Sol, Kimi K3, DeepSeek V4-Flash-0731 (MIT) — cheaper every week, and no longer where the money is; a bigger reservoir does not cut your water bill. The waterworks — the inference layer you own (where advantage moved): route (cheap model for routine, frontier only for the hard), cache (never pay twice), batch/quantise (more tokens per watt), the small-model/large-model split, and — inseparable — the meter on every tap plus the airlock, keyring and proving ground. Lisa Su's milestone (inference now exceeds training; ~35 quadrillion tokens/mo, ~160× in two years, ~60% of capacity) and Deloitte's ~two-thirds of AI compute are why this layer, not the model, is the P&L. The flood to plan for: Goldman sees token demand ~24× to 120 quadrillion/mo by 2030; OpenAI enterprise >40% of revenue, APIs >15B tokens/min — your inference bill scales with your success.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Instrument the meter before you scale a single agent further. You cannot manage a flow you cannot read. Stand up cost-per-token telemetry per agent and per unit of business value, and re-run every production and near-production agent's unit economics at 10× and 24× throughput. Report one number to the board: our fully-loaded inference cost per unit of value, and where it lands if throughput rises twenty-fold. The projects that belong in the cancelled column show themselves here, cheaply, before the invoice does.

2. Build the waterworks: route, cache, and split the reservoir. Stop paying frontier rates for routine work. Put a router in front of your agents (cheap model for common calls, frontier only for the hard), cache repeated answers, batch and quantise the always-on work, and push high-volume, low-stakes inference to the cheapest, closest place it can safely run. The model stays rented and swappable — keep the waterworks model-neutral and re-benchmark cost and quality per token on every swap.

3. Put the meter and the governance on the same tap. Unit economics and safety are one discipline: an unmetered flow is a runaway bill and an uncontained risk. Wire your cost meter to the same control points you fitted this week — the airlock (egress), the keyring (access), the proving ground (pre-production) — so one dashboard shows what each tap costs and whether it is clean. Then demand the same of every vendor and model you rent: show me the per-token cost, the routing, and the governance.
