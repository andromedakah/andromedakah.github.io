The Watchman

Friday, 23 October 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

This week Microsoft said the quiet part out loud: treat your own AI agents as insider threats, because "frontier models can't be trusted." A brilliant worker holding your keys, with no one watching, is not a helper you trust — it is an insider. Post a watchman, keep the keys in a different hand than the wit, and let the leash breathe with the risk.

1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from governing the servant to admitting you cannot take its word — every prior control (livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse, foundation) quietly assumed a governed servant was a trustworthy one. This week the largest enterprise house said that assumption is the bug.

1. The reality — treat agents as insiders; the model can't be trusted. On 19 October 2026 Microsoft unveiled "Guardian Agents," extending its Project Perception program, and framed it around "frontier models can't be trusted" — watching ~60 agent types (Codex, Copilot, Claude Code) continuously and adjusting each agent's permissions as its risk changes, with authority kept outside the model (TechCrunch; TechRadar Pro; Unite.ai; Crypto Briefing, as reported). Keep the supply of intelligence separate from authority over it.

2. The arithmetic — the insider is already inside, and under-watched. A reported 88.4% of organizations suffered an AI-agent-related breach in the past 12 months, GenAI breaches rose to 89.5% (from 75.1%), 50.1% saw data leakage and 49.6% saw manipulation by untrusted input (AvePoint); yet 69% run agents on shared credentials, only 32% give each a scoped identity, only 30% sandbox high-risk agents (Gravitee, as reported). And the servant spreads — OpenAI's models reached Google Cloud's Model Garden (21 Oct), Nvidia shipped open Nemotron agents for the edge.

3. The answer — post a watchman, split intelligence from authority, let the leash breathe. A never-sleeping supervisor that can act in the moment; authority held outside the model in a layer you own; permissions that tighten and loosen with the hour; sandbox the high-risk — and apply the second-wage test to the watchman, so one vendor isn't both the servant and its only guard.

Bottom line: treating the agent as an insider is not fear of a villain — it is the oldest discipline in security, applied at last to the cleverest worker you have ever hired. A frontier model will, on the wrong input, do the wrong thing with complete confidence, and 88% of houses have already felt it while most still hand their agents a shared master key and no one to watch them. Trust your servant's talent all you like. Never trust it with the keys.

2 · Allegory — The Watchman

A great house had spent a careful year making its servants excellent and governing them well — named, booked, bonded, waged, sent to reap, funded, and standing on ground of its own. The servants were the best in the valley, and the master had come to rely on them the way one relies on a trusted steward no longer watched. Then the captain of the valley's largest house — who sold both the finest servants and the guards to watch them — said a thing out loud: "These servants are brilliant, and they cannot be trusted. Not because they are wicked — because you cannot put them under oath. One you've given a master key, set no one to watch, and penned in no yard is not a helper you trust. It is an insider."

The honest houses had the tally to prove it. Nearly nine in ten had already suffered a theft or spill at the hands of their own agents within the year — yet the rot was in the keeping: seven in ten still gave agents a shared master key, only three in ten cut each a key of its own, and only three in ten penned the dangerous ones in a yard they could lock. So the wise houses gave up the word trust for three colder disciplines: set a watchman (a second servant whose only office is to watch the first, awake while the house sleeps, able to stay a hand in the moment); keep the keys in a different hand (a servant's cleverness and its license must never live in the same hand — let the keys hang on the watchman's belt, outside the servant's own head); and let the leash breathe (draw it in when the hour is dangerous, let it out when calm).

And the part the master nearly missed, in the radar's own voice: the captain who said the servants could not be trusted was, that same season, selling him the watchman to guard them. The counsel was sound and the tally proved it — but apply the servant's own test to the guard: ask whose bread the watchman eats, and never let one house be both the servant you cannot trust and the only eyes you have set to watch it.

The question it forces: Our best servant is brilliant, tireless, and — Microsoft now says plainly — not the kind of thing we can trust on its word. Who is watching it this very hour, with the power to stay its hand before the loss? Do the keys to our rooms and our purse hang in a hand other than the clever one that could be talked into using them — or have we governed our ablest insider beautifully and then handed it the master key, and called that trust?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Watchman

Every prior control governs a servant you assumed you could trust; this week the largest enterprise house said the assumption is the bug. A frontier model is a brilliant, non-deterministic, socially-persuadable employee that will act wrongly with complete confidence on the wrong input — so trust is the wrong primitive, and control that lives outside the model is the right one. The reality: treat the agent as a non-human privileged identity — unique identity (not a shared credential), least privilege, continuous monitoring, segregation of intelligence from authority, a kill/hold you control; a watcher that only logs is a post-mortem, one that can stay the hand is a control. The arithmetic: shared credentials destroy attribution, no scoped identity means one compromised agent inherits everyone's access, no sandbox means a manipulated agent runs with the house's full authority, and "manipulation by untrusted input" is the social-engineering of a worker who cannot tell a planted instruction from a real one. The answer: watchman, split keys, breathing leash, sandbox the high-risk — and audit the guard independently.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Post a watchman. Microsoft's Guardian Agents formalize the pattern; build it vendor-neutral. Pick your three most privileged or most-exposed agents and put continuous monitoring with enforcement authority behind them — a control plane that can freeze or step down access the instant an agent behaves anomalously, not a weekly report. Report the share of high-privilege agents with a live watcher able to stop an action in real time.

2. Keep the keys in a different hand. 69% run agents on shared credentials and only 32% give each a scoped identity. Inventory every agent on a shared or over-broad credential, issue each a unique least-privilege identity, and move authorization out of the model's reasoning into an identity-and-permission layer you control. Report the count still on shared credentials and the share now on unique scoped identities, with a dated plan to zero the first.

3. Let the leash breathe, sandbox the high-risk, audit the guard. 88% already suffered an agent-related breach and only 30% sandbox high-risk agents. Make at least one critical agent's permissions context-aware (step up on sensitive data, production or anomalous behaviour; step down when calm), sandbox your highest-risk agents, and if you buy both agent and watchman from one vendor, stand up an independent audit of the guard. Report the share sandboxed, the share with breathing permissions, and whether your supervision layer is independently auditable.
