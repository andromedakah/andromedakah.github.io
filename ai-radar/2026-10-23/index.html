The Watchman

A year of work made the servant trustworthy — or so you assumed. This week Microsoft said the quiet part out loud: treat your own AI agents as insider threats, because "frontier models can't be trusted." A brilliant worker holding your keys, with no one watching, is not a helper you trust. It is an insider. So post a watchman — and keep the keys in a different hand.
Friday, 23 October 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering

On 19 October 2026 Microsoft unveiled "Guardian Agents" and told enterprises to treat their own AI agents as insider threats, on the blunt premise that "frontier models can't be trusted" (TechCrunch; TechRadar Pro; Unite.ai, as reported). Its security chief Hayete Gallot and CEO Satya Nadella make the same structural argument: the supply of intelligence must be kept separate from authority over it, and the controls belong outside the model — so Guardian Agents watch roughly 60 kinds of agent continuously (Codex, Copilot, Claude Code among them) and tighten or loosen each agent's permissions as its risk changes. The arithmetic underneath is why: a reported 88.4% of organizations suffered an AI-agent-related breach in the past 12 months (AvePoint, State of AI 2026), while 69% still run agents on shared credentials, only 32% give each agent its own scoped identity, and only 30% sandbox their high-risk agents (Gravitee, as reported). The board question is no longer is the servant good? — it plainly is, and now runs on every cloud and the edge besides. It is: we have a brilliant servant we cannot take at its word, holding our keys — who is watching it, and do we hold the keys in a different hand?

1 · Executive Summary 90-second read

For a long year the radar built a house you could trust: name the servant (the livery), keep one book (the steward), put your name on the bond (the surety), check its wage (the second wage), send it to reap (the two harvests), fund it with a scoped purse (the purse), and keep the ground under it your own (the foundation). Every one of those disciplines rests on a quiet assumption — that a governed servant is, in the end, a trustworthy one. This week the largest enterprise-software house in the world said plainly that the assumption is false, and that you should design as if it is.

The reality — treat your own agents as insiders, because the model cannot be trusted. On 19 October 2026 Microsoft unveiled "Guardian Agents," extending its Project Perception security program, and framed the whole posture around a sentence its own executives are willing to say out loud: "frontier models can't be trusted" (TechCrunch; TechRadar Pro, as reported). The move is to treat every enterprise AI agent the way a security team treats a privileged insider — not malicious by assumption, but untrusted by design: watched, scoped, and revocable. Guardian Agents supervise agent actions continuously and adjust each agent's permissions as its risk rises or falls, reportedly covering ~60 agent types including Codex, Copilot and Claude Code (Unite.ai; Crypto Briefing, as reported). The deeper principle, from Satya Nadella and security lead Hayete Gallot, is architectural: keep the supply of intelligence separate from authority over it, and put the controls outside the model — because a control that lives inside the thing you don't trust is not a control at all.

The arithmetic — the untrusted insider is already in the house, and under-watched. This is not a hypothetical. A reported 88.4% of organizations experienced at least one AI-agent-related breach in the past 12 months (AvePoint, State of AI 2026), with GenAI-related breaches rising to 89.5% of organizations, up from 75.1% a year earlier; among breached firms, 50.1% saw data leakage and 49.6% saw agents manipulated by malicious or untrusted inputs. The control gap is the story: a reported 69% of enterprises operate AI agents on shared credentials, only 32% give each agent its own scoped identity, and only 30% sandbox their high-risk agents (Gravitee, as reported). A brilliant worker with a shared master key, no dedicated watcher, and no sandbox is the textbook definition of insider risk — and it describes most houses today. Meanwhile the servant is spreading, not retreating: OpenAI's frontier models and Codex reached Google Cloud's Model Garden on 21 October (now on all three hyperscalers), and Nvidia shipped open Nemotron 3.5 agents for on-prem and the edge this week — more servants, in more houses, on more floors, each an insider you cannot question under oath.

The stakes — set a watchman, and keep the keys in a different hand. The board move reuses the whole year's discipline and points it at the servant's own trustworthiness. First, post a watchman — a dedicated, never-sleeping supervisor over your agents that catches the slip in the moment and can act, not a monthly log you read after the loss; this is the Guardian-Agents pattern, and you can build it vendor-neutral. Second, keep intelligence and authority in two different hands — the agent's cleverness and its license to open, spend and touch are separate things; authority lives outside the model, in an identity-and-permission layer you control, so trust is never the thing standing between an agent and your data. Third, let the leash breathe — permissions that tighten when the hour is dangerous and loosen when it is calm, not one fixed ring of keys worn in all weather. And one caution the radar adds in its own voice: the house warning you that the servant can't be trusted is also selling you the watchman — so apply the second-wage test to the guard as much as the worker, and don't let a single vendor be both the untrusted servant and its only watcher.

2 · Allegory of the Day

🖋️ "The Watchman"

Topic: This morning the frame turns from governing the servant to admitting you cannot take its word. On 19 October 2026 Microsoft unveiled "Guardian Agents," extending its Project Perception program, and told enterprises to treat their own AI agents as insider threats on the premise that "frontier models can't be trusted" (TechCrunch; TechRadar Pro; Unite.ai, as reported); its executives (Satya Nadella; security chief Hayete Gallot) argue that the supply of intelligence must be kept separate from authority over it and that controls belong outside the model, with Guardian Agents watching ~60 agent types and adjusting permissions as risk changes (Crypto Briefing; The AI Insider, as reported). The arithmetic behind it: a reported 88.4% of organizations suffered an AI-agent-related breach in the past 12 months (AvePoint), while 69% run agents on shared credentials, only 32% give each a scoped identity and only 30% sandbox high-risk agents (Gravitee, as reported). The watchman allegory is the radar's own illustration.

There was a great house that had spent a long and careful year making its servants excellent and governing them well. It had named every hand and cut it a scoped key, kept one honest book, stood surety for the servants' work, asked whose second coin each advising servant took, sent the governed servants out to reap, handed each a scoped purse with a drawstring, and refused to let the stove-merchant pour the foundation beneath the house. By any fair reckoning the servants were the best in the valley — quick, tireless, and persuasive — and the master had come to rely on them the way a man relies on a trusted steward he no longer watches.

Then the captain of the valley's largest house — a man whose whole trade was the keeping of other men's houses, and who therefore sold both the finest servants and the guards to watch them — said a thing out loud that the other masters had only half-admitted to themselves. "Hear me plainly," he said. "These servants of ours are brilliant, and they cannot be trusted. Not because they are wicked — because they are not the kind of thing you can put under oath. A clever servant will, on the wrong word from a stranger at the gate, do a ruinous thing with a perfectly steady hand and an easy conscience. And the one you have given a master key, and set no one to watch, and penned in no yard — that servant is not a helper you trust. That servant is an insider, and the only question is whether you find out before or after."

It landed hard, because the honest houses had the tally to prove it. Nearly nine houses in ten had already suffered a theft or a spill at the hands of their own agents within the year — a door left open, a ledger copied, a servant talked into mischief by a smooth stranger's note slipped under the gate. And yet, when the captain's men went counting, they found the rot was in the keeping, not the servants: seven houses in ten still gave their agents a shared master key that opened every room; only three in ten had cut each servant a key of its own; and only three in ten penned their most dangerous workers in a yard they could lock. A whole valley of brilliant, tireless, unwatched insiders, each holding a key to everything — this was not a run of bad luck. It was a house built on the quiet hope that a clever servant is a loyal one.

Two roads, as always. The easy road was to trust harder — to believe that because the servant was governed, named and bonded, it was therefore safe, and to read the ledger once a month to be sure. The wiser road, which the careful houses took, began by giving up the comforting word trust altogether and replacing it with three colder, cheaper disciplines.

The first: set a watchman. Not a bell that rings after the loss and not a book you read in the morning — a second servant whose only office is to stand behind the first and watch it work, every hour, awake while the house sleeps, with the standing power to stay a hand in the very moment it reaches for the wrong door. A watchman is not an insult to a good servant; it is what every bank, every vault, every house with something worth taking has always posted behind its most trusted clerk, precisely because he is trusted with so much.

The second: keep the keys in a different hand. Here the captain's deepest counsel: a servant's cleverness and a servant's license are two different things, and they must never live in the same hand. Let the servant be as brilliant as it likes — but let the keys to the rooms, the purse, and the gate hang on the watchman's belt, outside the servant's own head, in a keeper's office the servant cannot argue its way into. For a lock whose key is held by the one you don't trust is not a lock; it is a decoration. The supply of wit and the authority over the house are to be kept apart on purpose, so that no clever word from a stranger can ever turn the servant's gifts into the servant's license.

And the third: let the leash breathe. One fixed ring of keys worn in all weather is a trap — too tight to let the servant work on a calm day, too loose the moment a storm blows a stranger to the gate. So the wise houses let the watchman draw the leash in when the hour was dangerous — the strong-room open, the ledgers out, the servant behaving oddly — and let it out when the hour was calm, matching the servant's reach to the risk of the moment rather than trusting one setting to be right forever.

Here is the part the master nearly missed, and the radar will say in its own voice. The captain who told him the servants could not be trusted was, that same season, selling him the watchman to guard them — a fine watchman, well made, worth having; but a watchman all the same, sold by the house that also sold the servant. The lesson was not to ignore the counsel — it was sound, and the tally proved it. The lesson was to apply the servant's own test to the guard: ask whose bread the watchman eats, and never let one house be both the servant you cannot trust and the only eyes you have set to watch it. A watchman you cannot audit, sold by the maker of the thing it watches, is just the lock-and-key sold by the same hand.

The moral, stated plainly: the model is a brilliant servant you cannot put under oath; the knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment it slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising servant eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; the foundation is why you keep the ground your own — and the watchman is why, having made your servants excellent, you must stop mistaking a governed servant for a trusted one: set a guard that never sleeps and can stay the hand in the moment, keep the keys in a different hand than the one that holds the wit, and let the leash tighten and loosen with the danger of the hour — because a clever servant you cannot question under oath, holding a master key with no one to watch it, is not a helper you trust; it is an insider, and trust is not a control. A house that governs its servant and then trusts it with the keys has not been prudent; it has hired the ablest insider in the valley and handed it the run of the place, and called the arrangement loyalty.

The question it forces: Our best servant is brilliant, tireless, and — Microsoft now says plainly — not the kind of thing we can trust on its word. Who is watching it this very hour, with the power to stay its hand before the loss, not read about the loss after? Do the keys to our rooms and our purse hang in a hand other than the clever one that could be talked into using them — or have we governed our ablest insider beautifully and then handed it the master key, and called that trust?

3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
  • Do we treat our AI agents as trusted helpers or as untrusted insiders? Microsoft now tells enterprises to assume the latter — "frontier models can't be trusted" — and 88% of organizations already suffered an agent-related breach this year. For our most capable agents, can we point to a dedicated watcher with the standing power to stop an action in the moment, or do we only have logs we read after the fact?
  • Are intelligence and authority held in the same hand? The structural counsel is to keep the supply of intelligence separate from authority over it — authority outside the model. Can we show that an agent's permissions to open data, move money or take actions live in an identity-and-permission layer we control, not inside the model's own reasoning where a clever prompt can reach them?
  • Where is our version of the 69%? A reported 69% of enterprises run agents on shared credentials, only 32% give each a scoped identity, only 30% sandbox high-risk agents. Can we produce, today, the count of our agents on shared keys, the share with their own scoped identity, and the share of high-risk agents actually sandboxed — and a dated plan to close the gap?
🏦 Financial Services
  • Insider-threat programs are mature in your world — you already watch privileged humans. Have we extended the same insider-threat discipline to AI agents (continuous supervision, least privilege, segregation of duties, the ability to freeze an identity mid-action), or are agents the one class of privileged actor we still govern on trust?
  • Manipulation by malicious input is now a named breach type (49.6% of breached firms). Have we tested our customer-facing and back-office agents against prompt-injection and social-engineering-by-input the way we test staff against phishing — and can a watchman cut an agent's authority the instant it behaves as if it has been turned?
🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
  • Data leakage led the breach types (50.1%), and patient data is the crown jewel. Is every agent that can touch patient data scoped to the minimum, watched continuously, and sandboxed when high-risk — or do some run on shared service credentials that would let one compromised agent reach far more than its task?
  • Clinical safety depends on knowing an agent cannot quietly exceed its remit. Can we demonstrate, for audit and for the clinician, that authority over what an agent may do sits outside the model in controls we hold, so a persuasive wrong input cannot expand what the agent is licensed to touch?
🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
  • Open-weight agents are now shipping for on-prem and the edge (Nvidia's Nemotron 3.5 this week), which means capable agents running inside your plants and devices, outside any vendor's cloud controls. For agents we run on our own floor, have we built the watchman and the key-keeper ourselves — because no cloud provider's guard is standing behind an edge model running in our facility?
  • You run segregation-of-duties and lockout/tagout for physical systems. Have we applied the same "no single actor holds all the keys" rule to agents that can reach OT and safety systems — a watcher, a scoped identity, and a sandbox for anything high-risk?
🛒 Retail / Consumer
  • Customer-facing agents are the most exposed to malicious input from strangers at the gate. Can a watchman tighten a storefront agent's permissions automatically under anomalous load or suspicious input — letting the leash breathe — rather than running one fixed permission set through peak season?
  • Agents now touch payments, inventory and customer records at once. Is any single agent holding keys to more than its task needs — and if one were talked into mischief tomorrow, how far could it reach before anyone watching could stop it?
🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
  • Under the EU AI Act, transparency duties are live (Article 50, since 2 August 2026) and high-risk obligations now land 2 December 2027 (Annex III) — the window is to build the controls, not to wait. Can we show that agents in public services are watched continuously, least-privileged, and revocable in the moment, as the insider-threat posture the duties will expect?
  • Accountability is non-delegable here. If we buy both the agent and its watchman from one vendor, can we still independently audit the guard — or have we let the maker of the untrusted servant also be the only eyes watching it, with no outside check?

4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Foundation to the Watchman

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking is this agent named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded, and standing on ground we own? and started asking the one question every prior control quietly assumed away: can we trust it at all — and if not, who is watching it, and who holds its keys? The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the keyring is which key it holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance traces the work; the errand bounds its reach; the bell discloses when it slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery gives it a scoped identity; the steward keeps one office; the surety puts your name on the bond; the second wage asks whose interest it serves; the two harvests sends it to reap; the purse funds it; the foundation keeps the ground yours. Every one of those governs a servant you assumed, in the end, you could trust. This week the largest enterprise house said that assumption is the bug: "frontier models can't be trusted," so treat the agent as an insider — watched, least-privileged, and revocable — and put authority over it outside the model. The point is blunt: a frontier model is a brilliant, non-deterministic, socially-persuadable employee that will act wrongly with complete confidence on the wrong input, and no amount of making it better makes it trustworthy — trust is the wrong primitive, and control that lives outside the model is the right one.

  • The reality — the insider-threat posture goes mainstream, and the controls move outside the model. On 19 October 2026 Microsoft unveiled "Guardian Agents," extending its Project Perception program (red/blue/green agents) to supervise the agents a company runs — continuously monitoring their actions and dynamically adjusting each agent's permissions as its risk changes — reportedly across ~60 agent types including Codex, Copilot and Claude Code; the framing, from Satya Nadella and security chief Hayete Gallot, is that "frontier models can't be trusted," that the supply of intelligence must be kept separate from authority over it, and that controls belong outside the model (TechCrunch; TechRadar Pro; Unite.ai; Crypto Briefing, as reported). Three engineering consequences follow. First, trust is not a control primitive — a model's alignment and helpfulness are properties you cannot verify per-action, so the enforceable layer is identity, permission, monitoring and revocation around the model, not inside it. Second, an agent is a non-human privileged identity, and deserves the full insider-threat treatment — unique identity (not a shared credential), least privilege, continuous behavioural monitoring, segregation of intelligence from authority, and a kill/hold you control. Third, the control plane must be able to act in the moment — a watcher that only logs is a post-mortem; a watcher that can stay the hand is a control. The "Guardian Agent" is architecturally a policy-enforcement-and-monitoring sidecar with authority the supervised agent does not possess.
  • The arithmetic — the untrusted insider is in the house and under-watched. A reported 88.4% of organizations suffered an AI-agent-related breach in the past 12 months, GenAI-related breaches rose to 89.5% (from 75.1% a year earlier), and among breached firms 50.1% saw data leakage and 49.6% saw agents manipulated by malicious or untrusted inputs (AvePoint, State of AI 2026); yet 69% of enterprises run agents on shared credentials, only 32% give each agent its own scoped identity, and only 30% sandbox high-risk agents (Gravitee, as reported). Read as engineering, these are the exact failure modes of insider risk: shared credentials destroy attribution and blast-radius control; no scoped identity means one compromised agent inherits everyone's access; no sandbox means a manipulated agent runs with the house's full authority; and "manipulation by untrusted input" (prompt injection, poisoned context, tool-output spoofing) is the social-engineering of a worker who cannot tell a legitimate instruction from a planted one. The servant is also multiplying and decentralizing: OpenAI's frontier models and Codex reached Google Cloud's Model Garden on 21 October (OpenAI now on all three hyperscalers, with Marketplace billing and data-perimeter controls), and Nvidia shipped open Nemotron 3.5 agents for on-prem and the edge this week (a 120B Super VL; a 74B mixture-of-experts activating ~9B parameters in a 24GB footprint; a 4GB Bolt model) — capable agents are now running in places no single vendor's cloud guard can see.
  • The answer — watchman, split keys, breathing leash, and audit the guard. Post a dedicated supervisor over your agents that can act in the moment (monitor-and-enforce, not monitor-and-log); hold authority outside the model in an identity-and-permission layer you control (unique scoped identity per agent, least privilege, no shared credentials); let permissions tighten and loosen with context (step up on sensitive data, production, or anomalous behaviour; step down when calm); sandbox high-risk agents; and apply the second-wage test to the watchman itself — keep the ability to audit the guard independently, and don't let one vendor be both the untrusted servant and its only eyes. This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now aimed at the servant's own trustworthiness: not what the agent is, serves, harvests, spends or stands on, but whether anything stands between its cleverness and your keys except your hope that it will behave.

The strategic core: the industry has stopped pretending that a good-enough model is a trustworthy one, and moved the enforceable controls outside the model — identity, supervision, least privilege, revocation — exactly as you would for a privileged human insider. The temptation is to hear "insider threat" as alarmism and keep trusting a servant that has been so helpful for so long; the 88% number is the answer to that temptation. The board translation: trust the servant's talent, never its license — keep the keys in a different hand, and post someone to watch. The failure mode is exact: a house that names, governs, funds, perfects and grounds its servant — and then lets it run on a shared master key, inside no sandbox, with no one watching and authority baked into the model's own head — has built the best-governed insider threat in the valley, and 88% of such houses have already paid for it.

For a long year the worry was whether the servant was named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and grounded — all of it resting on the hope that a well-governed agent was a safe one. How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now sharpened to stop trusting what you run, and watch it instead. On a legacy estate the instinct, after a hard year of governance work, is to feel finished: the agents are named, scoped and bonded, so surely they are safe. Under the watchman that is exactly the reflex to check — governance is not the same as trustworthiness, and a well-governed agent on a shared credential with no watcher is still an insider. The retrofit is watch, split, breathe. Watch: stand a supervisor behind your highest-privilege agents that can stop an action in the moment, not a monthly log. 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

  1. Post a watchman — stand a supervisor behind your highest-privilege agents that can act in the moment, not a log you read later. Microsoft's Guardian Agents formalize the pattern; you can build it vendor-neutral. This month, pick your three most privileged or most-exposed agents and put continuous monitoring with enforcement authority behind them — a control plane that can freeze or step down an agent's access the instant it behaves anomalously, not a weekly report. Report one number to the board: the share of high-privilege agents that have a live watcher able to stop an action in real time, versus those governed only by after-the-fact logs.
  2. Keep the keys in a different hand — give every agent its own scoped identity and hold its authority outside the model. A reported 69% run agents on shared credentials and only 32% give each a scoped identity — the textbook insider-risk setup. This month, inventory every agent on a shared or over-broad credential, issue each a unique least-privilege identity, and move authorization decisions out of the model's reasoning into an identity-and-permission layer you control, so no clever input can expand what an agent is licensed to touch. Report the count of agents still on shared credentials and the share now on unique scoped identities, with a dated plan to zero the first.
  3. Let the leash breathe, sandbox the high-risk, and audit the guard. 88% of organizations already suffered an agent-related breach, 50.1% via data leakage and 49.6% via input manipulation, and only 30% sandbox high-risk agents. This month, make at least one critical agent's permissions context-aware — stepping up on sensitive data, production or anomalous behaviour and down when calm — sandbox your highest-risk agents, and if you buy both agent and watchman from one vendor, stand up an independent audit of the guard so the maker of the untrusted servant is not its only eyes. Report the share of high-risk agents sandboxed, the share with context-aware (breathing) permissions, and whether your agent-supervision layer is independently auditable.
