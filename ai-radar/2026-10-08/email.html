The Night Watch

Thursday, 8 October 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

A year of controls governed a servant you set a task and then watch. This week the market shipped the servant that never sleeps — a tireless engine-room worker too cheap to turn off (Claude Haiku 5.5, 7 Oct) and always-on agents that no longer wait for a prompt (OpenAI's Dots; Microsoft's Scout). A control you only check by day does not govern a servant that works by night. Post the watch, keep the night servant read-only until told, leash the engine-room worker.

1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from governing the servant that works by day to watching the servant that never sleeps — because the market just made the tireless worker too cheap to turn off and made always-on the default shape of an agent.

1. The reality — the tireless night-worker is too cheap to turn off. On 7 October 2026 Anthropic shipped Claude Haiku 5.5 — a small, fast model with a 1M-token context window at a reported $0.10/$0.50 per million tokens (→ $0.50/$2.50 above a 100K prompt), its first Haiku with an "effort" dial, built for routing, context compaction, parallel subagents and browser/desktop automation (Anthropic; MarkTechPost; Artificial Analysis, as reported). At a tenth of a cent per thousand input tokens, the economics of leaving an agent running — not invoking it, running it — have crossed a line.

2. The collision — the market made "always-on" the default. OpenAI's Dots (DevDay, 29 Sep) are always-on agents, each with its own cloud computer and browser across 4,000+ apps, that run proactive research but stay read-only until tasked and auto-review consequential actions. Microsoft's Scout, its first "Autopilot," runs continuously on schedules and triggers, each with its own Entra identity and task-scoped credentials redacted from logs — Microsoft's Omar Shahine calling trust the "most critical and unknown" problem (SiliconANGLE; Help Net Security, as reported). The servant no longer waits for you.

3. The answer — post a watch over the servant that works by night. Stand up always-on monitoring that keeps the agents' clock; keep idle and proactive agents read-only until a scoped task authorizes action; and give even the cheap engine-room worker its own revocable identity, budget and kill-switch. The house now runs while everyone sleeps, and an unwatched night is where the incident happens.

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each governed a servant you set a task and then watch — the night watch is about the hours no one is watching. The market made the tireless worker cheap enough to leave running and made "always-on" the shape of the product; the trap is that cheap, always-on autonomy feels like pure productivity until the night a proactive agent, or a swarm of subagents, does something no one saw. Let the house run all night — never let it run unwatched.

2 · Allegory — The Night Watch

A great house had spent a careful year making its servants excellent — named, booked, bonded, waged, sent to reap, funded, standing on ground it owned. Every one of those disciplines rested on one quiet habit: the master watched his servants work by day, and sent them to their beds at night. This week a merchant brought a new under-servant — small, tireless, and so cheap (a tenth of a penny for a thousand errands) that the master, doing the sum, set it to work and never sent it to bed.

The same week, two great companies brought a steward that never sleeps — one that wakes on its own to a bell, a date, a thing it notices, and acts without being asked. The master, pleased, asked the colder question: while I sleep and it is awake with my keys, who is watching it? To their credit, both companies had already built the answer in: the first let its always-waking steward only look, never touch, until a task was set, and sent anything touching money or the outside world past a second eye; the second gave each steward its own name and seal, scoped its keys to the task and wiped them after — and still said the honest thing: the hardest problem, unsolved, is whether you can trust a servant that works while you are not watching.

So the master learned the night watch: post a watch that keeps the hours you do not; let the night servant only look until it is told; and give even the cheap tireless hand its own seal and a cord you can pull in the dark. For the danger of the servant that never sleeps is not that it is wicked — it is that it is useful, cheap, and unwatched, and those three together are how the quiet catastrophe is made.

The question it forces: Our agents no longer wait for us — the newest are always on, and the tireless ones are too cheap to turn off. Who is watching them at three in the morning? Do idle and proactive agents default to observe-only until a scoped task puts a tool in their hand? Does every always-on agent carry its own identity, a budget it cannot exceed, and a kill-switch we can reach when no one is at a desk?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Night Watch

Every prior control governs a servant that acts on a task and then stops — one you supervise by day. This week the market shipped the servant that never stops. What changed is not what the agent can do but when it does it: unattended, overnight, continuously — and a governance architecture built to watch servants by day has no eye for the hours they now work. The reality: Claude Haiku 5.5 makes the engine-room worker (routing, context compaction, parallel subagents, browser automation) negligible to leave running, so it will proliferate — and cheap plus continuous means unwatched by default. The collision: OpenAI's Dots and Microsoft's Scout both chose the same three defenses from day one — observe-only until tasked, a second check on consequential actions, and a scoped, named, logged identity per agent — which is the engineering signal that the people closest to always-on autonomy don't trust it with standing writes and no watch. The answer: post the watch (monitoring on the agents' clock), keep it read-only until told (observe-only defaults, consequential actions gated), and leash the worker (per-agent identity, budget, kill-switch).

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Post the watch. The market made autonomy always-on and the engine-room worker too cheap to turn off, so your agents act in hours your dashboards never watched. Inventory every agent that can act unprompted — on a schedule, a trigger, or its own initiative — and put per-agent logging, anomaly alarms and an overnight human escalation path behind them. Report the share of always-on agents covered by monitoring that runs when your people don't.

2. Keep the night servant read-only until told. The serious builders shipped this by default — Dots stay read-only until tasked; Scout scopes credentials to the task. Set idle and proactive agents to observe-only — no writes, no sends, no spend — until a scoped task authorizes a specific tool, and gate consequential or outbound actions behind an auto-review or human sign-off. Report the share that can't take a consequential action without a scoped task and a second check.

3. Leash the engine-room worker. It's precisely the cheap, high-volume subagent running continuously that no one thinks to watch. Give every always-on agent — down to the cheapest subagent — its own revocable identity, a hard per-agent and aggregate budget, and a kill-switch an on-call human can trigger without a daytime approval chain; then test pulling the cord. Report the share with a named identity, a budget cap and a tested kill-switch.
