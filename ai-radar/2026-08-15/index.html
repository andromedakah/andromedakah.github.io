2 · Allegory of the Day

🧵 "The Last Mile"

Topic: On 13 August 2026, OpenAI made two distribution moves on the same day. It announced a strategic partnership with IBM to embed its frontier models (GPT-5.6, Codex, ChatGPT Work) into IBM Consulting Advantage, standing up a dedicated "OpenAI Practice," certifying tens of thousands of IBM's ~150,000 consultants, and sending forward-deployed engineers into clients in financial services, government, telecom and retail — and it named Dali Rajic (ex-Wiz President & COO, ex-Zscaler, ex-AppDynamics) as Chief Revenue Officer, its second in under a year, to bring enterprise go-to-market discipline. The company already has a billion weekly users and two million businesses. The lesson: the model is a commodity and even grounded reliability is not enough; the scarce, valuable layer is distribution — the channel, the trusted relationships and the people who carry AI the last mile into a real enterprise. The board's discipline is to decide who owns that mile.

Picture a valley famous, for a hundred years, for the finest cloth in the world. Its master weavers weren't merchants and didn't care to be; they cared about the loom. And they were sublime at it — every season the bolts came off the frames a little finer, a little cheaper, until cloth that had once dressed only kings could be had by any householder in the valley. The weavers were proud of this, and they were right to be. But there is a cruel fact about a bolt of perfect cloth: in the weaver's own village, it clothes no one. A bolt in the valley is not a sale. Between the loom and the buyer's back lies a long, unglamorous distance — a road, a river-crossing, a customs house, a city full of strangers who have never heard the weaver's name and would not trust it if they had.

And so the valley learned who actually grew rich. It was not the finest weaver. It was the great trading house at the mouth of the valley — the one that owned the roads and the pack-trains, that kept a factor in every distant city, that had spent two generations earning the one thing no loom can weave: the trust of the buyer, who would take its bolts sight unseen because the house had never once delivered short or late. The house did not weave a single thread. It owned the last mile — the mile from the road's end to the customer's hand — and that mile, it turned out, was where the money lived. Cloth was a commodity, ordered by the crate and cheaper every year. Reach was scarce, and reach was dear.

Now here is what the valley saw this season, and it startled everyone. The most celebrated weaving-house of the age — the one whose cloth every household already knew, whose name a thousand villages spoke each week — did two things on a single day. First, it went to the greatest trading house in the land and handed over its whole vintage of cloth to travel that house's roads, under that house's factors, carried the last mile by that house's carters into the guarded warehouses of the richest and most careful buyers — the banks, the ministries, the great mills — the customers a weaver can never reach on charm alone. And second, on that very same day, it hired away the most feared road-master in the realm — a man who had built another house's routes across a continent — and set him over its own wagons and its own factors. The weaver, in one stroke, both rented the greatest distribution in the land and bought the muscle to build its own. Read plainly, it was a confession: we can weave better than anyone alive, and it is still not enough to reach the buyer's door.

There was, as ever, a gate on the road. The realm had lately appointed a customs authority at the crossing that would not pass an unlabeled bolt or an unlicensed carter — every wagon's manifest had to be honest, every consignment marked, every run entered in a book that could be audited. So the wise houses understood the last mile as three things at once: the route (who physically carries the goods to the buyer), the trust (whose name the buyer will take sight unseen), and the papers (what the authority at the gate will pass). A weaver with the finest cloth and none of the three had a warehouse full of bolts and an empty ledger. The trading house with all three grew richer than any weaver — on cloth it never made.

The moral: when the product becomes a commodity — abundant, cheap, nearly perfected — the advantage stops being the product and becomes the distribution: the route that carries it the last mile, the trust that makes a careful buyer say yes, and the papers the gate will pass. The intelligence is not the bottleneck anymore, and neither, quite, is the pilotage — the mile to the customer is. Decide, deliberately, whether you own that mile or rent it. Because the finest cloth in the valley, like the finest engine and the finest pilot, still clothes no one until someone carries it the last mile to a buyer who trusts the name on the bolt.

The question it forces: The most celebrated model maker on earth rented another firm's ~150,000-person delivery army and hired a new revenue chief on the same day — because a billion users is not the same as an enterprise deployment that sticks. For each of our AI bets, who actually walks the last mile into our operation: our own people and process, a system integrator, or the model vendor's forward-deployed engineers — and have we decided that on purpose, priced it, and kept the trust and the papers in our own hands?

🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
  • Who walks our last mile — and did we choose them on purpose? OpenAI rented IBM's ~150,000-consultant channel and hired a CRO on the same day. For each material AI initiative, name the party that actually carries it into production — our people, a systems integrator, or the vendor's forward-deployed engineers — and put a cost and an owner against that mile. If no one can name it, we have bought an engine with no road to the customer.
  • The gap that stalls AI is pilot-to-production, and it is a delivery problem, not a model problem. Where are our AI efforts actually stuck — on capability, or on the unglamorous work of redesigning a real workflow and making it stick in a regulated shop? Almost always the second — and the second is bought with people and process, not a bigger model.
  • Distribution is a strategy, not a purchase order. IBM's edge here is two generations of enterprise trust and delivery muscle, not a model. What is our equivalent moat — the relationships and delivery capability a rival cannot rent overnight — and are we investing in it or letting a vendor own it for us?
🏦 Financial Services
  • Your regulators, your controls and your incumbency are a distribution moat. IBM is pointing its OpenAI Practice straight at financial services for exactly that reason. Are we using our own trusted position to deliver AI into the business — or ceding the last mile, and the client relationship, to whoever brings the model?
  • Forward-deployed engineers inside a bank touch trades, limits and client data. Before we let any vendor's or integrator's people carry AI into production, can we show the license and the auditable log the EU AI Act now expects — and do we keep the exit rights if that channel is repriced or acquired?
🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
  • The last mile in clinical and discovery AI runs through your protocols, provenance and clinicians — not a model demo. Are we investing in the delivery capability (change management, validation, the people who make it stick at the bedside) rather than assuming a cleverer model deploys itself?
  • A partner's forward-deployed team is fast, but it walks out with the know-how. For any patient-facing or discovery workflow, are we building our own delivery muscle and retaining the institutional knowledge — or renting a mile we will never own?
🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
  • You have always known that the value in a supply chain migrates to integration and logistics — the last mile — not the raw good. Are we applying that instinct to AI: investing in the delivery and change-management layer that lands a workflow on the floor, rather than the horsepower spec of the model?
  • Pilots that never leave the lab are this decade's shelfware. What is our one high-value workflow where we have actually funded the last mile — the integrator, the retraining, the process redesign — and can we show it running in production, not in a slide?
🛒 Retail / Consumer
  • Your customer relationships and your channel are the last mile — the thing pure model makers must rent. Are we using that to deliver AI into the storefront and the supply chain ourselves, or handing the customer relationship to whoever ships the model?
  • Speed to production is margin. Have we resourced the delivery mile — the people and process that turn an agent from a demo into a reliable, disclosed, labeled part of the shopping experience the EU AI Act now governs?
🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
  • Public value is delivered through your institutions, records and trust — the ultimate last mile — not through procuring the largest model. Are we buying delivery capability and retaining accountability, or outsourcing the mile that touches citizens to a vendor's forward-deployed team?
  • The customs authority is real. With the EU AI Act's transparency duties live and the AITSC standard forming, can we produce the license and the log for every party — vendor, integrator or our own staff — that carries AI the last mile to a citizen, and do we hold the exit rights to stay accountable?

4 · Technical Deep-Dive — Own the Last Mile, Not Just the Engine

Read the stack, again, as layers priced very differently — and this week the market pointed past the priced pilotage to the layer beyond it: distribution. At the bottom is the engine — the raw model, now so abundant that three frontier releases landed on a single Thursday. Above it are the rails — the agent infrastructure that lets a model reach tools. Above that is the pilotage — the context-and-reliability layer that yesterday's edition watched take $190B. And above that, nearest the money and the hardest to buy, is the last mile: the channel, the trusted relationships, the delivery people and the go-to-market discipline that carry a working model into a real, regulated enterprise and make it stick. The engineering point this week is blunt: OpenAI has the first three layers and it still rented the fourth — twice, in one day.

  • The engine — abundant, commoditizing, shipping three-at-a-time. On 13 August alone, Google shipped Gemini 3.7 Flash, DeepSeek shipped V4-Pro-0813, and OpenAI shipped a GPT-5.6 "Sol" Ultrafast mode; open-weight Z.ai GLM-5.3 followed on the 14th, joining Opus 5, Grok 4.6 ($2/$6 per 1M) and Kimi K3. The cadence is the commoditization: when three labs release frontier-class engines in a day, the engine is not the moat. Enterprises are not paying up for the single cleverest brain (last week's Ramp read: Anthropic's flagship Fable 5 ≈ 6% of its tokens). The engine is not where the durable money is.
  • The rails and the pilotage — enabling, and priced, but not sufficient. The Model Context Protocol's 2026-07-28 specification went stateless, with ~500M SDK downloads/month and TypeScript and Python SDKs each past 1 billion — standard plumbing, trending to free. The pilotage layer (context + reliability) is real and valuable — it took Databricks to $190B. But this week's tell is that even OpenAI's own pilotage story (Frontier's semantic layer, eval loops, audit logs) did not spare it from needing someone else's delivery force. Enabling layers are necessary; they are not the mile to the customer.
  • The last mile — distribution, where OpenAI just spent. This is the layer the two 13-August moves bought. IBM × OpenAI: GPT-5.6, Codex and ChatGPT Work embedded in IBM Consulting Advantage; a dedicated OpenAI Practice; tens of thousands of IBM's ~150,000 consultants certified; forward-deployed engineers into financial services, government, telecom and retail; cybersecurity via OpenAI's Daybreak Cyber Partner Program plus IBM Autonomous Security. And Dali Rajic as CRO — a Wiz/Zscaler enterprise-sales operator over OpenAI's own revenue org. Forrester's read: IBM "validates OpenAI's push-pull-partner enterprise strategy." Distribution is the scarce, priced layer — and the model maker rented and bought it in a single day.

The strategic core: intelligence is abundant; grounding it is priced; delivering it is scarce. For a month the misread was "buy the cleverest model and you have deployed AI." Yesterday's correction was "ground it and make it reliable." This week's is sharper still: even a grounded, reliable model does not walk itself into a bank. "It benchmarks highest / it's grounded in our data" is not the answer to "who redesigns the procurement workflow, in our regulated shop, and makes it stick, with a log we can defend"; "we resourced the last mile — the channel, the delivery people and the trust" is the answer.

Why owning the last mile beats owning the engine

Every layer of the stack this month presumed the brain was cheap and swappable — and this week three frontier engines shipped in a day to prove it. The reason the engine and the last mile belong in the same picture is that they are complements the market keeps confusing for the whole: the finest engine in the world does nothing in a warehouse, and a modest engine delivered the last mile into a live workflow earns its keep every day. Read them together and you can answer the CFO's real question. The correct read of this week is not "IBM sells more consulting" but "the value sits in the mile to the customer — so decide, deliberately, who walks it for you."

How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — be deliberate about what you own, rent and finance. On legacy estates the danger is spending the AI budget on the engine and the pilotage while treating the last mile as an afterthought — and then wondering why the pilots never reach production. The retrofit is a delivery exercise. Name the carrier: for every initiative, decide who walks the mile — internal team, integrator, or the vendor's forward-deployed engineers — and price it. Keep the trust and the know-how: where you use a partner's people, structure the engagement so the institutional knowledge and the client relationship stay yours, not theirs. Keep the papers: the EU AI Act's disclosure and labeling duties are live and the AITSC standard is forming — every party that carries AI the last mile into production must be licensed and logged, whoever employs them. And keep the engine swappable underneath — re-benchmark Gemini 3.7 Flash, DeepSeek V4-Pro, GLM-5.3 and the next release freely, because the brain is the commodity and the delivery is the moat.

The clean mental model: The model is the engine — abundant, cheap, three-a-day, never your moat. The rails are the plumbing — MCP and its kin, trending to free. The pilotage is context and reliability — priced, and necessary. The last mile is distribution — the channel, the trust and the delivery people who carry a working model into your operation and make it stick, and it is the layer OpenAI rented and bought in a single day. Own or rent that mile on purpose — because the finest engine and the finest pilot still deliver nothing if no one carries them to your dock.

7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

  1. Name the carrier for every AI bet, and price the mile. For each material initiative, write down who actually carries it into production — internal team, systems integrator, or the vendor's forward-deployed engineers — and put a cost and an owner against that mile. Report it to the board beside the model choice. OpenAI rented IBM's ~150,000-consultant channel and hired a CRO on a single day; if the most celebrated model maker on earth resources distribution that hard, an unnamed, unfunded last mile is why your pilots never reach production. The scarce layer is delivery — treat it as a line item, not an afterthought.
  2. Keep the trust, the relationship and the know-how in your own hands. Where you use a partner's delivery force, structure it so the institutional knowledge and the client relationship stay yours — not walked out the door when the engagement ends. IBM's moat is two generations of enterprise trust; do not let a vendor quietly become the trusted face of AI to your own customers and regulators. Rent the mile if you must, but own the map.
  3. License and log every party that walks the mile — it's already law. The customs authority is live: whoever carries AI into production — vendor, integrator or your own staff — falls under the EU AI Act's disclosure and labeling duties, and the audit log must name them. Track the AITSC standard as it forms. A forward-deployed engineer touching a regulated workflow without a license and a log is not a shortcut to production; it is a liability with a deployment date. Make the last mile auditable before it touches the cargo.
