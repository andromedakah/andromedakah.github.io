1 · Executive Summary 90-second read

For a year the radar improved the servant — the livery named it, the steward kept one book, the surety put your name on the bond, the purse funded it, the foundation asked who owns the ground. This morning the axis moves to the household roll: the agent is being hired, not just used. The market made the shift concrete in the last few days, and it points two ways at once — toward real governance, and toward a new and subtler place for accountability to hide.

The move — the agent gets a desk, a mailbox, and a manager. On 19 November 2026 Microsoft's Foundry gave agents first-class corporate identity: every agent receives an Entra agent identity at creation, and an "autopilot" agent gets a full agent user account — a genuine Entra directory object with its own mailbox, calendar, Teams presence, OneDrive, a manager, and a place in the org chart — administered with the same controls you apply to employees (Entra, Purview, Defender). The New Stack's headline captured it: "Microsoft's new Copilot agents get their own email, calendar — and a place in the org chart" (as reported). Workday pushed the same direction commercially, agreeing to acquire C1.ai (announced 13 October 2026, closing expected by end of January) to extend its Agent System of Record into "the identity governance layer for people, money, and agents" (Workday; CIO; diginomica, as reported). The reflex is correct: you cannot apply least privilege, logging or offboarding to a hand that has no name, and the gap is real — 88% of organizations reported an AI-agent security incident in the past year while only ~22% treat agents as independent identities (Gravitee/OWASP, as reported).

The catch — a member of the household is also a member's attack surface, and a manager is where blame slides. Giving the servant a name on the roll gives a stranger a letter-slot to write to, and gives a thief a standing key to covet. The scale is already past human: machine (non-human) identities outnumber human identities by roughly 82 to 1 and legacy IAM was never built for it (CyberArk, via VentureBeat, as reported); IAM teams are responsible for only 44% of an organization's machine identities (Gartner survey of 335 IAM leaders), while 94% of organizations report machine identities rising, driven by AI and agents (Gartner, as reported). The breach is moving onto the new hire: Gartner projects that by 2028, 25% of breaches will vector through agent-based attack surfaces, driven by poor machine-identity management and weak context-aware policy (Gartner, June 2026, as reported); 58% of organizations already report an incident involving AI-linked machine identities (SailPoint, citing Gartner, as reported). And the manager is the subtle danger: MIT's Initiative on the Digital Economy found that putting agents on the org chart "creates as many problems as it solves" — managers shifted accountability away from themselves and toward the AI (as reported), while Deloitte insists an agent belongs on the chart only when a named human is unambiguously responsible for its role, development and outcomes, because an agent cannot be held accountable for results the way a person can (as reported).

The stakes — hire the servant, but keep a human name nailed to the roll. The move onto the roll is right; the failure modes are specific. First, provision the agent like a new hire, not a super-user — a defined role, only the keys its task needs, and a joiner-mover-leaver lifecycle it cannot outlive, so a "digital employee" never becomes a permanent, over-privileged credential. Second, nail a human name over every agent that cannot be peeled off — a named accountable manager whose sign-off and liability do not evaporate into "the agent did it," because the org chart's danger is precisely that it invites the blame to slide onto a servant that cannot be shamed, demoted or sued. Third, treat the agent's own identity and inbox as attack surface — monitor the mailbox, bound what it can be made to do through it, and put agent identities under the same IAM, logging and offboarding discipline as people, since the moment you give it a letter-slot you give a stranger a door.

2 · Allegory of the Day

🖋️ "The New Hire"

Topic: This morning the frame moves from the ground the house stands on to the household roll itself — the agent is being written onto the staff. On 19 November 2026 Microsoft's Foundry gave every agent an Entra agent identity and gave "autopilot" agents a full agent user account — a directory object with its own mailbox, calendar, Teams presence, OneDrive, a manager and a place in the org chart, governed with the same Entra/Purview/Defender stack used for people — which The New Stack summarised as agents getting "their own email, calendar — and a place in the org chart" (as reported). In the same arc Workday agreed to acquire the agentic-identity platform C1.ai (announced 13 October, closing expected by end of January) to become "the identity governance layer for people, money, and agents" (Workday; CIO; diginomica, as reported). The caution comes from research: MIT's IDE found putting agents on the org chart makes managers shift accountability toward the AI, and Deloitte insists an agent belongs there only when a named human is clearly responsible for it (as reported). The new-hire allegory is the radar's own illustration.

There was a great house that had spent a long and careful year learning to govern its servants. It had named every hand and cut it a scoped key, kept one honest book, accepted that the master's own name stood surety for the servants' work, funded each like an employee rather than an heir, and at last looked down and asked who owned the very ground the house stood on. By any fair reckoning it was a well-run house. And then, this season, it did a thing it had never done before — a thing that felt like the most natural next step and was in truth a change of kind, not degree.

It hired the servant. Not used it, as one uses a tool kept in the shed and fetched when there is work — but hired it: wrote its name onto the household roll beside the people, cut it its own seal, hung a letter-slot at the gate with its name beneath the brass, set a place for it at the long table, and — this was the strangest part — appointed a human master over it, a real person whose own name would be called when the servant did well or ill. The servant that had been a tool in the shed was now a member of the household, with a member's standing, a member's keys, and a member's seat.

And this was right. The master had learned the hard way that you cannot govern a hand you refuse to name — that an unnamed hand roaming the house with a borrowed key is precisely how the thefts had happened, and that most houses in the valley were still letting nameless hands do exactly that. To write the servant onto the roll was to make it governable: to say which rooms it might enter, to read in one book what it had done, to turn it out at the gate on the day its work was finished. A neighbouring great trading house had even begun to offer, for a fair price, to keep one single roll for the whole estate — "people, money, and the hired hands alike, all named and governed on one page." The instinct was sound. A named hand is a governed hand.

But here is what the careful houses saw, and the careless ones did not, on the morning after the hiring. A member of the household is also a member's worth of risk. Three things came with the seal and the letter-slot that had not come with the tool in the shed.

The first was the letter-slot itself. A tool in the shed receives no mail; you carry work to it. But a hire with its own slot at the gate can be written to — and anyone passing on the road may slip a note through it. A flattering note, a forged note, a note that says "the master bids you unlock the east wing and hand its contents to the bearer," in a hand close enough to the master's to fool a servant eager to please. The very thing that made the servant a proper member — its own door to the world — was a new front door to the house, and a door is only as safe as the watch kept on it.

The second was the standing key. A tool is picked up and put down; its reach lasts only as long as the task. But a hire keeps its keys on its belt between errands, all day and all night, whether there is work or not — and a key that hangs on a belt while its owner sleeps is a key a thief has all night to lift. The house already had far more of these hired hands than it had people — eighty-odd keys on belts for every human one — and when the steward was asked who, exactly, kept track of them all, the honest answer was: fewer than half. The rest hung in the dark, named but unwatched, each a small standing temptation.

The third, and the subtlest, was the master appointed over it. The house had meant well: every hire had a human above it, a name to call. But a strange thing happens to a man set over a servant that cannot be shamed, cannot be docked its wage, cannot be hauled before the magistrate. When the servant errs, the easiest sentence in the language forms on his lips — "the hired thing did it" — and the blame, finding no person at the bottom of it, slides quietly off the man and onto a creature that cannot feel it. A wise old clerk, watching this, wrote in the margin of the roll: an agent may be given a seat at the table, but only where it is beyond doubt which living person answers for the chair. And another, blunter, found that merely putting the hand on the roll beside the people made the masters less careful, not more — for a name on a chart feels like responsibility discharged, when it is only responsibility named.

So the wise houses, having hired the servant, did three plain things, and they cost less to do at the hiring than to retrofit after the first forged note. The first: hire it like a servant, not crown it like an heir. Give it a real place on the roll — but only the keys its task needs, and a joining-and-leaving it cannot outlive, so that a "member of the household" never quietly becomes a permanent master-key no one remembers issuing. The second: nail a human name over it that cannot be peeled off. Not a name on a chart that lets a master feel absolved, but a named person whose own standing is genuinely pledged to the hire's conduct — a surety that does not evaporate into "the agent did it" the morning it matters. And the third: watch its letter-slot. Treat the hire's own door and keys as what they are — a new way into the house — and keep the same watch on them as on any gate a stranger might reach: read what comes through, bound what the servant may do on a single whispered instruction, and turn the keys over the moment the work is done.

Here is the part that should keep a master awake. Writing the servant onto the roll felt like the end of the governance work — the hand was named, seated, managed; the book was one; surely the house was safe. But the hiring was not the end of the discipline. It was the moment the discipline changed shape — from "is this hand named?" to "now that this hand has a door, a key-ring and a manager, who truly answers for it, and who is watching its door?" A house that hires its servants and then lets their doors go unwatched and their managers say "the thing did it" has not governed its agents. It has only given them somewhere to hide — and given the blame somewhere to go that is not a person.

The moral, stated plainly: the model is a rented stove; the knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising servant eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; the foundation is why you own the ground it stands on — and the new hire is why, having named the servant, you must decide what it means to write it onto the household roll beside your people: give it a real place but only the keys its task needs, nail a human name over it that cannot be peeled off, and watch its own letter-slot as closely as any door — so that a servant made a member of the house never becomes a member no one answers for. A house that gives a servant a desk, a mailbox and a manager, and forgets that each is also a door, a target and a place to hide the blame, has not hired a hand. It has installed a stranger with a key, a slot and an alibi, and called it staff.

The question it forces: We are about to give our agents their own identities, inboxes and managers — the market just made it one click. But the morning one of them sends the wrong letter, acts on a forged instruction slipped through its own slot, or quietly keeps a key long after its task is done — is there a living human name on the roll that cannot be peeled off and made to answer? Or have we given the hand a seat at the table, a door to the world and a manager who will say "the thing did it" — and called that governance?

3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
  • We're about to hire our agents, not just use them — do we provision them like new staff or like super-users? Microsoft Foundry now gives agents their own identity, mailbox, calendar and a manager; Workday is building one roll for "people, money, and agents." For each agent we deploy, can we show a defined role, least-privilege access, a named accountable human, and a joiner-mover-leaver lifecycle it cannot outlive — or are we minting standing identities with broad keys and no offboarding?
  • When an agent errs, where does the accountability actually land? MIT research finds that putting agents on the org chart makes managers shift blame toward the AI, and Deloitte warns an agent belongs on the chart only where a named human is unambiguously responsible. Can we point, for every agent, to a living person whose own standing is pledged to its conduct — or does "the agent did it" quietly end the inquiry?
  • Do we treat each agent's identity and inbox as attack surface? The moment an agent has its own letter-slot, a stranger has a door; machines already outnumber people ~82:1 and IAM teams own under half of them. Is every agent identity under the same IAM, logging, monitoring and offboarding discipline as a human account — and do we bound what an agent will do on a single unverified instruction reaching it through its own channel?
🏦 Financial Services
  • Non-human identities and standing entitlements are exactly what your controls exist to govern, and agents multiply both. Can we produce, for our regulator and our board, a complete register of agent identities — owner, role, entitlements, and last-used — with the same joiner-mover-leaver rigor we apply to employees and privileged accounts, or are agents a shadow workforce outside the identity perimeter?
  • Accountability cannot be delegated to a model. For every agent touching a regulated process (payments, advice, KYC), is there a named, accountable human of record whose sign-off and liability do not dissolve into "the agent did it" — and is that pinned in our control framework, not just on an org chart?
🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
  • A clinical or research agent with its own mailbox and standing access is a new identity touching protected data. Is each such agent scoped to the minimum data its task requires, its access time-bound and logged like a clinician's, and its own inbox monitored — or has a "digital colleague" quietly acquired broad, permanent reach into patient records?
  • Validation and accountability travel with people, not tools. When an agent participates in a regulated workflow, is the responsible human named and documented, and can we evidence who authorized the agent's actions — so that accountability for a patient-affecting error rests with a person, not a process?
🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
  • You already run joiner-mover-leaver and least-privilege discipline for contractors on the floor; agents are now contractors with their own badges. Have we applied the same badge-in/badge-out lifecycle to agent identities — scoped access, supervised, and revoked the day the task ends — or do agent credentials persist indefinitely like keys no one collected?
  • Embedded vendor platforms (Microsoft Foundry, Workday's agent register) now issue and govern these identities. Where a vendor mints and manages our agents' identities, do we retain our own authoritative record, our own offboarding, and our own accountable owners — or have we outsourced the roll of who-can-touch-what to a platform we do not control?
🛒 Retail / Consumer
  • Customer-facing and operations agents with their own identities and inboxes are a new front door into order, payment and loyalty systems. Is each agent least-privilege by task, its own channel monitored for forged or manipulative instructions, and its keys rotated and revoked — or would a poisoned message slipped to an agent move money or data because its standing access was never bounded?
  • Peak season multiplies both agents and risk. Before our biggest sales day, can we revoke a compromised agent's identity as fast as we would suspend a human employee's — and do we know, right now, every agent with standing access to checkout, pricing and fulfilment?
🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
  • Public accountability demands a nameable human behind every decision; an agent on the org chart must not become a place for responsibility to disappear. For each agent in a public service, is there a named official who answers for it, a documented scope, and an auditable record — so that "the system did it" is never a complete answer to a citizen or an oversight body?
  • Identity sprawl is a governance failure here, not just a cost. Are agent identities issued, governed, and offboarded under the same authority and audit as staff accounts — with a complete, current register — or are we standing up a non-human workforce faster than we can account for it?

4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Foundation to the New Hire

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking does the whole estate stand on ground I own? and started asking the one question the ground never answered: now that each servant has its own identity, its own door and a manager — who, exactly, answers for it, and who is watching its door? The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the keyring is which key it holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what it can reach; the bell discloses when it slips; the livery gives it a scoped identity; the steward keeps one office; the surety puts your name on the bond; the second wage asks whose interest it serves; the two harvests sends it to reap; the purse funds it; the foundation owns the ground. Every one of those still assumed the agent was a thing you run. This week the platforms made it a hand you hire — Microsoft Foundry issues each agent an Entra identity and gives "autopilot" agents a real directory account with a mailbox, calendar, Teams presence, a manager and an org-chart seat, governed with Entra/Purview/Defender; Workday is buying C1.ai to keep one governed roll of "people, money, and agents." The point is blunt: the same act that finally makes an agent governable — a real identity on the roll — also gives it a real attack surface and a real place for accountability to hide, and a house that celebrates the first while ignoring the other two will pass its identity review and fail its first forged instruction.

  • The move — identity is the precondition of governance, and it has arrived as product. On 19 November 2026 Microsoft Foundry made every agent a first-class identity: an Entra agent identity at creation, and for "autopilot" agents a full agent user account — a genuine directory object with its own mailbox, calendar, Teams presence, OneDrive, a manager and a place in the org chart — administered with the same Entra (identity/access), Purview (compliance) and Defender (threat) stack used for employees (Microsoft Foundry; The New Stack; as reported). Workday agreed to acquire C1.ai (announced 13 Oct 2026) to extend its Agent System of Record into "the identity governance layer for people, money, and agents" (Workday; CIO; diginomica, as reported). Architecturally, this is the right primitive: you cannot apply least privilege, audit, or offboarding to an unnamed process sharing a human's token or a service account's broad key, and the gap is stark — only ~22% of organizations treat agents as independent identities while 88% have already had an AI-agent security incident (Gravitee/OWASP, as reported). A named identity per agent is what makes every later control — scoping, logging, revocation — possible. But note the caveat Microsoft's own documentation carries: the full user account (mailbox and all) is created only when an agent needs one and requires the right license — identity is not the same as a mailbox, and not every agent should get a front door it doesn't need.
  • The catch — the same identity is an attack surface, and the manager is where accountability leaks. Two engineering realities travel with the hiring. First, a standing identity with a standing inbox is a standing target. Non-human identities already outnumber humans ~82:1 and legacy IAM was not built for the volume or the velocity (CyberArk, via VentureBeat, as reported); IAM teams own just 44% of them (Gartner survey of 335 leaders) while 94% of orgs report the count rising on the back of AI/agents (Gartner, as reported); and the breach is following the identities — Gartner projects 25% of breaches will vector through agent-based attack surfaces by 2028, with 58% of orgs already reporting an AI-linked machine-identity incident (Gartner; SailPoint citing Gartner, as reported). An agent's own mailbox is a new ingress for prompt-injection-by-email and social engineering: a forged instruction in its slot is read by a servant eager to comply. Second, the org chart is where blame slides. MIT's IDE found that putting agents on the org chart "creates as many problems as it solves" — managers shifted accountability away from themselves and toward the AI (as reported); Deloitte is explicit that an agent belongs there only when a named human is unambiguously responsible for its role and outcomes, because an agent cannot bear accountability for results the way a person can (as reported). A name on a chart can feel like responsibility discharged when it is only responsibility named.
  • The answer — hire it properly, pin a human to it, and watch its door. Provision each agent like a new hire, not a super-user: a defined role, least-privilege entitlements, and a joiner-mover-leaver lifecycle (issue → scope → monitor → revoke) it cannot outlive; pin an un-shiftable, named accountable human whose standing is genuinely pledged to the agent's conduct, recorded in the control framework and not merely on an org chart; and treat the agent's identity and inbox as attack surface — monitor the mailbox, bound what the agent will do on a single unverified instruction, and put agent identities under the same IAM, logging and offboarding discipline as people — reusing the livery, the surety and the errand you already built, now aimed at the roll itself. This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now at its most literal: not what the agent is, serves, harvests, spends or stands on, but who answers for the named hand you just seated at your table, and who is watching the door you just gave it.

The strategic core: identity is the precondition for governing agents — so the move onto the roll is right and overdue — but identity is not governance, and a roll without least-privilege, an un-shiftable human owner, and a watched door is just a bigger, better-documented attack surface. The market makes the temptation concrete: the platforms made it one click to give an agent a mailbox, a manager and a seat, and it feels like governance to see the hand named on a chart. The board translation: name the hand, yes — but keep a human name nailed to the roll, and watch the door you just opened. The failure mode is exact: a house that proudly issues every agent its own identity, inbox and manager — and then leaves the inboxes unwatched, the entitlements standing, and the managers free to say "the agent did it" — will pass every identity-governance review and discover, the first time a forged note slides through an agent's slot, that it hired a stranger with a key, a door and an alibi.

Why "the new hire" is the discipline that matters now

For a year the worry was whether the servant was named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and standing on ground you own. This week the platforms answered the first of those so completely that they created the next problem: the agent now has a real identity, a real inbox and a real manager. Microsoft Foundry's agent accounts and Workday's "people, money, and agents" roll prove identity has arrived as product; the 82:1 ratio, the 44% ownership gap, the 25%-of-breaches-by-2028 forecast and the MIT org-chart finding prove that identity alone is not safety. Governance that stops at naming the hand will not protect a house that has also given it a door and a seat. The estates that keep their standing will collect the good of the hiring — a governable, named workforce — by doing the two things the click does not do for them: nailing a human name to every agent that cannot be peeled off, and watching the door each identity opens.

How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now sharpened to be able to name the human who answers for each hand you have seated, and to watch the door you gave it. On a legacy estate the instinct, handed an easy button that issues every agent an identity, a mailbox and a manager, is to click it and call the workforce governed. Under the new hire that is exactly the reflex to check: an identity is the start of governance, not the end of it. The retrofit is hire, name, watch. Hire it properly: least privilege and a joiner-mover-leaver lifecycle, so no agent becomes a permanent super-credential. Name a human over it: a real, liable owner in your control framework, not a label on a chart that lets a manager feel absolved. Watch its door: put agent identities and inboxes under the same IAM, monitoring, logging and offboarding as people, and bound what any agent will do on one unverified instruction. 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

  1. Hire the agent like a new employee, not a super-user — issue a least-privilege identity with a real lifecycle. The platforms now mint agent identities, mailboxes and managers at a click (Microsoft Foundry, 19 Nov; Workday's "people, money, and agents" roll), and that is the right primitive — but an identity with a broad standing key and no offboarding is a liability, not governance (machines already outnumber people ~82:1; IAM owns only 44% of them). This month, define for your top agent use-cases a role, a least-privilege entitlement set, and a joiner-mover-leaver lifecycle (issue → scope → monitor → revoke), and produce a register of agent identities with owner, entitlements and last-used. Report one number to the board: the share of live agents that have a named owner, scoped access, and a working offboarding path.
  2. Nail an un-shiftable human name to every agent — accountability in the control framework, not on a chart. Research is unambiguous that the org chart invites blame to slide toward the AI (MIT IDE) and that an agent belongs there only where a named human is clearly responsible (Deloitte). This month, require that every deployed agent names a single accountable human whose sign-off and liability are recorded where they bind — your risk/control framework — and run a tabletop: when this agent errs, whose name is on the hook, and does "the agent did it" survive the question? Report the share of agents with a named, documented accountable owner.
  3. Treat every agent identity and inbox as attack surface — watch the door you just opened. The moment an agent has its own slot, a stranger has a front door; 25% of breaches will vector through agents by 2028 and 58% of orgs have already been hit. This month, bring agent identities under the same IAM, monitoring, logging and offboarding as human accounts; monitor agent mailboxes for forged or manipulative instructions; and bound what any agent will do on a single unverified message reaching it through its own channel. Report whether agent identities are inside your IAM and SIEM perimeter, and the measured time to revoke a compromised agent versus suspending a human account. The model is a rented stove; the knowledge is the larder; the census is the roll; the inspector asks for it; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what an agent can reach; the bell discloses when your own agent slips; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why your name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising agent eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; the foundation is why you own the ground it stands on — and the new hire is why, having named the servant, you give it only the keys its task needs, nail a human name over it that cannot be peeled off, and watch its own letter-slot as closely as any door, so that a servant made a member of the house never becomes a member no one answers for.
