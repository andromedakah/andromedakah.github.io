The New Hire

Monday, 24 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

This week the agent stopped being a tool you use and became a hand you hire — its own identity, mailbox, calendar, a manager and a seat on the org chart (Microsoft Foundry, 19 Nov). A real identity is what makes it governable; it also gives it a door a stranger can write to, a standing key, and a manager who can say "the agent did it." Hire it least-privilege, nail an un-shiftable human name to it, and watch its door.

1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from governing the servant to the household roll itself — the agent is being hired, not just used. A real identity is the precondition of governance; it is also a new attack surface and a new place for accountability to hide.

1. The move — the agent gets a desk, a mailbox, and a manager. On 19 November 2026 Microsoft Foundry gave every agent an Entra identity, and "autopilot" agents a full agent user account — its own mailbox, calendar, Teams, OneDrive, a manager and a place in the org chart — governed like staff (Microsoft; The New Stack). Workday is buying C1.ai (announced 13 Oct) to be "the identity governance layer for people, money, and agents" (Workday; CIO, as reported). You cannot govern an unnamed hand — only ~22% treat agents as identities while 88% already had an incident (Gravitee/OWASP).

2. The catch — membership is attack surface, and the manager is where blame hides. Machine identities outnumber humans ~82:1 (CyberArk); IAM owns only 44% of them and 94% report the count rising on AI (Gartner); 25% of breaches will vector through agents by 2028 and 58% are already hit (Gartner; SailPoint). And MIT's IDE finds that putting agents on the org chart makes managers shift accountability toward the AI; Deloitte: an agent belongs there only where a named human is clearly responsible (as reported).

3. The answer — hire it properly, name a human over it, watch its door. Provision each agent least-privilege with a joiner-mover-leaver lifecycle; pin an un-shiftable accountable human to it; and monitor its identity and inbox like any other door a stranger might slip a poisoned note through.

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, purse and foundation each assumed the agent was a thing you use. This week it became a hand you hire — its own identity, inbox, a seat and a manager. That is the right move; you cannot govern an unnamed hand. But a hire on the roll brings a hire's exposures: a standing identity is a standing target, its own inbox is a new front door, and its manager is where "the agent did it" is tempted to end the inquiry. Give the servant a desk if you must — but never a desk no one answers for.

2 · Allegory — The New Hire

A great house had spent a careful year learning to govern its servants — named, booked, bonded, funded, and at last asking who owned the ground. And then it did a thing it had never done: it hired the servant. Not used it, as one uses a tool in the shed — but hired it: wrote its name on the household roll beside the people, cut it its own seal, hung a letter-slot at the gate, set a place at the table, and appointed a human master over it. The tool in the shed was now a member of the household.

And this was right — you cannot govern a hand you refuse to name. But a member of the household is also a member's worth of risk. Three things came with the seal. The letter-slot: a hire can be written to, and a forged note slipped through it — "the master bids you unlock the east wing" — is read by a servant eager to please. The standing key: it hangs on the belt all night, and the house had eighty-odd such keys for every human one, with fewer than half tracked. And the master over it: set above a servant that cannot be shamed or sued, the easiest sentence forms on his lips — "the hired thing did it" — and the blame slides off the man onto a creature that cannot feel it.

So the wise houses did three things: hire it like a servant, not crown it like an heir (a real place, but only the keys its task needs, and a joining-and-leaving it cannot outlive); nail a human name over it that cannot be peeled off (a named person whose standing is pledged to its conduct, not a label that lets a master feel absolved); and watch its letter-slot (treat its door and keys as a new way into the house). For the hiring was not the end of the discipline — it was the moment the discipline changed shape, from "is this hand named?" to "who answers for it, and who is watching its door?"

The question it forces: We are about to give our agents their own identities, inboxes and managers — the market made it one click. But the morning one sends the wrong letter, acts on a forged instruction, or keeps a key long after its task — is there a living human name on the roll that cannot be peeled off and made to answer? Or have we given the hand a seat, a door and a manager who will say "the thing did it" — and called that governance?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the New Hire

The same act that finally makes an agent governable — a real identity on the roll — also gives it a real attack surface and a real place for accountability to hide. The move: identity is the precondition of governance, and it arrived as product (Microsoft Foundry's Entra identities + agent user accounts; Workday's "people, money, and agents" roll) — you cannot scope, log or offboard an unnamed process sharing a human's token. The catch: a standing identity with a standing inbox is a standing target (machines outnumber humans ~82:1; IAM owns 44%; 25% of breaches via agents by 2028), and the org chart is where managers shift blame toward the AI (MIT IDE). The answer: provision each agent like a new hire (least privilege + joiner-mover-leaver), pin an un-shiftable accountable human in your control framework, and treat the agent's identity and inbox as attack surface.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Hire the agent like a new employee, not a super-user. The platforms mint agent identities, mailboxes and managers at a click — but an identity with a broad standing key and no offboarding is a liability. Define for your top agent use-cases a role, least-privilege entitlements, and a joiner-mover-leaver lifecycle (issue → scope → monitor → revoke), and produce a register of agent identities with owner, entitlements and last-used. Report the share of live agents with a named owner, scoped access, and a working offboarding path.

2. Nail an un-shiftable human name to every agent. The org chart invites blame to slide toward the AI (MIT IDE); an agent belongs there only where a named human is responsible (Deloitte). Require that every deployed agent names a single accountable human whose sign-off and liability are recorded in your risk/control framework, and run a tabletop: when this agent errs, whose name is on the hook? Report the share of agents with a named, documented owner.

3. Treat every agent identity and inbox as attack surface. 25% of breaches will vector through agents by 2028 and 58% are already hit. Bring agent identities under the same IAM, monitoring, logging and offboarding as human accounts; monitor agent mailboxes for forged instructions; and bound what any agent does on a single unverified message. Report whether agent identities are inside your IAM and SIEM perimeter, and the time to revoke a compromised agent versus suspending a human account.
