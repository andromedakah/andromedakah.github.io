### The toll — the cheapest tier is paid for in your data (why it is a board problem)

- CryptoBriefing, "Meta offers discounted access to Muse Spark 1.3 model for data sharing" — https://cryptobriefing.com/meta-muse-spark-discounted-data-sharing/ (via search summary)
- Codersera, "Muse Spark 1.3: Complete Guide 2026 (Pricing, Specs, and the Contributor Tier)" — https://codersera.com/blog/muse-spark-1-3-complete-guide-2026/ (via search summary)
- Coursiv, "Meta Muse Spark 1.3: Benchmarks, Pricing, and the Price of Cheap Tokens" — https://coursiv.io/blog/muse-spark-1-3 (via search summary)
- New Pelican, "The Muse Spark 1.2 Contributor Tier: What You're Actually Paying With" (framing carried forward to 1.3) — https://www.newpelican.com/articles/the-muse-spark-1-2-contributor-tier-what-youre-actually-paying-with/ (via search summary)
- Fello AI, "Muse Spark 1.3: Benchmarks, Pricing and the Catch" — https://felloai.com/muse-spark-1-3/ (via search summary)

Claims sourced here (as reported): Meta's Muse Spark 1.3 ships two pricing tiers — a Standard tier at $1.25 per million input tokens and $4.25 per million output tokens, with no training on your data per Meta; and a Contributor tier at $0.10 / $0.20 per million tokens (up to roughly 21× cheaper), whose discount is granted "in exchange for permission to use your prompts and completions to train future Meta models." Meta's terms instruct Contributor-tier users not to submit sensitive, confidential or personal information. Critically, Contributor mode is not a signed agreement or an account-level setting — it is a different model id, so any engineer who changes one string in a config file moves that workload onto the training tier, with no procurement step and no audit trail until someone reads the config. Open questions the terms do not resolve include retention of Contributor prompts, human review, revocability of training rights after a request is sent, and whether tool-call arguments, tool results and file attachments count as "prompts" for training. Carried here as the "miller's toll" (the multure): when the model rents for almost nothing, the cheapest tier is paid for in your own data.

### The default — training-on-your-data is spreading by default (the pattern)

- HelpDocs, "You Already Paid the Subscription. Now They Want Your Data for AI Too" — https://blog.helpdocs.io/ai-data-mining-default-on/ (via search summary)

- Airia, "Shadow AI Statistics: Key Data Points Every CISO Needs in 2026" — https://airia.com/blog/shadow-ai-statistics-key-data-points-every-ciso-needs-in-2026/ (via search summary)

Claims sourced here (context, as reported): starting 17 August 2026, Atlassian began using Confluence and Jira content by default to train its Rovo AI, affecting roughly 300,000 organizations, with no content opt-out for Free and Standard plans and a genuine opt-out reserved for Enterprise and Premium customers; consumer free tiers (and, by default, some paid consumer tiers) of major assistants train on user content. Per Airia's 2026 CISO data, roughly 43% of organizations report they cannot audit the AI tools in use, and only about 24% have implemented AI-specific data-loss-prevention controls. Carried here as the "invisible toll spreading by default": the tooling to route your grain into someone's larder is everywhere and mostly on-by-default; the control to stop it is rare.

- McKinsey & Company, "The State of AI: Global Survey 2026" — https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai (primary; egress-blocked; via prior-day coverage)

Claims sourced here (context, as reported): McKinsey's State of AI 2026 reports that only ~6% of firms are "AI high performers" (attributing ≥5% of EBIT to AI with significant impact), unchanged from a year earlier, and only ~37% attribute any EBIT impact at all ("about the same" as 2025), with high performers nearly three times as likely to fundamentally redesign their workflows end to end.
