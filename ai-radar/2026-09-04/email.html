The Miller's Toll

Friday, 4 September 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

Yesterday the board asked whether the wheel grinds any flour. This morning the river floods: three frontier-class models shipped in ~48 hours (Claude Fable 5.1, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Muse Spark 1.3) and token prices fell ~60% in one window (Tech Startups, 3 Sep). The rented stove is nearly free — but the cheapest tier (Muse Spark Contributor, $0.10/$0.20, ~21× cheaper) is paid for by training on your data, and the switch is a model id, not a contract. The cheapest stove keeps a taste of everything you cook.

1 · Executive Summary

For a week this radar built, counted, inspected, secured and questioned the value of the agentic workforce. This morning the model layer itself moved, loudly: capability is commoditizing in real time, and the cheapest capability is now sold for data instead of dollars.

1. The wheel is nearly free. Three frontier-class models shipped in ~48h — Claude Fable 5.1 (26 Aug; Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index 66, current top), Gemini 3.8 Flash (1 Sep; $0.75/$3.75 per M), Muse Spark 1.3 (2 Sep; Index 61–62) — and the trade press reported token prices down ~60% in one window (Tech Startups, 3 Sep), with GPT-4-class quality now under $0.50/M vs ~$30 three years ago. Renting the model is no longer the moat or the cost center (as reported).

2. The cheapest grinding takes a toll in grain, not coin. Meta's Muse Spark 1.3 lists a Standard tier ($1.25/$4.25, no training on your data) and a Contributor tier ($0.10/$0.20 — up to ~21× cheaper) whose discount is paid by permission to train on your prompts and completions; Meta's terms say do not submit sensitive data. The near-free tier is paid for with your proprietary data — the one asset this radar keeps calling your moat (as reported).

3. The toll is invisible — a model id, not a contract. Contributor is not a signed agreement or an account setting; one changed config string moves a workload onto the training tier, with no procurement gate and no audit trail until someone greps the config. The same default is spreading — Atlassian began training Rovo on Confluence/Jira content by default from 17 Aug (~300k orgs) (as reported).

Bottom line: the model is collapsing toward free — but "free" is a pricing tier, not a fact. The cheapest tokens are sold for training rights on your own prompts, and the switch is a config string. The old mill never charged coin; it took the multure — a handful of every sack — and the cheapest miller kept the biggest toll, learned every recipe, and sold your own bread back to you. Price your model tiers in data as well as dollars, make the training-tier model id a governed procurement decision, and keep your proprietary grain out of the cheap mill.

2 · Allegory — The Miller's Toll

In the old country a miller did not ask for coin. He asked for the multure — the toll — a fixed portion scooped from every sack of grain that passed under his stone. Then a season came when new mills sprang up all along the river in the space of a few days, each grinding faster and finer, until grinding that had once been dear was almost free. And into that crowd stepped a miller who offered to grind for almost nothing — a tenth of what the others charged. The careful few asked: if he charges almost no coin, what is he living on? He was living on the toll; he had simply moved it out of sight. He took barely a coin, but kept a generous handful of every sack, and studied it — learning whose grain made the finest bread, which recipes sold — and by harvest he was baking, from the accumulated scoops of a whole valley, the exact loaves his own customers had brought him to grind, and selling them back at his own gate.

The dear miller up the road, who still charged honest coin and kept not a grain that was not his, understood what his neighbors did not: the cheapest mill was not cheap. It had only changed what it charged — grain instead of coin — and grain is dearer than coin, because coin buys one loaf and grain buys every loaf you will ever bake. And the toll was invisible: no contract, no ledger, only which gate your cart rolled through — so a single hired hand, told merely to "use the cheap mill, it's faster," could route a season's harvest into the tolling gate without anyone deciding to. The households that kept their footing wrote down which grain could go to the cheap gate and which could not — common wheat gladly, seed stock never — pricing their grain in two currencies at once.

The question it forces: the model just got cheaper than ever — three labs shipped in a week, token prices fell by more than half. So the question is not "are we getting a good price," it is "what are we paying with?" Do we know which of our AI workloads run on a data-tolling tier? Can any engineer flip a production workload onto the cheap mill with one config string? And have we written down which of our data is common wheat we'll trade for cheap tokens, and which is seed stock that must never pass through a mill that keeps a handful?

3 · Technical — Pricing the Model in Two Currencies

Capability is commoditizing in real time: three frontier-class releases in ~48h — Claude Fable 5.1 (26 Aug; Index 66, top), Gemini 3.8 Flash (1 Sep; $0.75/$3.75), Muse Spark 1.3 (2 Sep; Index 62 "max"/61 "xhigh"; 75.4 DeepSWE v1.1, 88.8 Terminal-Bench 2.1) — with token prices ~-60% in one window (Tech Startups, 3 Sep) and GPT-4-class quality now <$0.50/M vs ~$30 three years ago. The model is a near-commodity, near-perfectly substitutable; the scarce asset is on your side of the API — your data. The cheapest tier is a data toll, and the toll is a model id: Muse Spark 1.3 Standard $1.25/$4.25 (no training) vs Contributor $0.10/$0.20 (~21× cheaper, trains on your prompts and completions; "do not submit sensitive data"). Contributor is not a signed agreement or an account setting — it is a different model id, so one config string moves a workload onto the training tier with no procurement gate and no audit trail. This is the default, not the exception: Atlassian trains Rovo on Confluence/Jira by default from 17 Aug (~300k orgs); ~43% of orgs cannot audit their AI tools and ~24% have AI-specific DLP (as reported).

On legacy: the temptation is to chase the ~21× discount everywhere, because the dollar saving is loud and the data cost is silent. Inventory: flag every model id that trains on your data. Classify: common wheat (tradeable) vs seed stock (never tolled). Gate: enforce at the AI gateway which data classes reach which model ids. Govern: make adopting a data-tolling model id a procurement-and-approval decision, not a config change. The census is the roll, the inspector asks you to produce it, the two mills are whether it grinds flour — and the miller's toll is what the cheapest grinding charges in your own grain.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Inventory every model id and flag the ones that train on your data. The dollar price of a token is collapsing, but the cheapest tiers charge a second price your cost dashboard cannot see. Enumerate every model and tier in production and mark which train on your prompts and completions — Contributor-style tiers, default-on free plans, train-by-default enterprise features. Report the share of production AI workloads whose data-training terms have been reviewed and named.

2. Classify your data as common wheat or seed stock — and enforce the split at the gateway. The near-free tier is a data-acquisition instrument; the defense is deciding, in advance, which grain you trade for cheap tokens and which you never will. Enforce at the AI gateway which classes may reach which model ids, so the split is a control, not a developer's good intention. Report the share of seed-stock flows hard-blocked from data-tolling model ids.

3. Make adopting a data-tolling model id a procurement decision, not a config change. Today the switch to the cheap mill is one string an engineer can change unnoticed; that is the governance failure, not the price. Require that turning on any train-on-your-data tier is a named-owner procurement-and-approval step, and capture the ~21× discount deliberately on the common-wheat workloads where it is safe. Drive the count of unapproved data-tolling model ids to zero.
