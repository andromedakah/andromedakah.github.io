1 · Executive Summary 90-second read

For a week this radar assembled the agentic workforce, counted it, faced the inspector, priced the break-in, and asked whether any of the motion grinds flour. This morning the model layer itself moved, loudly: capability is commoditizing in real time, and the cheapest capability is now sold for data instead of dollars. Three frontier-class labs shipped inside ~48 hours and token prices fell off a cliff — but the discount tier that everyone will be tempted to flip on is the one that turns your proprietary prompts into someone else's training set.

The number that frames it — the wheel is nearly free now. On 2–3 September the trade press reported AI token prices collapsing ~60% in a single window (Tech Startups, 3 Sep), extending a multi-year slide that has taken GPT-4-class quality from ~$30 to under $0.50 per million tokens and Google's Flash line down ~99.7% in three years (as reported). Three frontier-class releases landed almost on top of each other — Claude Fable 5.1 (26 Aug; Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index 66, the current top), Gemini 3.8 Flash (1 Sep; $0.75/$3.75 per million), and Muse Spark 1.3 (2 Sep; Index 61–62, behind only Fable 5.1 and Claude Opus 5) (as reported). The rented stove is now a near-commodity. Renting it is no longer where the money — or the risk — lives.

The catch that makes it a board issue — the cheapest tier is paid for in your data. Meta's Muse Spark 1.3 lists a Standard tier at $1.25/$4.25 per million tokens (no training on your data) and a Contributor tier at $0.10/$0.20 — up to ~21× cheaper — whose discount is granted in exchange for permission to train on your prompts and completions (as reported). Meta's own terms tell users not to submit sensitive, confidential or personal information to the discounted tier. And the governance failure is structural: Contributor is not a signed agreement or an account-level control; it is a different model id, so any engineer who changes one config string moves that workload onto the training tier — no procurement, no approval gate, invisible until an audit greps the config (Atlassian defaulted ~300k orgs into training Rovo on 17 Aug) (as reported).

2 · Allegory of the Day

🏛️ "The Miller's Toll"

Topic: This week the model layer commoditized in public. Three frontier-class systems shipped within ~48 hours — Claude Fable 5.1 (26 Aug; Intelligence Index 66), Gemini 3.8 Flash (1 Sep; $0.75/$3.75 per million tokens) and Muse Spark 1.3 (2 Sep; Index 61–62) — and on 2–3 September the trade press reported AI token prices collapsing ~60% in a single window, extending a slide that has taken GPT-4-class quality from ~$30 to under $0.50 per million tokens. The board-level catch sits in Meta's pricing: Muse Spark 1.3 offers a Standard tier ($1.25/$4.25, no training on your data) and a Contributor tier ($0.10/$0.20, up to ~21× cheaper) whose discount is paid by granting Meta permission to train on your prompts and completions — and Contributor is not a contract but a different model id. The lesson: when the model rents for almost nothing, the cheapest tier is paid for in your own data — the mill's ancient "multure."

In the old country a miller did not ask for coin. He asked for the multure — the toll — a fixed portion scooped from every sack of grain that passed under his stone, and out of those scoops he made his living. So when a household carried its wheat to be ground, it paid not in money but in flour: a handful here, a measure there, taken from the very grain it brought. For most of a lifetime this seemed a fair and settled thing, because the toll was small and the grinding was necessary, and no one thought very hard about the slow river of everyone's grain running quietly into one man's storehouse.

Then a season came when new mills sprang up all along the river, one after another in the space of a few days, each grinding faster and finer than the last, until the grinding that had once been dear was suddenly almost free. And into that crowd stepped a miller who made an offer no one had heard before. Bring your grain to me, he said, and I will grind it for almost nothing — a tenth of what the others charge, less than the cost of the water. The households could not believe their luck, and the queues at his gate ran long. Only the careful few asked the question that mattered: if he charges almost no coin, what is he living on? And the answer was the oldest answer in milling. He was living on the toll. He had simply moved it out of sight. The cheap miller took barely a coin — but from every sack he kept a generous handful, and he did something with those handfuls that the old millers never had: he studied them. He learned which household grew which wheat, whose grain made the finest bread, which recipes sold at market and which did not — and by harvest he was baking, from the accumulated scoops of a whole valley's grain, the exact loaves his own customers had brought him to grind, and selling them back at his own gate.

The dear miller up the road, the one who still charged honest coin and kept not a single grain that was not his, watched the queues pass him by and said nothing, because he understood what his neighbors did not: the cheapest mill was not cheap. It had only changed what it charged. It took its price in grain instead of coin, and grain — your grain, your seed, your recipe — is dearer than coin, because coin buys one loaf and grain buys every loaf you will ever bake. The households that flocked to the cheap gate thought they were saving money. They were paying the most expensive toll in the valley, and they were paying it in the one thing they could never buy back.

And here was the cruelest part, the part that undid even careful households: the toll was invisible. There was no contract to sign at the cheap mill, no ledger, no moment where a steward looked up and said this sack will be tolled in grain. There was only which gate your cart happened to roll through — and the gates stood side by side, unmarked, so that a single hired hand, sent down with the morning's grain and told merely to use the cheap mill, it's faster, could route a season's harvest into the tolling gate without anyone in the household ever deciding to. By the time the master noticed, the grain was already ground, the handfuls already scooped, the recipe already learned. You cannot refuse a toll you never saw yourself agree to.

The households that kept their footing did the unglamorous thing. They did not swear off the cheap mill entirely — the grinding was genuinely good, and coin saved is coin saved. But they wrote down, plainly, which grain could go to the tolling gate and which could not. The common wheat, the grain whose recipe was no secret, the flour anyone in the valley could already make — that they sent to the cheap mill gladly and pocketed the savings. But the seed stock, the heirloom strain, the recipe that was the whole reason anyone bought their bread — that grain went only to the dear mill that took coin and kept nothing, and it went there by rule, not by the whim of whoever happened to be driving the cart. They priced their grain in two currencies at once — coin and toll — and they decided, deliberately and in advance, which sacks they were willing to pay for in each.

The moral, stated plainly: the model is a rented stove, and this week the rent fell to almost nothing; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the lingua franca is the tongue it speaks; the conductor keeps the ensemble in time; the ear hears whether it plays well; the repertoire is the music only your house can play; the census is the roll that counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the break-in is what the missing entries cost; the two mills are whether the motion grinds flour — and the miller's toll is what the cheapest grinding actually charges: not coin, but a handful of your grain, taken invisibly, learned, and sold back to you. When the wheel is nearly free, price it in data too, mark which gate each sack may pass through, and keep your seed stock out of the cheap mill — because free grinding is never free; you pay the toll in grain.

The question it forces: The model just got cheaper than it has ever been — three frontier labs shipped in a week and token prices fell by more than half. So the question is not "are we getting a good price," it is "what are we paying with?" Do we know which of our AI workloads run on a data-tolling tier — a Contributor model id, a default-on training setting, a free plan that trains on our content? Can any engineer flip a production workload onto the cheap mill with one config string, or is the tolling model id a governed procurement decision with a named owner? And have we actually written down which of our data is common wheat we will happily trade for cheap tokens, and which is the seed stock that must never pass through a mill that keeps a handful — or are we, like most of the valley, carting our whole harvest to the cheapest gate because the coin price looked too good to ask what the real toll was?

3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
  • What are we actually paying with? Token prices fell ~60% in a single window this week, and the cheapest tier on the market (Muse Spark 1.3 Contributor, $0.10/$0.20) is discounted up to ~21× in exchange for training on your prompts and completions. Do we know which of our AI workloads run on a data-tolling tier — and did anyone decide that on purpose, or did it arrive as a config default?
  • Can one config string move us onto the cheap mill? The tolling tier is a different model id, not a contract or an account setting — no procurement step, no audit trail until someone greps the config. Is our training-tier model id a governed procurement decision with a named owner, or can any engineer flip a production workload onto it unnoticed?
  • Which of our data is common wheat, and which is seed stock? Free and discounted tiers now train on your content by default across the stack (Atlassian Rovo defaulted ~300k orgs on 17 Aug). Have we written down, in advance, which data may pass through a data-tolling model and which must never — or do we decide it by whoever happens to be driving the cart?
🏦 Financial Services
  • Customer records, transaction data and proprietary models are regulated seed stock, not common wheat. Do we have a hard control that blocks data-tolling model ids (Contributor tiers, train-by-default free plans) from touching regulated data — enforced at the gateway, not asked of the developer?
  • The cheap-token temptation lands hardest on high-volume, low-margin automation. Where we chase the ~21× discount, can we prove the workload carries only non-sensitive grain — or are we tolling customer data to shave inference cost?
🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
  • PHI and proprietary research are the definition of grain you can never buy back. Does any pathway exist by which a clinician's or researcher's prompt could reach a train-on-your-data tier — and can we prove it is closed, given the switch is a model id an engineer controls?
  • The savings are real and the pressure to capture them is real. Have we designated which non-clinical, non-identifiable workflows may use the cheap mill, so the discount is captured deliberately rather than the sensitive data leaking into it by accident?
🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
  • Process data, yields and designs are the recipe competitors would pay for. Do our engineering and design tools default to a Standard (no-training) model id, or could a Contributor-tier config string route proprietary process data into a vendor's training set?
  • Commoditizing model prices are a genuine cost win at industrial token volumes. Have we split our workloads — common wheat to the cheap mill, seed stock to the dear one — so we bank the savings without tolling the designs?
🛒 Retail / Consumer
  • Customer behavior, pricing and assortment logic are exactly the "recipe" the cheap miller learns and resells. Are our customer-facing and merchandising agents pinned to no-training model ids, or are we trading tomorrow's competitive edge for today's cheaper tokens?
  • Consumer free tiers train by default too. Do we know which shadow, employee-adopted AI tools are grinding company data at a tolling gate for free — and can we even see them?
🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
  • Citizen data tolled into a vendor's training set is a headline, not a saving. Can we demonstrate, to an auditor, that no public-service workload runs on a train-on-your-data tier — by policy and by enforced control, not by developer good intent?
  • Procurement is the natural gate. Have we made the tolling model id a procurement-and-approval decision, so the cheapest tier cannot enter production as a quiet config change?

4 · Technical Deep-Dive — Pricing the Model in Two Currencies

Read the whole stack one more time, but from the seat of the person signing the model bill — and paying a second, invisible bill they never see. The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the lingua franca lets it speak; the conductor coordinates it; the ear hears its errors; the repertoire is the data it performs; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the break-in is what the missing entries cost; the two mills are whether the motion grinds flour. The miller's toll is what the model layer underneath all of it now charges — because this week the model rented for almost nothing, and the cheapest rent is paid not in dollars but in the right to train on your data. The engineering point is blunt: a model tier is now two prices at once — a dollar price per token and a data price in training rights — and the second price is set by a model id, invisible to every dashboard that only tracks the first.

  • Capability is commoditizing in real time. Three frontier-class releases in ~48h: Claude Fable 5.1 (26 Aug; Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index 66, current top); Gemini 3.8 Flash (1 Sep; $0.75/$3.75 per million tokens); Muse Spark 1.3 (2 Sep; Index 62 for the preview "max," 61 for the shipping "xhigh," behind only Fable 5.1 and Opus 5; 75.4 on DeepSWE v1.1, 88.8 on Terminal-Bench 2.1) — and token prices reported ~-60% in a single window (Tech Startups, 3 Sep), with GPT-4-class quality now <$0.50/M vs ~$30 three years ago and Google's Flash line down ~99.7% in three years (as reported). Read architecturally: the model is a near-commodity utility, near-perfectly substitutable across vendors, and the per-token dollar cost is trending toward the floor. If your AI strategy still treats the model as the scarce, expensive, differentiating asset, it is fighting the last war. The scarce asset is on your side of the API: your data.
  • The cheapest tier is a data toll, and the toll is a model id. Meta's Muse Spark 1.3: Standard $1.25/$4.25 per million tokens (no training on your data); Contributor $0.10/$0.20 (up to ~21× cheaper), discounted in exchange for permission to train on your prompts and completions — with Meta's terms instructing users not to submit sensitive, confidential or personal information to it (as reported). The design failure is that Contributor is not a signed agreement or an account-level setting — it is a different model id. Any engineer who changes one string in a config moves that workload onto the tier that feeds the vendor's training pipeline: no procurement step, no approval gate, nothing that surfaces in an audit until someone reads the config. Open questions the terms do not answer: retention of Contributor prompts, whether humans review them, whether training rights can be revoked after a request is sent, and whether tool-call arguments, tool results and file attachments count as "prompts" for training. If your cost dashboard tracks dollars per token and nothing else, you are instrumented for the cheap price and blind to the dear one.
  • This is the default, not the exception. The refrain holds and sharpens: the model is a rented, now near-free utility; portability (MCP, A2A under neutral governance) keeps it swappable; the moat is your proprietary data — which is precisely what the cheap tier is designed to acquire. Atlassian began training its Rovo AI on Confluence and Jira content by default from 17 August (~300,000 organizations; genuine opt-out reserved for Enterprise/Premium), and consumer free tiers of major assistants train on user content by default (as reported). Standing context: ~43% of organizations say they cannot audit the AI tools already in use, and only ~24% have implemented AI-specific data-loss-prevention controls (as reported). The tooling to route your grain into someone's larder is everywhere and mostly on-by-default; the control to stop it is rare.

The strategic core: when the model commoditizes toward free, value and risk both migrate to your data — and the cheapest model tier is the market's most efficient mechanism for acquiring it. For weeks the frame climbed the stack — own the layer, build the gate, own the knowledge, name the agent, speak the open tongue, conduct the ensemble, keep the ear, own the repertoire, take the census, face the inspector, shut the break-in, rebuild the mill's works. This morning it returns to the foundation and finds the ground shifting: the model is nearly free, and "nearly free" is a tier with a toll. "We got a great price on tokens" is not the whole sentence; the whole sentence is "we got a great price on tokens, and here is the data we agreed to pay with, and here is the control that keeps our seed stock out of the cheap mill."

Why "the miller's toll" is the layer that matters now

For two years the reflex was to treat the model as the expensive, differentiating part of the stack — pick the best one, pay the premium, guard the API key. This week broke that frame in public: capability is commoditizing toward free, and once the dollar price of a token approaches zero, the only price left to charge is your data. The cheapest tier on the market is now, explicitly, a data-acquisition instrument dressed as a discount. What separates a firm that banks the commoditization win from one that quietly hands over its moat is not a better model or a bigger budget — it is the discipline to price every model tier in two currencies, to classify data as common wheat or seed stock, and to enforce the split at the gateway rather than trust it to whoever writes the config.

How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now aimed at the model bill's invisible second line. On a legacy estate the temptation is to chase the ~21× discount everywhere, because the dollar saving is real and immediate and the data cost is silent. The retrofit is inventory, classify, gate, govern. Inventory: enumerate every model id in production and flag which ones train on your data (Contributor tiers, default-on free plans, train-by-default enterprise features). Classify: label your data as common wheat (no-secret, tradeable for cheap tokens) or seed stock (proprietary, regulated, never tolled). Gate: enforce at the AI gateway which data classes may reach which model ids, so the split is a control, not a request. Govern: make adopting a data-tolling model id a procurement-and-approval decision with a named owner, not a config change any engineer can make. And keep the whole refrain: the model is a rented stove — now nearly free — the knowledge is the larder, the gate is the one door, the passport names each agent, the census is the roll, the inspector asks you to produce it, the break-in is what the missing entries cost, the two mills are whether it grinds flour — and the miller's toll is what the cheapest grinding charges in your own grain.

7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

  1. Inventory every model id and flag the ones that train on your data. The dollar price of a token is collapsing, but the cheapest tiers charge a second price your cost dashboard cannot see. This month, enumerate every model and tier in production and mark which train on your prompts and completions — Contributor-style tiers, default-on free plans, train-by-default enterprise features (Atlassian Rovo defaulted ~300k orgs on 17 Aug). Report one number to the board: the share of production AI workloads whose data-training terms have been reviewed and named, versus those running on whatever model id a config happened to set.
  2. Classify your data as common wheat or seed stock — and enforce the split at the gateway. The near-free tier is a data-acquisition instrument; the defense is deciding, in advance, which grain you will trade for cheap tokens and which you never will. Label your data as tradeable (no-secret, non-regulated) or seed stock (proprietary, regulated, identifiable), and enforce at the AI gateway which classes may reach which model ids — so the split is a control, not a developer's good intention. Report the share of seed-stock data flows that are hard-blocked from data-tolling model ids versus those protected only by policy.
  3. Make adopting a data-tolling model id a procurement decision, not a config change. Today the switch to the cheap mill is one string an engineer can change unnoticed; that is the governance failure, not the price. Require that turning on any train-on-your-data tier is a named-owner procurement-and-approval step, and capture the ~21× discount deliberately on the common-wheat workloads where it is safe.
