The Passport

Wednesday, 26 August 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

Yesterday the radar found the moat — governed proprietary knowledge, the larder behind the gate. The day before, it named the gate itself. Today it asks the question the gate cannot answer alone: who, exactly, is standing at the door? This week the market moved to issue the papers. Okta brought Agent SSO to general availability, making an AI agent a first-class identity — registered, policy-governed, handed a short-lived scoped credential — via the open Cross App Access standard, inside a platform used by 20,000+ customers. The reason: Palo Alto Networks finds machine identities now outnumber humans ~109 to 1, up from ~82:1 a year ago, and ~79 of those 109 are AI agents. Yet only 10% of firms have a strategy to manage them, and 88% report an agent security incident.

1 · Executive Summary

For a week this radar walked the value up the stack; today it closes the loop with the one thing that makes a gate, a larder and an audit log actually work: a name for every agent. The shift is no longer "can an agent act?" or "through what door does it pass?" or "what does it draw on?" but "who is the agent, who does it answer to, what may it touch, and can we revoke it in an instant?" The market's answer this week: agent identity as a first-class, centrally governed control point.

1. The papers arrive — agents become first-class identities. In the last week of August, Okta brought Agent SSO to general availability, bringing the open Cross App Access (XAA) standard into an identity platform used by 20,000+ customers. It registers an AI agent in the same directory as employees, governs it with the same central policy, and issues short-lived, scoped tokens instead of static API keys. CEO Todd McKinnon: "AI agents are a powerful new identity type. They can act independently, on their own or on behalf of a user or a team or a company."

2. The census demands it — machines, mostly agents, dwarf people. Palo Alto Networks' 2026 Identity Security Landscape: machine identities now outnumber humans ~109 to 1 (up from ~82:1 a year ago), and ~79 of those 109 are AI agents (~72%). CyberArk puts the ratio at "more than 80 to 1" and calls identity the control plane for the AI era. The population you must govern is overwhelmingly non-human and increasingly agentic.

3. But the papers are missing — adoption has outrun identity. Okta's AI Agents at Work 2026 (Apprize360; 292 executives + 492 knowledge workers, seven countries): 91% already use AI agents, but only 10% have a developed non-human-identity strategy, 88% report a suspected or confirmed AI-agent incident, only 34% apply human-grade identity controls to agents, and only 22% treat an agent as an identity. The EU AI Act's Article 12 reconstructability logging (live since 2 Aug, fines up to €15M/3%) is unmeetable without it.

Bottom line: when machine actors outnumber people a hundred to one and most are agents acting on their own or a human's behalf, the enterprise control point is identity: a real, centrally issued, short-dated, revocable passport for every agent, checked at every door and written into every log. Stop treating agents as anonymous traffic: register every agent in the same directory as your people, govern it with the same policy, issue it a scoped, short-lived credential instead of a static key, and make sure you can revoke it in a second. When the workforce is mostly machines, the one thing that turns a crowd into a governed workforce is a name.

2 · Allegory — The Passport

A prosperous realm kept its borders easily while travelers were few and known. Then the roads filled — not with people but with couriers and stewards sent to act on others' behalf, a hundred for every citizen, tireless and everywhere at once. They did good work, but carried no papers of their own: to pass a door, a courier borrowed a citizen's signet, copied a key cut years ago and never recalled, or waved a letter no clerk had time to read. The realm gained a vast workforce and lost the one thing that makes a workforce governable — the ability to say, at any door, who is this, and on whose authority? When a strongroom went short, the magistrate's question — who did this? — met a shrug: the ledger held only a borrowed signet.

The realms that kept their footing built one passport office — the single place any traveler got papers. Each courier now carried a passport of its own: a name, a sponsor (it acts for this house, within these bounds), a stamp that expired (good for this errand, not a key honored forever), and an entry in one central register any gate could check and the office could revoke in an instant. The passport didn't slow the good couriers; it made them legible. But some realms did it badly — a different office per quarter, passports that never expired, papers no guard checked — and were no safer: a passport is only worth the one office that issues it, the expiry that limits it, the gate that checks it, and the register that can cancel it.

The question it forces: our systems are worked by a hundred machine travelers for every human, most of them agents acting on someone's behalf. Have we built the one passport office — every agent a first-class identity, its sponsor and bounds named, its authority short-dated, revocable in a second — or are our agents still moving on borrowed signets and old keys, unnamed in the ledger, so that when the magistrate asks who did this, all we can offer is a shrug?

A gate can only check papers the traveler carries; a log can only record a name the actor has. Identity is the precondition.

On legacy: machine identity is the estate's oldest weak point (long-lived service accounts, keys cut once and never recalled), and agents pour a hundredfold more onto it. Census, then passports, then a register: inventory every agent and non-human identity (you can't govern what you can't count); register each in your identity provider with a sponsor and bounded scope, replacing static keys with short-lived scoped tokens on an open standard (Cross App Access-class); make revocation instant and central, and wire every agent's identity into the Article-12 log. Keep the engine swappable behind it — the model is the commodity, the knowledge is the moat, and the identity that makes every agent legible is the control plane.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Take the census — count every agent and non-human identity. Inventory every AI agent and service account touching your systems — who sponsors it, what it can reach, what credential it uses, how you'd revoke it. Only 22% even treat an agent as an identity; machine IDs now run ~109 to a human. That inventory is the base map.

2. Issue the passports — make every agent a governed identity. Register your highest-risk agents in your identity provider, give each a sponsor and bounded scope, and replace static API keys and broad OAuth with short-lived scoped tokens — on an open agent-identity standard (Cross App Access-class) so the papers are portable, not locked to one tool. Prove it on one high-value flow first.

3. Keep the register — instant revocation and attributable logs. Make agent revocation instant and central, and wire every agent's identity into an Article-12-grade log so "which agent did what, for whom, under what authority" always has an answer. Report two numbers to the board: share of agents with a centrally governed identity, and mean time to revoke one.
