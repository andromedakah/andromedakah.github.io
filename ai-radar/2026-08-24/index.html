1 · Executive Summary 90-second read

For a week this radar walked the value up the stack and, yesterday, found the moat is the plumbing between record spend and a harvested P&L root. Today it names the one length of plumbing the whole market is now racing to lay: the agent gateway — a single governed chokepoint through which every agent's request to a model, a dataset or a tool must pass, so the enterprise can finally see, permission, meter and record what its agents actually do. The governing shift is no longer "can an agent act?" (the deputy), "who watches it?" (the watchman), "who is accountable?" (the guardrail) or "why is so little value reaching the P&L?" (the reservoir) — it is "through what single door does all of this pass, and have we built it?"

The datable signal — the wall is full of gaps. The reason a gate suddenly matters is that today most enterprises don't have one. Gravitee's survey (750 CIOs, CTOs and engineering leaders across the US and UK) finds 47% of the ~3 million AI agents these firms have deployed are not actively monitored or secured — roughly 1.5 million agents "at risk of going rogue" — and 88% have had or suspected an AI-agent security or data-privacy incident in the past twelve months. Beneath the agents sits an even less-governed layer: MCP servers, the connectors that wire agents into file systems, databases and APIs, adopted >400% in 2025 largely outside any security review, with 315 MCP vulnerabilities logged in 2025 (14.4% of all AI vulnerabilities) and a 270% jump quarter-over-quarter. And you cannot govern what you cannot see: IBM's Think 2026 research finds only 18% of organizations maintain a complete inventory of their AI agents. Gartner expects shadow AI to be a contributing factor in 40% of enterprise AI failures by 2027.

The move — the gatehouse gets built. The market's answer is a single governed door. At Black Hat 2026, Snowflake launched its Cortex AI Gateway: it enforces identity, policy and audit at the tool-call level, centralizes authentication, permissions and audit logging in one place across more than 100 MCP servers, and applies zero-copy boundaries, dynamic data masking and real-time exfiltration safeguards before an agent ever touches a row of data — built on Natoma (acquired May 2026) and launched with seven identity partners (1Password, Aembit, Cyera, Linx Security, Okta, SailPoint, Saviynt). It is not one product but a category: Forbes calls agent gateways "the control plane for enterprise AI," and analysts say MCP gateways are "crystallizing as infrastructure." The same door also meters the toll — VentureBeat frames the gateway as a way to control agents and prevent runaway costs, the same escalating-cost driver behind Gartner's forecast that 40%+ of agentic projects are cancelled by 2027.

2 · Allegory of the Day

🏰 "The Gatehouse"

Topic: Around 22–24 August 2026, the dominant enterprise-AI thread became the emergence of the agent/MCP gateway as the control plane for enterprise AI — the single governed point through which every agent's access to models, data and tools is routed, named, permissioned, metered and logged. Snowflake's Cortex AI Gateway, launched at Black Hat 2026 (identity, policy and audit at the tool-call level across 100+ MCP servers, with zero-copy boundaries, dynamic data masking and real-time exfiltration prevention, built on its May-2026 Natoma acquisition and seven identity partners), is the marquee example, with analysts calling MCP gateways "crystallizing as infrastructure" and Forbes calling agent gateways "the control plane for enterprise AI." The urgency is the ungoverned status quo: Gravitee finds 47% of ~3M deployed agents unmonitored (~1.5M at risk of going rogue) and 88% of firms hit by an agent incident; IBM finds only 18% keep a complete agent inventory; MCP adoption is up 400% with 315 logged vulnerabilities in 2025; Gartner expects shadow AI to factor in 40% of AI failures by 2027 and 40%+ of agentic projects cancelled over cost, value and risk. The EU AI Act's Article 12 reconstructability logging and Article 50 transparency duties (live since 2 August, fines up to €15M/3%) make audit-grade gateway logs a hard requirement. The lesson: after naming the deputy, hiring the watchman and building the guardrail, the market's converging answer is a single gate — build the one governed door every agent must pass.

There was a walled city that had grown rich on trade, and for a hundred years its wall had done its work. But the trade grew faster than the stone, and the city, impatient, did a careless thing: rather than widen its one great gate, it let the wall fall into a hundred small gaps — a breach here where a cart could pass, a gap there behind the tannery, a place by the river where the stone had simply crumbled and no one had troubled to mend it. And through those hundred gaps the trade poured. It was wonderfully fast. A carter no longer queued at the gate; he slipped in through whichever gap was nearest, did his business, and slipped out again the way he came. The merchants marveled at how quick the city had become, and pointed to the crowded markets as proof that the old, slow gate had only ever held them back.

But a wall with a hundred gaps is not a wall, and a city that cannot see who enters cannot govern what happens inside it. No one at the gaps asked a stranger his name, or what his papers permitted, or what lay under the cloth in his cart. No one weighed what he carried out against what he had carried in. And when a warehouse was found empty in the morning, or a fire started in a district no honest trader had reason to be in, there was no ledger anywhere in the city that could say who had passed, when, or where they had gone — because the passing had never been written down. The assessors who finally counted found the bitter arithmetic: of every hundred souls moving through the city on a given day, the watch could account for barely a handful; the rest had come and gone through gaps, unnamed and unrecorded, and a great many of them, it turned out, had no honest business there at all.

And the deeper danger was not the thief who slipped a gap on purpose; it was that the city had lost the very act of knowing. It could not meter a toll it never collected, could not enforce a law at a threshold it did not man, could not answer the magistrate who asked who did this because it had kept no record of anyone. Speed had been bought by tearing out the one thing a wall exists to provide: a single, watched, written place where the inside meets the outside on terms the city sets.

The cities that thrived did not tear the wall down, and — this is the part the careless city never grasped — they did not turn a single honest trader away. They did the plain, unglamorous thing: they mended the gaps and built one gatehouse, and they made every cart pass through it. At that one door a traveler gave his name; his papers were checked against what he was permitted to carry; his cargo was inspected before it ever reached the market, and dyed goods that must not leave were marked so a gap could not smuggle them out; a fair toll was metered on what he brought; and a clerk wrote in the ledger who had passed, what they carried, and where they were bound. It was slower than a gap by a minute, and it made the city richer and safer by a mile — because now the city could see, and a thing you can see you can permit, price, and prove. The one gate was not a wall against trade; it was the wall doing at last what a wall is for.

The moral: an agent that reaches your models, your data and your tools through a hundred unrecorded paths has not made you fast — it has torn out your wall. The point was never to keep agents out; it is to make them all pass one door you control, where each is named (identity), its papers checked (policy and permission), its cargo inspected (data-loss and exfiltration control), its toll metered (cost governance), and its passage logged (audit). The seduction is the gap: it is quicker this quarter to wire one more agent straight into the database than to route it through a gate. But a system whose agents come and go through gaps no one records is not agile; it is ungoverned, and it will be un-auditable on the day a regulator, or a breach, asks who did what. Mend the gaps, build the gatehouse, and make the rule absolute: no gate, no access.

The question it forces: We have wired our agents into our systems for speed, and most of them now come and go through gaps in the wall no one records — 47% unmonitored, only 18% even inventoried. Have we mistaken a wall full of holes for an open city? Have we built the one gatehouse every agent must pass — named, permissioned, inspected, metered and logged — or are we one breach, or one audit, away from discovering we cannot say who walked through, what they took, or how to prove it?

3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
  • Do our agents pass a gate, or a gap? Gravitee finds 47% of deployed agents unmonitored and IBM finds only 18% of firms keep a complete agent inventory. Can we produce, today, a single list of every agent that can reach our data and tools — and does every one of them route through one governed point, or straight into the system through a connection no one records?
  • Who owns the gatehouse? Agent gateways are becoming the control plane for enterprise AI — identity, policy, cost and audit at one door. Is there a named owner and a funded programme for that control plane, or is agent access still being wired ad-hoc, team by team, with no one accountable for the wall as a whole?
  • Could we answer "who did this?" The EU AI Act's Article 12 now requires logs built for full reconstructability. If a regulator or an incident asked us to replay exactly what an agent did, when, and on whose authority, could we — or have we kept no ledger because the agent walked in through a gap?
🏦 Financial Services
  • Credit, payments and customer data are exactly the "high-risk" categories where audit-grade gateway logs are becoming a hard requirement. Does every agent touching a regulated dataset pass a gate that enforces permission and records the passage, or do we have agents querying financial data through ungoverned MCP connections we could not defend to an examiner?
  • Runaway agent spend is a real P&L risk (reports of an enterprise burning through its AI budget in a single month). Is agent cost metered and capped at the gate, or can an agent loop run our token bill to a number no one authorized?
🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
  • Patient data demands provenance, minimization and an answer to "who accessed this." Do our clinical and research agents reach PHI only through a gate that masks, minimizes and logs — or have we let agents wire directly into systems that hold the most sensitive data we own?
  • Shadow MCP is the layer below the agent. Do we even know which MCP connectors are running against our clinical systems, or are 315-vulnerabilities-a-year connectors quietly bridging our agents to our patient data with no review?
🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
  • Agents touching OT, MES and supply-chain systems can move physical outcomes, not just rows. Is there one gate between our agents and our operational systems that enforces least privilege and records every command, or can an agent reach a line or a supplier through a gap?
  • Critical-infrastructure data falls squarely inside the EU AI Act's logging duties. Have we built the reconstructable audit trail at the gate, or is our compliance resting on connections no one inventoried?
🛒 Retail / Consumer
  • Consumer-facing and commerce agents sprawl across a large estate, each a potential gap. Do all of them — recommendation, service, pricing, fulfilment — pass one governed gateway, or are we counting agents we deployed but cannot see or meter?
  • Data exfiltration is the consumer-trust risk. Does the gate stop an agent from carrying customer data out (zero-copy, masking, exfiltration control) before it ever touches a row, or do we find out what left only after it has left?
🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
  • Public accountability means every automated action must be attributable. Can we name the agent, its authority and its full action trail for any decision taken in a citizen's case — or did the agent act through an ungoverned path we cannot reconstruct?
  • Procurement and statute both now assume a control plane. Are we buying or building the one gate that gives us identity, permission, cost-control and Article-12-grade logging, rather than bolting oversight onto agents already loose inside the wall?

Why "the control plane" is the artifact that matters

For a year the reflex was to chase the smartest model; this week's news says the defensible thing is the single governed point every agent passes. A gateway is model-agnostic and unglamorous — it does not make an agent smarter; it makes the whole fleet visible, permissioned, metered and provable. That is why the market is converging on it fast: a data platform (Snowflake) buys an MCP-gateway company (Natoma) and ships a gateway; the identity vendors consolidate around it (Cyera/Oasis, Okta/Permiso); the analysts name it "the control plane." The interesting artifact this week is not a benchmark and not a model — it is an architectural chokepoint you can either own or leave as a hundred holes.

How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — be deliberate about what you own, rent and finance, and on what terms. On legacy estates the danger is the seductive gap: it is quicker this quarter to wire one more agent straight into the database or bolt an MCP connector onto a workflow than to route it through a gate. That speed is borrowed against governability. The retrofit is gatehouse discipline. Mend the gaps: inventory every agent and every MCP server (only 18% can today), and make "no gate, no access" a hard rule. Build (or buy) the gatehouse: route all agent traffic through one control plane and put identity, policy, cost-metering and audit at that single door — don't scatter oversight across every team. And keep the engine swappable behind the gate — because the model is the commodity, and the governed door every agent passes is the moat.

7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

  1. Take the census — inventory every agent and every MCP server. You cannot govern what you cannot see, and only 18% of firms can see it. This month, produce one list of every agent that can reach your data or tools, and every MCP connector running in your estate, and mark which ones pass a governed point and which walk in through a gap. If you cannot build the list, that is the finding — you are the 82%, and Gravitee's 47%-unmonitored is your number too until proven otherwise.
  2. Build (or buy) the one gate — and make "no gate, no access" the rule. The market has named the pattern: route all agent traffic through a single control plane. Pick your gateway — build on the MCP standard or buy a Cortex-class gateway — and put the five checks at that one door: identity (name), policy (papers), data-loss/exfiltration control (cargo), cost metering (toll) and reconstructable audit (ledger). Then close the gaps: any agent or MCP connector that reaches data another way gets cut over to the gate or turned off.
  3. Wire the ledger to cost and to compliance. The same door that permits the agent should meter its spend and record its actions. Set token/cost caps at the gate (a direct defense against the 40%+ of agentic projects cancelled on runaway cost) and make the gate's logs Article-12-grade — built for full reconstructability, so that when a regulator or an incident asks "who did this, when, and on what authority," you answer from the ledger, not from silence. Report two numbers to the board next quarter: the share of agents that pass the gate, and the share of agent spend metered at it. The speed of the gap is borrowed; the ledger of the gate is what you own.
