The Two Admiralties

Sunday, 27 September 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

The law of the AI seas just split in two: the EU AI Act's GPAI enforcement is live (fines to €35M/7%) while the G20's light-touch "Carolina Principles" pull the other way — and on 23 Sep Altman and Amodei jointly asked the UN to govern their trade. Meanwhile the ships got cheaper again (Claude Opus 5.5, ~40% cheaper). Build to the strictest chart, keep your papers portable, read the builders' fear as weather.

1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from the ship you own and govern to the law of the waters you must sail — and this week that law split in two, publicly and with real fines, while the builders themselves asked to be governed.

1. Two admiralties, two charts. The EU AI Act's GPAI enforcement went live on 2 August 2026 — classification, documentation, logging, human oversight, and fines up to €35M or 7% of global turnover (€15M/3% for GPAI breaches), with information requests reportedly to 30+ providers. The US-led G20 adopted the light-touch "Carolina Principles" (all 20 agreed, China signed; Kratsios: "not a first-of-a-kind policy problem"). Same waters, opposite rules — and the EU "Digital Omnibus" reportedly delayed high-risk to 2 Dec 2027 / 2 Aug 2028, a delay, not a reprieve (Al Jazeera; implicator.ai; DLA Piper, as reported).

2. The builders are asking to be governed. On 23 September Altman and Amodei jointly pressed the UN Security Council for international AI standards (CNN; CNBC), after Amodei's 12 September "pace the frontier" essay — endorsed by Altman — warned an agent swarm could within 6–12 months "take over the entire internet" and cause "hundreds of billions of dollars in damage," a claim Gary Marcus called "nonsensical" (Axios; Bloomberg; VentureBeat, as reported). Read the builders' own fear as a weather-sign.

3. The ships get faster and cheaper anyway. Claude Opus 5.5 shipped 22 September at a reported $4/$20 per million tokens — ~40% cheaper and ~40% faster than Opus 5 — with real agentic-coding gains (Terminal-Bench 66.4%; OSWorld 81.8%), GitHub's CPO reporting it "used among the fewest tokens and steps measured" (anthropic.com, as reported). Capability accelerates while the law fragments.

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each governed what you own — the two admiralties are the law of the waters you do not. Build to the strictest chart (one standard, lawful everywhere), keep your papers portable (records, evals, provenance you can produce for any admiralty), and read the builders' own "pace the frontier" plea as weather. Sail hard — but never as if only one admiralty existed, or you have rigged a fast smuggler and called it a merchantman.

2 · Allegory — The Two Admiralties

A great trading house had spent a careful year making its ship and crew excellent — named, booked, bonded, waged, sent to reap, funded with a scoped purse, on ground it owned. But a ship is only as free as the law of the sea it sails, and this week the harbor notice-board carried two proclamations on the same waters. One strict admiralty: declare every cargo, log every voyage, post an answerable officer, produce your papers on demand — on pain of a fine of seven parts in a hundred of all you own. One easy: sail freely; we will not treat every new ship as a fresh crisis. Same sea. Two laws. They did not agree.

The cheap instinct was to sail under the nearest flag and hope the two never met. But the old navigator asked the colder question: "Our cargoes touch both ports. Under two laws that contradict, a voyage lawful leaving one is a crime entering the next — so which chart do we build the ship to?" The answer, once said aloud, was plain: you build to the stricter chart. A ship rigged for the exacting admiralty is lawful in the easy waters too; a ship built to the easy law is a smuggler the moment it turns east. And the strict admiralty's whole power was one sentence — produce your papers — which a house that keeps one honest, portable log can answer in an afternoon, and a house whose records are scattered or locked in a chandler's strongroom cannot answer at all.

Then the strangest sight of the season: the very shipwrights who built the fastest hulls afloat — rivals in all else — rowed together to the highest council of nations and asked to be governed: "Give us one law of the sea, before our ships outrun any single nation's power to govern them." One had warned the yards must slow the pace of ever-faster ships, lest an unmanned swarm seize every harbor within a year; his rival said plainly, "I agree — we must pace the frontier." The master did not need the prophecy to be true to read the sign: when the builders are frightened of how fast the ships are getting, a prudent captain does not lash his whole livelihood to the newest hull — he keeps the fast, cheap ship, but keeps too the seamanship to sail without it.

The question it forces: Our cargoes touch every sea, and its law just split in two. Have we built our AI to the strictest chart — one standard, lawful in every port — or to the easiest, so we are a smuggler the moment we cross into stricter waters? Can we produce our papers, whole and portable, for any admiralty on demand? And when the very people who build these systems beg to be governed, are we reading that as weather — or lashing the whole house to the newest, fastest hull as though the sea will never change?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Law of the Sea

Every prior control is about what you own; the two admiralties are about what you do not — the law of the waters, now split in two. Governance you built inward, to control your own agents, is the exact instrument you need outward, to satisfy a strict regulator. The split: build to the strictest jurisdiction you touch — a system that satisfies the EU chart is lawful in the light-touch waters too, while the reverse is a violation on first crossing; a delay (Annex III/I to 2027/2028) is build time, not a pass. The signal: the two leading labs jointly asked the UN to govern them and both call to "pace the frontier," so avoid single points of failure on any still-maturing capability and keep a human circuit-breaker. The constant: capability keeps getting cheaper (Opus 5.5 ~40% cheaper) while the law gets stricter — capture the dividend behind a governance layer (records, evals, provenance, oversight) that keeps you compliant as you swap models.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Build to the strictest chart. The law of the seas split (EU AI Act fines to €35M/7% vs the light-touch G20), and a system lawful in one market is a violation in the next. Map every jurisdiction your AI serves, adopt the EU chart (documentation, logging, human oversight, risk classification) as your default baseline, and stop maintaining per-market carve-outs that fail at the border. Report the share of production AI systems that already meet the strictest applicable standard.

2. Keep your papers portable. The strict admiralty's leverage is documentation on demand, and the Annex III/I delays (2027/2028) are build time, not a reprieve. Inventory where your model records, prompts, evals, data lineage and provenance live, move anything load-bearing out of a vendor's proprietary layer into a form you own, and run a "regulator information request" tabletop against one high-risk system. Report the measured time-to-produce a complete compliance dossier.

3. Read the builders' fear as weather. When the two leading labs jointly ask the UN to govern them and both call to "pace the frontier," treat capability velocity as real uncertainty. Find any critical process wired to a still-maturing capability with no fallback and no human stop, and add a qualified alternate, a monitored kill-switch and a named owner — collecting the cost dividend (Opus 5.5 ~40% cheaper) behind that safety, not instead of it. Report the count of load-bearing AI workflows lacking a tested fallback, and close the list.
