# 🗓️ AI Tech Radar — The Far Bank

**Monday, 17 August 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering**

> For a month this radar has argued the model is a commodity and the moat is the layer you own around it — the controls, the compute, the pilotage, the last mile, the business model. Today it turns to the uncomfortable question underneath the whole enterprise-AI project: **after all the spend, how much of it actually crossed over into value?** The answer, hardening this week into hard numbers, is *not much.* **MIT's Project NANDA — "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business" — finds that 95% of enterprise generative-AI pilots deliver no measurable P&L impact; only 5% cross into real value.** And **Gartner expects more than 40% of agentic-AI projects to be cancelled by the end of 2027** — for escalating cost, unclear value and inadequate risk controls — even as it forecasts **40% of enterprise apps will embed task-specific agents by year-end, up from under 5% a year ago.** The tell is that the failures are *not* about model quality: they are about the crossing — integration, data, people and governance. The board's question this morning: ***of everything we have piloted, what has actually reached the far bank and shown up in the P&L — and are we buying a faster boat when what we lack is the bridge?***

## 1 · Executive Summary (90-second read)

For a month this radar has said the model is a commodity and the durable advantage is the layer you own around it. Today the map turns to the hardest, least glamorous truth in enterprise AI: **most of the spend never crosses from pilot to value.** Not because the engine is weak — it has never been cheaper or better — but because the **crossing** from a working demo to a booked P&L result is an integration, data, workflow, people and governance problem, and that is where almost everyone runs aground. The scarce discipline this week is not buying a smarter model. It is **finishing the bridge.**

**The datable signal — the reality check hardens.** The body of evidence crystallizing this month is blunt. **MIT Project NANDA's "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business"** — built on 52 executive interviews, a survey of ~150 leaders and analysis of ~300 public deployments — reports that **95% of enterprise gen-AI pilots produce no measurable P&L impact, and only 5% create significant value.** Its diagnosis is the part that should stop a boardroom: the failure is **not the quality of the model** but a **"learning gap"** in tools and organizations — and the single sharpest correlate of success is *how* you build. Pilots that paired internal teams with outside expertise hit a **67% success rate; internal-IT-only builds, just 22%.** Alongside it, **Gartner** projects **more than 40% of agentic-AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027** (cost, unclear value, weak risk controls), while separately forecasting **40% of enterprise apps will carry task-specific agents by end-2026, up from <5%** — a boom and a bust filed in the same cabinet. This is the through-line for enterprise AI right now: **adoption is near-universal, value is not.**

**Why it matters more than any single launch.** Read against a month of "the model is a commodity," the divide is the natural consequence: when the engine is abundant and cheap, *owning a great engine buys you nothing* — the advantage is entirely in the crossing, and the crossing is hard, unglamorous work most organizations under-resource. The evidence stacks up on the governance side too: **88% of enterprises confirmed or suspected an AI-agent security incident in the past year** (Gravitee's *State of AI Agent Security 2026*, 900+ respondents), and shadow-AI surveys find **~78% of employees use AI tools with no company policy in place and ~58% admit pasting sensitive data into them** — so the 95% that never reach value are, many of them, still leaking data on the near bank. Meanwhile the engine kept commoditizing right through the week: open-weight **Z.ai GLM-5.3** and **Alibaba Qwen3.8** shipped **14 August**, **xAI's Grok 4.6** (12 Aug) tied GPT-5.6 Sol Max at the top of the intelligence index at **$2/$6 per 1M**, **Grok 3 was retired 15 August**, and **Palantir posted ~93% revenue growth** — bookable AI money, on the far bank, for the few who cross. The brain gets better and cheaper by the week; the bridge does not build itself.

1. **95% of pilots never cross — and it isn't the model's fault.** MIT NANDA puts the number on a suspicion every operator already had: near-universal experimentation, almost no P&L. The cause is the crossing — integration, data quality, workflow fit, adoption and governance — not model capability. **Stop treating "we ran a pilot" as progress; treat "it reached the P&L" as the only milestone that counts.**

2. **The agent boom and the agent bust are the same story.** Gartner sees **40% of apps embedding agents by year-end** *and* **>40% of agentic projects cancelled by end-2027.** Both are true because pace of adoption and rate of value have decoupled. **Fund agents against a realized-value gate, not a demo — and expect to kill the ones that can't show it.**

3. **The near bank is where the data leaks.** With **88% reporting an agent-security incident** and most shadow-AI use running with no policy, the pilots that stall don't stay harmless — they accumulate ungoverned access and exposure. The trust gate is live to meet it: the **EU AI Act's transparency duties have applied since 2 August** (disclose AI, label output; fines up to €15M/3%, and up to €35M/7% for prohibited practice) and the **AITSC consortium launched 12 August.**

**Bottom line:** the model is a commodity, the moat is the layer you own — and this week the numbers showed how few organizations have actually built that layer well enough to cross. **95% of pilots produce no measurable value; the 5% that do won by finishing the bridge — pairing inside and outside talent, fixing the data and the workflow, and governing the whole crossing — not by buying a bigger engine.** Count what reached the far bank, resource the crossing like the hard project it is, and kill the boats that will never make it across.

## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Far Bank"

*Topic: The reality-check evidence hardening in August 2026 — MIT Project NANDA's "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business" finding that 95% of enterprise gen-AI pilots deliver no measurable P&L impact and only 5% create significant value, with the decisive factor being how you build (external partnership 67% success vs internal-only 22%) rather than model quality; Gartner's forecast that >40% of agentic-AI projects will be cancelled by end-2027 even as 40% of enterprise apps embed agents by end-2026; the governance backdrop of 88% of enterprises reporting an AI-agent security incident and pervasive ungoverned shadow-AI use. The lesson: when the engine is a cheap commodity, value lives entirely on the far side of the crossing — integration, data, people and governance — and almost everyone runs aground trying to buy a faster boat instead of building the bridge. The river / far-bank allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

Picture a great river, and on the near bank a fairground that never sleeps. Every week the enginewrights roll out a newer, cheaper, more dazzling engine, and the crowd gathers to watch it roar — faster than last week's, and cheaper, and louder in the demonstration yard. The whole near bank is a carnival of horsepower. And across the water, dim through the spray, lies the **far bank** — the only ground where any of it is worth anything, because the far bank is where the mills turn and the goods are made and the coin is actually counted. Everyone on the near bank can *see* the far bank. Almost no one is standing on it.

For there is a hard fact the carnival does not advertise: an engine on the near bank grinds no grain. To be worth a copper it must be carried across, installed in a real mill, fed real water, tended by real millers who trust it, and made to run day after day in the rain. And the crossing is treacherous — not because the engine is weak, but because the *river* is wide: the banks must be joined, the roads on the far side rebuilt to reach the mills, the millers taught and won over, the tolls and the customs satisfied. Of every hundred who buy an engine and set out, the tale of the valley is now told in a hard number: **five reach the far bank and turn a mill. Ninety-five do not** — their engines rust in the mud at the water's edge, magnificent and idle, while their owners insist the next engine, faster still, will surely make the crossing this time.

The five who crossed were not the ones with the finest engine. They were the ones who understood that the engine was never the problem, and spent their effort on the **bridge.** They yoked their own people to outside bridge-builders who had crossed before — and the valley's ledgers show it plainly: those who built with both hands, inside and out, reached the far bank **three times as often** as those who tried to throw the span with household labour alone. They rebuilt the muddy roads on the far side so a cart could actually reach a mill (the dull work of data and workflow that no demonstration ever shows). They trained the millers until the engine was trusted on the foggiest morning, not just cheered on the fair-weather day of the demo. The crossing was slow, and unglamorous, and it was the whole of the work — and at the end of it they owned something no near-bank carnival could sell: a mill that ran.

And there was, of course, a toll-keeper on the bridge — newly posted, and not to be waved past. The realm now required that every cart crossing declare its cargo honestly, that no engine pose as a human hand, and that the crossing be logged and licensed — for the near bank had filled with unwatched engines wandering into millhouses they were never granted, and the valley had learned, at a cost of *nearly nine mills in ten touched by some mishap,* that an ungoverned engine idling at the water's edge is not harmless: it is a fire waiting for a wind. The keeper did not slow the five who had done the work; their manifests were already in order. It slowed the ninety-five who had hoped to rush an untended engine across in the dark.

**The moral:** when engines are cheap and everywhere, the engine is never the prize — the **far bank** is, and the far bank is reached only by building the bridge: the integration, the data, the trained people and the governance that carry a working demonstration into a running mill. Do not mistake the roar of a new engine on the near bank for a mill that turns on the far one. Count the mills, not the engines. And know that the ninety-five who never cross do not merely waste their coin — they leave magnificent, ungoverned machinery rusting and smoking at the water's edge, which is its own kind of danger.

**The question it forces:** *The finest, cheapest engines in history are rolling out weekly, and ninety-five of every hundred enterprises that buy one never turn a single mill with it — not because the engine is weak, but because they never built the bridge. Of everything we have piloted, how many have actually reached the far bank and shown up in our P&L — and are we about to buy another faster engine when what we are missing is the crossing itself: the integration, the clean data, the trained people and the governance that finish the bridge?*

## 3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

### 🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
- **How many of our AI pilots have actually reached the P&L — and do we count crossings or launches?** MIT NANDA says 95% of pilots produce no measurable value. **For every AI initiative, can we state the realized value it has booked, not the demo it passed?** If our dashboard counts pilots started rather than mills turning, we are measuring the near bank and mistaking it for the far one.
- **Are we resourcing the crossing, or just the engine?** The decisive success factor was *how* teams built — external partnership hit 67% success versus 22% for internal-only. **Have we budgeted integration, data, change-management and outside expertise as the main line item — or did the money go to models and licences while the bridge went unfunded?**
- **What is our kill discipline for agents that can't show value?** Gartner expects >40% of agentic projects cancelled by 2027. **Do we have an explicit realized-value gate at which a stalled agent is retired — or will ours drift on, funded and ungoverned, until someone notices the cost?**

### 🏦 Financial Services
- The 95% that stall don't stay harmless: 88% of enterprises report an agent-security incident, and shadow-AI use runs largely without policy. **Can we prove that every AI pilot — including the abandoned ones — has its access revoked, its data flows logged, and no client-confidential information sitting in a tool that never made it to production?**
- Value discipline is your native language. **Are we applying the same realized-return, risk-adjusted gate to AI initiatives that we apply to any book of business — and retiring the positions that don't clear it — or is "we're piloting AI" getting a pass that no trading desk would get?**

### 🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
- A clinical or discovery workflow that "demos" is worthless until it runs reliably on messy, real-world data. **Are our AI programs funded to cross — integration into the EHR/LIMS, validation on real records, clinician trust and adoption — or are we admiring pilots that will never touch a patient pathway?**
- Ungoverned pilots are a patient-data risk. **Can we guarantee no protected health information is sitting in a stalled or shadow AI tool, and that every deployment carries the disclosure, labeling and audit trail the EU AI Act now expects?**

### 🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
- You have always known that a machine in the crate is not a machine on the line. **Apply that instinct to AI: are we counting installed, running, value-producing systems — or engines still on the near bank? And have we funded the "far-side roads" — the data plumbing and workflow integration — that let a model actually reach the floor?**
- Reliability is your discipline. **Are we grading AI agents on sustained, production reliability against real inputs — not one clean demo — and pairing our own engineers with outside builders who have crossed before, the way the 67% did?**

### 🛒 Retail / Consumer
- Adoption without value is a trap you know from every channel fad. **Of our AI pilots in merchandising, service and supply chain, how many have moved a real number — conversion, margin, cost-to-serve — versus how many are impressive demos we keep funding out of fear of missing out?**
- Speed of experimentation is fine; ungoverned sprawl is not. **Do we know how many AI tools our teams are using without review, and is proprietary pricing or customer data sitting in any of them?**

### 🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
- Public money cannot fund a 95% failure rate quietly. **For every AI program, can we report realized public value and the cost of the pilots that never crossed — and have we built the outside-partnership model that tripled success in the private sector?**
- Citizen trust dies on the near bank. **Can we guarantee no citizen data sits in an abandoned or shadow AI pilot, and that every live deployment meets the EU AI Act's disclosure, labeling and logging duties — with a named owner accountable for the crossing?**

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — The Crossing, Not the Engine

Read the stack, once more, as layers priced very differently — but this week look past *which layer you buy* to **whether any of it crosses into value,** because that is the variable the data just made undeniable. At the **bottom** is the *engine* — the raw model, so abundant that open-weight frontier releases land weekly and the per-token price races toward zero. Above it are the **rails** — agent infrastructure (MCP and its kin), trending to free. Above those sit the layers this month's editions mapped — the **pilotage** (context, reliability), the **last mile** (distribution), the **business model** (how the vendor gets paid). And running *across all of them,* separating a pilot from a P&L, is the thing that is not a layer at all but a **crossing:** integration, data quality, workflow fit, adoption and governance. This week the market measured the crossing and found almost no one on the far side. The engineering point is blunt: **capability is no longer the constraint — the crossing is. A model that benchmarks at the frontier and a pilot that dazzles in the demo yard are worth exactly nothing until they are integrated, trusted, adopted and governed in production, and 95% never get there.**

- **The engine — abundant, commoditizing, still shipping weekly.** Open-weight **Z.ai GLM-5.3** and **Alibaba Qwen3.8** landed **14 August**; **xAI's Grok 4.6** (12 Aug) tied **GPT-5.6 Sol Max** at the top of the Artificial Analysis intelligence index at **$2/$6 per 1M**; **Grok 3 was retired 15 August**; and **Palantir posted ~93% revenue growth** — the rare case of AI money booked on the far bank. Atop Opus 5, Kimi K3 and DeepSeek V4-Pro, the engine is a swappable, cheapening part. *None of this abundance moves the 95%.* The constraint was never the engine.
- **The rails and the higher layers — necessary, not sufficient.** The **Model Context Protocol's 2026-07-28 specification** went stateless (110M+ monthly SDK downloads; TS & Python SDKs each past 1B), the **pilotage** took Databricks to $190B, the **last mile** is what OpenAI rented from IBM. Every one of these is real and each helps *some* crossings — but the divide persists *above* all of them, in the enterprise's own ability to integrate, clean its data, win adoption and govern the result. You can buy the best of every layer and still stall on the near bank.
- **The crossing — where the value is won or lost.** This is the "layer" the reality-check data exposed. **MIT NANDA:** **95%** of pilots → no measurable P&L; **5%** → significant value; decisive factor is *build model* — **external partnership 67% success vs 22% internal-only** — and a **"learning gap,"** not model quality. **Gartner:** **>40%** of agentic projects cancelled by end-2027; **40%** of apps embedding agents by end-2026. **Governance:** **88%** report an agent-security incident (Gravitee); **~78%/~58%** shadow-AI use/data-pasting (Anagram); most agentic deployments lack agent-specific policy. The crossing is an organizational and engineering discipline — and it is the whole game.

The strategic core: **the engine is cheap, the rails are free, and the value is on the far bank — reached by the 5% who built the crossing, not bought a bigger boat.** For a month the misread was "own the right layer." This week's correction is sharper: **owning the layers is table stakes; the return comes only from the crossing — integration, data, adoption and governance — and 95% never finish it.** "We piloted the best model" is not the answer to "what value reached the P&L, and is it governed"; ***"we built the bridge — paired inside and outside talent, fixed the data and the workflow, won adoption, and governed the whole crossing"*** is the answer.

| Leave stalled pilots running, ungoverned | Govern — or revoke — every crossing, live and abandoned |

### Why it's the crossing, not the capability

Every layer of the stack this month presumed the brain was cheap and swappable — and this week two more frontier engines shipped to prove it. The reason the *crossing* belongs at the centre of the picture is that **the models already work; the enterprises around them mostly don't cross.** MIT's own diagnosis is that the failure is a **"learning gap"** in tools and organizations, not a capability gap in the model — which is why a better model cannot fix it and a better *build model* can. A pilot that pairs internal knowledge with outside crossing experience succeeds three times as often as one thrown across by household IT alone. None of that is about tokens. All of it is about the unglamorous engineering and organizational work of integration, data, adoption and governance — the bridge. The correct read of this week is not "AI doesn't work" — it plainly does for the 5% — it is "**capability is abundant and value is scarce, and the gap between them is a crossing you have to build.**"

### How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — **be deliberate about what you own, rent and finance, and on what terms.** On legacy estates the divide is widest, because the far-side roads are worst: brittle integrations, dirty data, siloed systems and change-resistant workflows are exactly the terrain a pilot cannot cross. The retrofit is a **crossing discipline. Count crossings, not launches:** make realized P&L value — not "a pilot ran" — the only reported milestone. **Fund the bridge:** put the budget into integration, data quality, adoption and outside partnership (the 67% pattern), not into another model licence. **Prove it on real data:** grade every candidate on sustained production reliability against messy inputs, not a clean demo. **Post the toll-keeper:** govern every deployment — and revoke the access of every abandoned pilot — under the EU AI Act's disclosure, labeling and logging duties, because the near bank is where 88% of the incidents happen. And keep the engine swappable underneath — re-benchmark GLM-5.3, Qwen3.8, Grok 4.6 and the next release freely, because the brain is the commodity and *the crossing is the moat.*

**The clean mental model:** *The model is the engine — abundant, cheap, paid by the token, roaring on the near bank, never your moat. The rails, the pilotage, the last mile and the business model are the layers you buy or own. And running across all of them is the crossing — integration, data, adoption and governance — the only thing that carries a pilot to the far bank where value is counted. This week the numbers said 95% never cross, and the 5% that do win by building the bridge, not buying a faster boat. Count the mills, not the engines — and govern the ungoverned machinery rusting at the water's edge.*

## 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. **Count crossings, not launches — put realized value on the board dashboard.** Replace "pilots started" with "value booked in the P&L" as the reported milestone for every AI initiative. For each program, record the realized result, the cost to date, and whether it has actually reached production. MIT NANDA says 95% won't have — so the exercise will be uncomfortable, and that is the point. **You cannot manage a divide you refuse to measure, and a fairground of pilots is not a portfolio of value.**

2. **Fund the bridge, not another engine — and build with both hands.** Redirect budget from model licences toward the crossing: integration, data quality, workflow redesign, adoption and change-management — and pair your internal teams with outside builders who have crossed before, the pattern that tripled success (67% vs 22%). Grade candidates on sustained reliability against messy production data, not a clean demo. **The engine is the cheap part; the bridge is the moat — resource it like one.**

3. **Post the toll-keeper — govern and revoke, live and abandoned alike.** Stand up agent-specific governance now: inventory every AI tool and agent (including shadow and stalled ones), revoke the access of pilots that didn't cross, and log and label every live deployment under the EU AI Act's disclosure duties. With 88% reporting an agent incident and most shadow-AI use running unpoliced, the ungoverned machinery rusting on the near bank is your live risk. **Kill the boats that will never cross — before they catch a wind.**
