1 · Executive Summary

For a month this radar has said the model is a commodity and the moat is the layer you own around it. Today the map turns to the hardest truth in enterprise AI: most of the spend never crosses from pilot to value — not because the engine is weak, but because the crossing is an integration, data, people and governance problem.

1. 95% of pilots never cross — and it isn't the model's fault. MIT Project NANDA's "The GenAI Divide" (~52 interviews, ~150 leaders, ~300 deployments) finds 95% of enterprise gen-AI pilots deliver no measurable P&L impact; only 5% create significant value. The cause is a "learning gap," not model quality — and the sharpest correlate of success is how you build: external-partnership builds hit 67% success versus 22% for internal-IT-only.

2. The agent boom and the agent bust are the same story. Gartner sees 40% of apps embedding agents by end-2026 (from <5%) and >40% of agentic projects cancelled by end-2027 (cost, unclear value, weak risk controls). Adoption is near-universal; value is not. Fund agents against a realized-value gate, and kill the ones that can't show it.

3. The near bank is where the data leaks. 88% of enterprises report a confirmed/suspected AI-agent security incident (Gravitee, 900+); shadow-AI surveys put ~78% of employees using AI with no policy and ~58% pasting sensitive data. The stalled pilots are not harmless. The trust gate is live: EU AI Act transparency since 2 Aug (fines up to €15M/3%, up to €35M/7%); AITSC launched 12 Aug.

Bottom line: the model is a commodity, the moat is the layer you own — and this week the numbers showed how few have built it well enough to cross. 95% of pilots produce no measurable value; the 5% that do won by finishing the bridge — pairing inside and outside talent, fixing the data and workflow, governing the whole crossing — not by buying a bigger engine. Count the mills, not the engines.

2 · Allegory — The Far Bank

Picture a great river, and on the near bank a fairground that never sleeps — every week a newer, cheaper, more dazzling engine roars in the demonstration yard. Across the water lies the far bank, the only ground where any of it is worth anything, because that is where the mills turn and the coin is counted. Everyone can see it; almost no one stands on it. For an engine on the near bank grinds no grain: it must be carried across, installed, fed, tended by millers who trust it, and run day after day in the rain. The crossing is treacherous — not because the engine is weak, but because the river is wide. Of every hundred who set out, the valley's tale is now a hard number: five reach the far bank and turn a mill; ninety-five do not, their engines rusting in the mud while their owners insist the next, faster engine will surely cross.

The five who crossed were not those with the finest engine — they were those who spent their effort on the bridge: they yoked their own people to outside builders who had crossed before (and reached the far bank three times as often), rebuilt the muddy roads of data and workflow, and trained the millers until the engine was trusted on the foggiest morning. And a toll-keeper now stands on the bridge, requiring every cart to declare its cargo, no engine to pose as a human hand, and the crossing to be logged — because the near bank had filled with unwatched engines, and the valley learned, at a cost of nearly nine mills in ten touched by mishap, that an ungoverned engine at the water's edge is a fire waiting for a wind.

The question it forces: the finest, cheapest engines in history ship weekly, and ninety-five of a hundred enterprises never turn a mill with one — not for want of a better engine, but for want of a bridge. Of everything we have piloted, how many have reached the far bank and shown up in our P&L — and are we about to buy another faster engine when what we lack is the crossing: the integration, the clean data, the trained people and the governance that finish the bridge?

3 · Technical — The Crossing, Not the Engine

The engine — cheap, shipping weekly: open-weight GLM-5.3 & Qwen3.8 (14 Aug), Grok 4.6 tying GPT-5.6 Sol Max at $2/$6 (12 Aug), Grok 3 retired (15 Aug), Palantir ~+93% revenue, atop Opus 5, Kimi K3, DeepSeek V4-Pro — none of it moves the 95%. The rails and higher layers (MCP stateless, SDKs past 1B; pilotage; last mile) are necessary, not sufficient — the divide persists above them. The crossing — where value is won or lost: integration, data quality, workflow fit, adoption and governance. Capability is no longer the constraint; the crossing is.

Keep the engine swappable — the brain is the commodity, the crossing is the moat.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Count crossings, not launches. Replace "pilots started" with "value booked in the P&L" as the reported milestone for every AI initiative — record the realized result, the cost to date, and whether it reached production. MIT says 95% won't have; that discomfort is the point. You cannot manage a divide you refuse to measure.

2. Fund the bridge, not another engine — build with both hands. Redirect budget from model licences to the crossing: integration, data quality, workflow redesign, adoption — and pair internal teams with outside builders who have crossed before (67% vs 22%). Grade candidates on production reliability against messy data, not a clean demo.

3. Post the toll-keeper — govern and revoke. Inventory every AI tool and agent (shadow and stalled included), revoke the access of pilots that didn't cross, and log and label every live deployment under the EU AI Act. With 88% reporting an agent incident, the ungoverned machinery rusting on the near bank is your live risk.
