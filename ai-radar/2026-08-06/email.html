1 · Executive Summary

For a month this radar has argued the model is a commodity, so the durable advantage is the layer you own around it. Every edition described the layer; almost none of it existed as a product. Today one piece shipped. On 5 August 2026 Anthropic launched Inference hooks (beta) for Claude Enterprise — the first vendor-native control point the enterprise itself owns in the path between its data and the rented model. When an employee submits a prompt on a governed surface (claude.ai chat, Claude Code, Cowork), Anthropic routes the transcript to your own AI security server and waits for an allow-or-deny verdict before inference runs — a denied request never reaches the model. It's signed (Standard Webhooks), answered within a timeout you set (5s default); your server sees the transcript, tool calls and their results, and extracted text — never raw bytes, system prompts, or internal context.

1. The control point is yours, not the vendor's. Anthropic built the socket so your guard stands in the path and your server holds the stamp. You decide allow/deny; you keep the failure policy; you own the log. Model safety is theirs; data control is now yours.

2. It is inline, not after-the-fact. Audit tools tell you what already happened; Inference hooks stop a prompt before it reaches the model — an enforcement point, not just an inventory.

3. Real, and early — know the gaps. Beta, Claude Enterprise only; not on Bedrock or Google Cloud; no voice; allow/deny only (no redaction); raw image bytes not read; response-side blocking on the roadmap.

Bottom line: the month said own the layer around the commodity model; today the enterprise can buy the first bolt of it. Stand up the AI security server, run it in shadow mode this week, and make the allow/deny verdict yours — because the model is rented and swappable, but the one door your secrets leave through should be manned by your crew, on your ground, for every vendor.

2 · Allegory — The Airlock

A great vessel carries something precious at one atmosphere of your own pressure — your cargo, your charts, your crew's private words. Outside the hull is a medium you cannot breathe: the vacuum, the rented brain beyond your walls. Between the two there is one lawful passage, and it is not a door but a chamber with two doors never open at once — the airlock. For years the enterprise had none: whatever an employee typed simply left, and the only record was a logbook kept after the crossing — useful for grief, useless for prevention. This week the fitting for a real airlock was welded in at the one place your cargo leaves for the void. A message enters the chamber; the inner door seals; and your own inspector, not the vendor's, calls allow or deny. On allow the outer door opens; on deny it never breaks seal, and nothing of yours reaches the void. You can run it with the lock disengaged to watch (shadow mode), open it to a fraction first, exempt a trusted few, and write the drill for when the inspector sleeps.

Two truths keep the crew honest. The vendor built the socket, not the guard — the shipwright cut a fitting into the hull so your door, your lock, your inspector stand in the only passage to the void. That is the month's thesis in iron: own the layer around the commodity model. And the airlock is new, not yet whole: it doesn't fit the other ships in your fleet (Bedrock, Google Cloud), can't inspect a sealed crate you only see the label of (an image), watches what comes back only on the next cycle, and can bar the door but not repack the cargo. A partial airlock is a great advance — but a captain who calls it a sealed hull drowns on the hatch he forgot.

The question it forces: When our people speak to the rented brain, is there a chamber between our world and the void — and who holds the stamp inside it, us or the vendor? If the answer is "the model provider keeps us safe," we've described their engine, not our airlock. Where is the single passage our data takes to every model; whose inspector calls allow or deny; and have we demanded the same fitting from every vessel in the fleet?

3 · Technical — Own the Checkpoint, Rent the Model

The vacuum — the commodity model (rented, swappable): Opus 5 (#1, $5/$25), Gemini 3.6 Flash, GPT-5.6 Sol, Kimi K3 open weights, DeepSeek V4-Flash-0731 (MIT). Its safety is the provider's; the vacuum was never where your advantage lived. The airlock — the control point you own (shipped this week): a governed prompt goes to your AI security server for an allow/deny verdict before inference; a deny never reaches the model. Runs on Anthropic's servers, covers claude.ai / Claude Code / Cowork (web/desktop/CLI), nothing installed; signed (Standard Webhooks), 5s default timeout; server sees transcript + tool calls/results + extracted text, never raw bytes/system prompts/internal context; you choose shadow mode, rollout %, role exclusions, and the failure policy. The hatches still open: beta; Claude Enterprise only; not Bedrock/GCP; no voice; allow/deny only (no redaction); raw image bytes not read; response-side blocking on the roadmap.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Build the airlock — stand up an AI security server and run it in shadow mode this week. Point it at the DLP infrastructure you already run (Netskope, Palo Alto, Proofpoint, Zscaler, or in-house), turn on shadow mode so it observes live traffic without blocking, and read what your people actually send to the model. A week of shadow-mode logs teaches more about your exposure than a quarter of policy documents — and you'll have built the enforcement point, not just the inventory.

2. Own the verdict and set the failure policy deliberately. The decision that keeps your data yours is allow/deny, and it must sit on your server. Decide, per surface, what happens when your inspector is unreachable — fail-closed for regulated data or customer money, fail-open only where availability truly outweighs egress risk — and keep a per-denial record in your own log. Report one metric to the board: the share of AI traffic that passes a checkpoint you control.

3. Make it model-neutral and map the open hatches. The pattern is the asset: write the checkpoint as a webhook contract you can demand from every model vendor, so swapping Opus 5 for Gemini or GPT-5.6 never re-opens the door. Then list the hatches this fitting doesn't yet close — Bedrock/GCP surfaces, voice, image content, response-side blocking — assign each an owner, and treat them as the next fittings. Don't call a partial airlock a sealed hull.
