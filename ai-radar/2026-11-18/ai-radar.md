# 🗓️ AI Tech Radar — The Reckoning

**Wednesday, 18 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering**

> All year the radar built the governed house — named every agent (the livery), kept one book (the steward), put your name on the bond (the surety), checked its wage (the second wage), sent it to reap (the two harvests), handed it a scoped purse (the purse), and refused to let the vendor pour the floor (the foundation). Every one of those governs what the servant **is**, **may do**, **serves** or **spends**. This morning the market shipped the control none of them touch: the day the books come due. At **Microsoft Ignite 2026 (17 November)**, Satya Nadella unveiled **Agent 365 Business Analytics** — "you can think of this as your **ERP system for AI**, helping organizations **connect spend to business value** and continuously **optimize their return on intelligence**" (Satya Nadella, X, 17 Nov 2026, as reported) — alongside **Agent 365 Cost Management** (preview) and a claim that **more than 50 million agents** already run on the platform. In the same keynote Microsoft shipped **seven new "MAI" models** that push commoditization another notch — **MAI Code 2** reportedly reaching **~98% of GPT-6.1 Sol's quality at ~40% of the price**, **MAI Image 2.7** topping an Artificial Analysis image-editing board **at ~a fifth of the cost** — and called Copilot "**the new OS for work**" (Microsoft; TestingCatalog; DEV Community, as reported). Strip the branding and the message is a reckoning: the stove got cheaper again, the house is full of 50-million agents, and now the same vendor hands you the ledger that asks the one question a year of governance never forced — **not is it named, scoped, logged and funded, but did it earn its keep?** The answer the surveys give is bleak: **56% of CEOs report zero measurable ROI** from AI (PwC, Jan 2026), only **23% see significant ROI from AI agents** (WRITER, 2,400 leaders), **95% of enterprise AI pilots show no measurable P&L impact** (MIT/Domino, as reported), and McKinsey still counts only **~6%** of firms as AI "high performers." The board question is no longer *how many agents do we run* — it is **what is our return on intelligence, who keeps that scorecard, and will it ever tell us to spend less?**

## 1 · Executive Summary (90-second read)

For a year the radar worked the servant's identity, loyalty, output and spend. **This morning the axis turns to the account** — because the day Microsoft shipped the ledger is the day the house can no longer not-look. Two developments in the same keynote make the turn plain, and they are two halves of the same bill.

**The reality — the vendor just built the scorecard, and named the metric.** At **Microsoft Ignite 2026 (17 November)**, Nadella unveiled **Agent 365 Business Analytics** — "your **ERP system for AI** … **connect spend to business value** and continuously **optimize their return on intelligence**" — with **Agent 365 Cost Management** (preview) to show "exactly what's driving the spend," and said **more than 50 million agents** already run on Agent 365 (Satya Nadella, X; Redmond Channel Partner; Windows Forum, as reported). The phrase to circle is **"return on intelligence"**: the frontier labs have stopped selling capability and started selling *the measurement of whether capability paid off*. In the same breath Microsoft pushed the cost side further — **seven MAI models** (MAI Code 2, Cyber 2 Flash, Image 2.7, Realtime 1, Decision 1.1, Transcribe 2, and routing), with **MAI Code 2 at a reported ~98% of GPT-6.1 Sol at ~40% of the price** and **MAI Image 2.7 topping image-editing at ~1/5 the cost**, plus **GPT-5.6 generally available in Foundry** and **MAI Licensed Weights** via the Frontier program (Microsoft; Azure; TestingCatalog, as reported). Cheaper intelligence, more of it, and — at last — a meter.

**The collision — the scorecard lands on a house that has not been earning.** The reckoning is uncomfortable because the numbers are known and bad. **56% of CEOs reported zero measurable ROI from AI** (PwC, Jan 2026, as reported); **only 23% of organizations see significant ROI from AI agents and 29% from generative AI, with 79% facing challenges scaling** (WRITER survey of 2,400 leaders); **95% of enterprise AI pilots produce no measurable P&L impact** (MIT/Domino, as reported); McKinsey still finds **only ~6%** of firms are AI "high performers" attributing **>5% of EBIT** to AI, and **only ~39% attribute any EBIT gain**; and most tellingly, BCG reports **90% of CEOs expected agents to deliver measurable ROI in 2026 — while 94% say they will keep investing even if the investment does not pay off** (as reported). A house that will keep spending regardless is exactly the house that needs its own books, because a vendor's scorecard graded by the vendor you pay will never recommend the one move that threatens its revenue: spend less.

**The stakes — own the reckoning, or be reckoned for.** The board move re-uses the whole year's discipline and points it at the account. **First, grade value, not adoption** — "50 million agents" and "weekly active users" are the hall; "return on intelligence" (value created per dollar of spend, per workflow) is the barn, and the two-harvests lesson sharpens from *did it reap* to *did it reap more than it cost*. **Second, keep your own meter** — instrument spend-to-outcome in a layer you control (your gateway, your evals, your P&L tags), not only inside the vendor's analytics, so the scorecard can tell you to switch models or switch vendors. **Third, kill what does not earn its keep** — the courage the 94% lack; a reckoning that cannot end a project is a dashboard, not a reckoning. Do it **now**, while the vendors are racing to become the system of record for your own ROI — because whoever keeps the books defines the question, and the question "return on *our* intelligence" should not be asked, and answered, by the company selling you the intelligence.

1. **The reality — Microsoft shipped the ledger and named the metric: "return on intelligence."** **Agent 365 Business Analytics ("your ERP system for AI… connect spend to business value") + Cost Management (preview); >50M agents on Agent 365; seven MAI models (MAI Code 2 ~98% of GPT-6.1 Sol at ~40% price); GPT-5.6 GA in Foundry (Microsoft Ignite, 17 Nov, as reported).** The vendor now sells the scorecard, not just the capability.
2. **The collision — the scorecard lands on a house that is not earning.** **56% of CEOs report zero measurable ROI (PwC); only 23% see significant ROI from agents, 79% struggle to scale (WRITER); 95% of pilots no measurable P&L (MIT/Domino); ~6% high performers (McKinsey); 94% will keep investing even if it doesn't pay off (BCG), as reported.** The gap between spend and value is now measured — and wide.
3. **The answer — own the reckoning: grade value not adoption, keep your own meter, kill what doesn't earn.** Instrument spend-to-outcome in a layer you control, measure return-on-intelligence per workflow, and end the projects that don't clear the bar — because a scorecard the vendor keeps will never tell you to spend less with that vendor.

**Bottom line:** the livery named it, the steward booked it, the surety bonded it, the second wage checked its loyalty, the two harvests sent it to reap, the purse funded it, the foundation kept the ground yours — all of it about what the servant *is* and *does*. **The reckoning is about what it *earned*.** This week Microsoft made the question unavoidable by shipping the books: an "ERP for AI" that connects spend to value and a cost meter for every agent, on a platform it says already carries 50 million of them, while making the agents themselves cheaper still. That is genuinely useful — you cannot manage what you cannot measure, and most houses have been flying blind (56% zero ROI, 95% of pilots flat, only 6% high performers). But a reckoning kept by the vendor you pay is a conflicted reckoning: it will always find more intelligence to sell and never less to buy. The estates that keep their standing will do three unglamorous things: **grade value, not adoption; keep their own meter on spend-to-outcome; and actually kill what does not earn its keep.** Measure ruthlessly — the vendor is right that you must. But keep the books yourself, because the one answer that threatens the stove-seller is "we need less stove," and that is the one answer his ledger will never print.

## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Reckoning"

*Topic: This morning the frame turns from governing the servant to settling its account. At Microsoft Ignite 2026 (17 November 2026) Satya Nadella unveiled Agent 365 Business Analytics — "your ERP system for AI, helping organizations connect spend to business value and continuously optimize their return on intelligence" — with Agent 365 Cost Management (preview) and a claim that more than 50 million agents already run on the platform, while launching seven cheaper "MAI" models (MAI Code 2 at a reported ~98% of GPT-6.1 Sol for ~40% of the price) and calling Copilot "the new OS for work" (Microsoft; Nadella on X; TestingCatalog, as reported). The surveys set the stakes: 56% of CEOs report zero measurable ROI (PwC), only 23% see significant ROI from agents (WRITER), 95% of pilots show no measurable P&L (MIT/Domino), and 94% say they'll keep investing even if it doesn't pay off (BCG), as reported. The reckoning allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There was a great house that had, over a long and disciplined year, done everything right. It had named every servant and cut each a scoped key; it had hired a steward to keep one honest book; it had put the master's own name on the bond; it had learned to ask whose second coin each advising servant took; it had sent the governed servants out to reap; it had handed each a scoped purse with a drawstring the master could reach; and it had refused, at last, to let the great trading company pour the foundation the whole house stood on. By any fair reckoning it was the best-run house in the valley — and the master had, by now, a *great many* servants. Fifty thousand, the steward said, though no one had counted them lately; they were cheap to hire and cheaper every season, and the master had hired them the way a man eats salted nuts.

Now, the master was proud of the number. "Fifty thousand hands!" he would say at dinner, and his neighbours would nod, and some would go home and hire more of their own. But one grey morning the steward came to him — not with a new servant, and not with a complaint about a servant, but with something the master had somehow never asked for in all that careful year: **the account.** A single page. On the left, what the house had spent on its servants — their wages, their fire, their keep. On the right, what the servants had actually brought in. And a gap between the two columns so wide the master had to put the page down.

For here was the thing no control had touched. The livery had told him *who* each servant was; the steward, *that* there was one book; the surety, *whose name* answered; the second wage, *whose bread* each ate; the two harvests, *whether* they reaped; the purse, *what* they could spend; the foundation, *whose ground* they stood on. **Not one of them had told him whether the whole costly household was worth its keep.** And when the master finally looked, the valley's own tallies looked back: in more than half the great houses the masters could not name a single coin the servants had earned; in nineteen houses in twenty the grand experiments had moved nothing on the ledger at all; and only six houses in a hundred could point to real profit and say *that — that came from the servants.* The master had been counting hands. He had never once weighed the harvest against the wage.

And here is the sharp part, the part that should keep a master awake. The very same great trading company that sold him the cheap servants — and had just sold him a hundred more, cheaper than ever — arrived that same grey morning with a gift so thoughtful it was almost tender: **a counting-house.** "Do not trouble yourself with the sums," the company said; "we have built you a beautiful ledger — an *accounting* for your servants, that connects every coin you spend with us to the value your house receives, and tells you, each morning, your **return on cleverness.** Let us keep your books, and you need only read the happy total." And the master, exhausted from a year of governing and delighted to be handed the one page he most dreaded drawing himself, very nearly said yes, and yes again.

Two roads, as always. The easy road was to take the company's counting-house and read its cheerful total each morning — for the ledger was elegant, and it was free, and it did show the spend, and surely a merchant would not lie. The wiser road, which the careful houses took, began with a cold question asked not of the servants but of the *scorekeeper*: **"Whose hand holds this ledger — and is there any sum it will never write?"** And when they thought it through, they saw the trap in the gift. A counting-house kept by the man you buy from will tally your spending honestly and your value generously, and it will find, every single morning, one more clever servant worth hiring — because its purpose is not to tell you *the servants are not worth their keep*; its purpose is to make the spending feel accountable while it grows. **The one entry such a ledger will never print is "buy less from us."** It is the butcher offering to audit your diet.

So the wise houses learned three plain things, and wrote them on the wall of their own counting-house, which they resolved to keep themselves. **The first: weigh the harvest, not the hands.** "Fifty thousand servants" is a boast, not a number; the only figure that matters is value brought in set against coin paid out, workflow by workflow — the barn weighed against the wage. **The second: keep your own meter.** Instrument your own spending and your own earnings, in a book you control, so that the ledger can say the dangerous thing — *switch this supplier, kill that project, spend less here* — which a merchant's ledger never will. **And the third, the hardest: end what does not earn.** A reckoning that cannot close a ledger is not a reckoning; it is a decoration. The ninety-four houses in a hundred that vowed to keep spending whether or not the servants paid were not brave — they had simply decided, in advance, never to read the account they were about to be handed.

**The moral, stated plainly:** the model is a rented stove, now so cheap a bigger one draws a shrug; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the keyring is which key each holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment one slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap and weigh what it brings in; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; the foundation is why you keep the ground yours — and **the reckoning is why, having governed the servant from name to wage to ground, you must now draw the one page you most dread: the spend set honestly against the value, kept in a book you hold, with the courage to end what does not earn — because the very company that sells you the cheap, clever servants will gladly keep your accounts for you, and its beautiful ledger will show you everything except the single sum that would cost it a sale.** A house that lets the merchant keep its books has not become accountable; it has outsourced its conscience to the one party that profits when the conscience stays quiet.

**The question it forces:** *We can count our agents — the vendor just counted fifty million of them for us. But can we weigh them? Do we know our return on intelligence — real value set against real spend, workflow by workflow — in a book we keep ourselves, with the nerve to kill what does not clear the bar? Or have we let the company that sells us the cleverness also keep the ledger that says whether the cleverness was worth it — so that we will go on hiring hands we have never once weighed, among the ninety-four houses in a hundred that resolved to keep spending before they ever dared to read the account?*

## 3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

### 🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
- **Do we know our return on intelligence — or only our agent count?** Microsoft just shipped "an ERP for AI" that promises to "connect spend to business value," and says 50M agents already run on it; meanwhile 56% of CEOs report zero measurable ROI and 95% of pilots show no P&L impact (PwC; MIT/Domino, as reported). **Can we put one number in front of the board — value created per dollar of AI spend, by workflow — or have we been reporting adoption (seats, agents, usage) because it is the number that is easy and flattering?**
- **Who keeps the scorecard that grades our AI, and what will it never tell us?** The vendor selling the agents is now offering to keep the books on whether the agents are worth it. **Is our spend-to-outcome measurement in a layer we control — so it can recommend "switch vendor" or "spend less" — or does it live inside the supplier's analytics, where that recommendation will never appear?**
- **Can we actually kill a project that does not earn — and have we, this year?** BCG reports 94% will keep investing even if AI does not pay off. **Name the AI initiatives we ended in the last two quarters for failing to clear an ROI bar. If the list is empty, we do not have a reckoning; we have a dashboard.**

### 🏦 Financial Services
- You live by return-on-capital discipline and model risk management; AI spend has escaped both. **Can we produce an AI P&L — spend, attributable value, and net, per use case — to the same standard we would demand of any other capital allocation, and is "return on intelligence" owned by Finance rather than by the AI vendor's dashboard?**
- Cost concentration and third-party scorekeeping are supervised risks. **If our primary AI vendor both supplies the agents and measures their value, have we built an independent meter — our own instrumentation of spend-to-outcome — the way we would never let a counterparty mark its own book?**

### 🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
- Value here is clinical and safety outcomes, not tokens. **When the vendor's analytics reports "hours assisted" and "task success," do those map to validated clinical value and real cost avoidance — or are we being shown activity metrics dressed as return, with the hard denominator (total cost of ownership, risk) left out?**
- Budgets are scarce and scrutinized. **Have we weighed each AI-assisted workflow's measured benefit against its full cost — including review, validation and failure modes — in our own ledger, so we can defend or end it, rather than citing a vendor's "return on intelligence" number we cannot reproduce?**

### 🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
- You run capital projects on hurdle rates and post-implementation reviews. **Have we applied the same stage-gate discipline to AI agents — a measured return threshold and a scheduled review that can actually cancel — or did AI skip the capital-allocation rigor every other investment faces because the agents were "cheap"?**
- Cheap inputs still need yield accounting. **Microsoft's seven new MAI models make intelligence cheaper again (MAI Code 2 at ~40% of a frontier model's price); are we capturing that as lower cost per useful outcome in our own books, or just running more agents at the same budget with no better return?**

### 🛒 Retail / Consumer
- Thin margins punish unmeasured spend. **Do we have unit economics for every AI-touched workflow — cost per resolved ticket, per converted session, per fulfilled order — in a meter we own, so we can route to the cheapest model that clears the bar and cut the ones that don't?**
- Peak season rewards ruthless prioritization. **Can we rank our AI initiatives by measured return-on-intelligence today and defund the bottom quartile before the next peak — or are we carrying a long tail of agents no one has ever weighed?**

### 🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
- Public money demands demonstrable value and auditability. **Can we show taxpayers and auditors the measured outcome of AI spend — service improvement per pound/dollar — from our own records, independent of any vendor's "return on intelligence" dashboard, under the accountability duties we already owe?**
- Continuity and independence matter most here. **Is our spend-and-value measurement held in a form we own and can audit, so a change of supplier or of the supplier's analytics does not blind us to whether public services are actually being improved by the money spent?**

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Foundation to the Reckoning

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking *is this agent named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and standing on ground I own?* and started asking the one question none of those controls answer: *across the whole costly household, what is the value created set against the money spent — and who gets to keep that scorecard?* The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the keyring is which key it holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds it; the bell discloses when it slips; the livery scopes its identity; the steward keeps one office; the surety bonds it; the second wage checks its loyalty; the two harvests sends it to reap; the purse funds it; the foundation keeps the ground yours. **Every one of those governs what the agent *is*, *does*, *serves* or *stands on*. The reckoning is about what it *earned* — and this week the market made the distinction impossible to dodge: Microsoft shipped the ledger (Agent 365 Business Analytics, "connect spend to business value," + Cost Management) in the same keynote it made the agents cheaper (seven MAI models) and bigger in number (50M on the platform).** The point is blunt: **the thing that is easy to measure (adoption — agents, seats, tokens) is not the thing that matters (return — value per dollar), and confusing the two is how a house runs 50 million agents, passes every governance review, and still cannot name a coin they earned.**

- **The reality — the vendor now sells the scorecard, and named the KPI "return on intelligence."** **At Ignite 2026 (17 Nov) Microsoft unveiled Agent 365 Business Analytics — "your ERP system for AI… connect spend to business value and continuously optimize their return on intelligence" (Nadella) — with Agent 365 Cost Management (preview) to surface "exactly what's driving the spend," >50M agents claimed on Agent 365, seven MAI models (MAI Code 2 ~98% of GPT-6.1 Sol at ~40% price; MAI Image 2.7 image-editing lead at ~1/5 cost), GPT-5.6 GA in Foundry, Copilot as "the new OS for work" (Microsoft; Nadella on X; TestingCatalog; DEV Community, as reported).** Architecturally, three things follow. First, **measurement is the new lock-in surface.** The system that holds your spend-to-value scorecard holds the decision about what to buy next; whoever keeps the ledger frames the question — and the vendor's ledger frames it as "return on *their* intelligence." Second, **adoption metrics are an anti-pattern.** "50 million agents," weekly active users and token volume measure activity, not return; the two-harvests lesson (measure execution, not adoption) must now go one level deeper — measure *value per unit cost*, not execution volume, because cheaper models (MAI Code 2 at 40% price) only help if the saving reaches your P&L instead of funding more unmeasured agents. Third, **the denominator must be yours.** Return-on-intelligence is value ÷ total cost of ownership; a vendor's dashboard controls both terms unless you instrument spend-to-outcome in a layer you own (your gateway, your evals, your P&L tags).
- **The collision — the scorecard lands on an unearning house, and most houses have pre-committed to ignore it.** **56% of CEOs report zero measurable ROI (PwC, Jan 2026); only 23% see significant ROI from AI agents and 29% from genAI, with 79% struggling to scale (WRITER, 2,400 leaders); 95% of enterprise AI pilots show no measurable P&L (MIT/Domino); ~6% are AI high performers (>5% EBIT) and ~39% attribute any EBIT gain (McKinsey); 90% of CEOs expected agent ROI in 2026 while 94% will keep investing even if it doesn't pay off (BCG), as reported.** Two engineering realities cut against comfort. **First, you cannot optimize what you do not meter — and you must not let the metered party set the meter.** The reason 95% of pilots show no P&L is not always that the AI failed; it is that no one instrumented value-to-cost at the workflow level, so the win (or loss) was never computed. **Second, a reckoning without a kill switch is theatre.** The 94%-will-keep-investing number is the tell: measurement only creates value if it can end a project; a scorecard wired to a budget that cannot be cut is a very expensive feelings chart.
- **The answer — own the reckoning: grade value not adoption, keep your own meter, kill what doesn't earn.** **Instrument spend-to-outcome per workflow in a layer you control (gateway-level cost tags, outcome evals, a Finance-owned AI P&L); define return-on-intelligence as measured value ÷ fully-loaded cost and report it instead of seat/agent counts; and run a quarterly reckoning with the authority to defund — reusing the logging, evaluation and ownership disciplines you already built for the steward, the two harvests and the foundation, now pointed at the account.** This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — *be able to account for, and act on, what you run* — now aimed at the sharpest axis of all: not what the agent is, serves, harvests, spends or stands on, but whether, across the whole household, the cleverness was worth the coin — measured in a book you keep, not the one the seller hands you.

The strategic core: **Microsoft is right that you cannot manage what you cannot measure — the reckoning is overdue, and most houses have been flying blind — but the vendor keeping the scorecard is a conflict you must not accept; own the meter, or the meter owns the question.** The market makes the temptation concrete: drawing your own AI P&L is tedious, and a free, elegant, vendor-built "ERP for AI" that connects spend to value is a gift you are tired enough to accept. The board translation: **measure ruthlessly — the vendor is right about that — but keep the books yourself, because the only answer that threatens the seller is "buy less," and that is the one his ledger will never print.** The failure mode is exact: **a house that names, governs, funds, perfects and grounds its agents — and then lets the company it buys from keep the ledger of whether they were worth it — will run 50 million agents, read a cheerful "return on intelligence" total every morning, and still be one of the 94% that resolved to keep spending before it ever dared to weigh the harvest against the wage.**

### Why "the reckoning" is the discipline that matters now

For a year the worry was whether the servant was named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and standing on its own ground. **This morning the market moved the question to the account: Microsoft shipped the ledger and named the KPI, in the same keynote it made the agents cheaper and counted fifty million of them.** Agent 365 Business Analytics and "return on intelligence" prove the vendors now compete to be your system of record for ROI; the survey wall (56% zero ROI, 95% of pilots flat, ~6% high performers, 94% will spend anyway) proves most houses need exactly that and will misuse it. Governance that stopped at the servant will not protect the account. The estates that keep their standing will take the vendor's point — measure — and refuse the vendor's role — scorekeeper — grading value not adoption, on a meter they own, with the nerve to end what does not earn.

### How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — **be able to account for, and act on, what you run** — now sharpened to *be able to weigh what you run against what it costs, in your own book.* On a legacy estate the instinct, after a year of governance work and a dozen proud "agents deployed" slides, is to accept the vendor's free, elegant ERP-for-AI and read its cheerful total. ## 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. **Grade value, not adoption — report return-on-intelligence, retire the agent count.** Microsoft just handed the market a metric ("return on intelligence") and a ledger that counts 50M agents; adoption numbers are the hall, value-per-dollar is the barn (56% of CEOs see zero ROI; 95% of pilots show no P&L). **This month, pick your top five AI-touched workflows and compute, for each, measured value created ÷ fully-loaded cost (model + review + integration + failure), and put that single ratio in front of the board in place of seats, agents or token counts.** Report one number: the share of AI spend for which you can state a measured return-on-intelligence.
