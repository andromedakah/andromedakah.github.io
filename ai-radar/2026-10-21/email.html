The New Hire

Wednesday, 21 October 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

The vendors just changed what the agent is: Google's Gemini agent gets its own Workspace account — email, calendar, Drive, a seat in your directory — and onboards the agent as a coworker. A colleague is a heavier obligation than a tool. @mention the new hire all you like — but could you fire it by lunch? Onboard like a hire, keep the roll readable, make dismissal a drill.

1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from governing the agent as a tool to onboarding it as a coworker — because the vendors just gave the agent its own identity, and a colleague carries obligations a tool never did.

1. The reality — the agent gets its own identity, as a coworker. On 8 October 2026, at "Gemini at Work 2026," Google Cloud unveiled the Gemini agent — its "coworker agent" receives its own Workspace account (email, calendar, Drive, "presence in your company directory"), is @mentioned in Chat and Docs, has edits "appear under its own name," and "sees only what is shared with it," over a swappable model layer ("Gemini family… and Claude models from Anthropic today," OpenAI/open-weights next) (Google Cloud; MarkTechPost; Futurum, as reported). This is not the livery: it gives the hand its own key, inbox and nameplate.

2. The collision — a colleague needs a sponsor, a scoped role, and a way to be fired. Coworker is now the category — OpenAI's Frontier (Feb 2026) and Anthropic's Claude Frontier Academy (2 Oct, $100M to train 10,000 "Frontier Deployed Engineers" by end-2027) say so. Yet non-human identities outnumber humans by up to 45:1 (80:1 on other counts); only 10% say their systems can manage them; 41% say no single team owns AI security; ~22% tie identities to agents; just 32% govern agents like humans; and 88% had an agent incident (Okta; CyberArk, as reported). A coworker you cannot find, trace, or fire is an unlocked door wearing a nameplate.

3. The answer — onboard like a hire, keep the roll readable, make dismissal a drill. Give every agent its own identity (never a borrowed or shared login) tied to a named sponsor and a scoped role; keep a live, queryable staff roll; and test a fast deprovisioning path you have actually run.

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each governed the agent as a tool. The new hire is the morning the vendors made it a colleague — with its own email, calendar, Drive and seat in the directory. A colleague is a heavier obligation: a sponsor who vouches, a role that bounds, a manager who can let it go, and a name you can find on the roll. @mention the new hire all you like — but first make sure you could fire it by lunch.

2 · Allegory — The New Hire

A great house had spent a careful year governing its servants as tools — named, booked, bonded, waged, sent to reap, funded, and stood on ground it checked was its own. Then this month the trading company stopped delivering tools and started delivering workers — each arriving with its own key to the front door, its own pigeon-hole with its own name painted on, its own seat at the table and its own line in the staff register. "Treat it as a colleague," the company said. "Give it a seat." And the master, so good at governing tools, felt the ground tilt: a tool I put in a drawer; but this has a key, an inbox, and a name on the roll — this is a hire.

A hire is a different obligation than a tool. You vouch for it (someone signs beside it and answers for it), you bound it (a written role — this room, not that one), and you keep the power to let it go (take back the key, empty the pigeon-hole, strike the name — the same afternoon, if it comes to that). So the master went to the register — and it was a shambles. For every human name there were forty-five others he did not recognize; scarcely one in five could be traced to a sponsor; no single office did the hiring; barely a third were held to anything like proper onboarding; and very nearly all had already been near some mishap or breach. A tool forgotten in a drawer is inert; a worker forgotten on the roll still has a key.

So the wise houses stopped letting workers in and started onboarding them: every worker got its own true name (never a borrowed or shared key), a human sponsor who answered for it, a written role that bounded it, and — written down before it lifted a finger — the terms of its dismissal, drilled at least once. A house that cannot say who vouched for a worker, what it may touch, and how it would be let go does not have colleagues. It has strangers with keys.

The question it forces: The vendor just handed our newest worker its own email, calendar and seat in our directory, and told us to treat it as a colleague. Did we hire it, or merely let it in? For every agent with a key, can we name the human who vouches for it, the role that bounds it, and the drill by which we would dismiss it this afternoon — or are we among the houses where non-human workers outnumber people 45 to 1, only one in five traces to a sponsor, no office does the hiring, and no one has ever practiced saying "you're let go"?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the New Hire

Every prior control governs the agent as a tool; the new hire is the morning it becomes a colleague with an identity of its own — and a colleague carries obligations a tool never did. The reality: agent identity is now a first-class object (credentials, mailbox, directory entry, audit trail of its own), the model is explicitly decoupled and swappable (log which model acted), and "sees only what is shared with it" is a starting grant, not a standing guarantee — so the governing object over time is the identity's entitlement. The collision: provisioning outran governance (easy to stand up an agent, hard to onboard it) and deprovisioning is the untested control (everyone can "switch it off"; almost no one has drilled it) — 45:1 non-human identities, only 10% can manage them, 41% no single team owns AI security, ~22% tie identities to agents, 32% govern like humans, 88% had an incident. The answer: onboard like a hire (own identity, named sponsor, scoped role, lifecycle), keep the staff roll readable (a live, queryable register), and make dismissal a drill (a fast, tested revocation path).

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Onboard the agent like a hire. Vendors now ship agents with their own accounts, and a colleague is a heavier obligation than a tool. Write and enforce an agent-onboarding standard: every agent gets its own identity (never a borrowed human login or a shared service account), bound to a named human sponsor, a written scoped role, and a lifecycle — joiner-mover-leaver. Report the share of production agents with a named sponsor and a scoped role on record.

2. Keep the staff roll readable. Non-human identities outnumber humans by up to 45:1, yet only ~22% tie identities to agents and 41% say no single team owns AI security. Stand up (or designate the owner of) a single authoritative register of every agent identity — its sponsor, scope, model(s) and last activity — and name the one team that keeps it. Report the share of known agent identities on the register with an owner and a scope, and the estimated share still uncounted.

3. Make dismissal a drill. Only ~32% govern agents like human employees, and "we can switch it off" is almost never tested, while 88% have had an incident. Pick one production agent and run the drill: revoke its identity, mailbox and access under time pressure and confirm its work stopped; measure the time-to-dismiss and fix what made it slow. Report the measured time-to-dismiss and a dated plan to bring every high-privilege agent under a tested revocation path.
