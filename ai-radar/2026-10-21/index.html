The New Hire

A year of work went into governing the agent as a tool — named, scoped, logged, funded. This month the vendors change what it is: Google's Gemini agent gets its own Workspace account — email, calendar, Drive, a seat in your directory — and onboards the agent as a coworker. A colleague is a heavier obligation than a tool. @mention the new hire all you like — but could you fire it by lunch?
Wednesday, 21 October 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering

On 8 October 2026, at "Gemini at Work 2026," Google Cloud unveiled the Gemini agent — and its "coworker agent" is given its own Workspace account: its own email address, calendar, Drive, and "presence in your company directory," which colleagues @mention in Chat and Docs, whose edits "appear under its own name in version history," and which "sees only what is shared with it" (Google Cloud; MarkTechPost; AI Weekly — "Gets a Mailbox and Directory Seat," as reported). The model underneath is now a swappable layer — Google routes each task "across our Gemini family of models and Claude models from Anthropic today," with OpenAI and open-weights to follow — so the agent, not the model, is the product. This is not the livery: the livery put your uniform on your servant; this gives the hand its own key, its own inbox, and its own name on the staff roll. The board question is no longer is the tool named, scoped and funded? It is: when a worker in our house is not a person, who hired it, who vouches for it, who can dismiss it — and can we even find all of them on the roll? The honest answer at most houses is no: non-human identities outnumber humans by as much as 45:1, only 10% of firms say their systems can manage them, 41% say no single team owns AI security, ~22% tie identities to agents, and just 32% govern agents like humans (Okta, Businesses at Work 2026; CyberArk, as reported). We have begun handing out keys, inboxes and nameplates to coworkers we cannot count.

1 · Executive Summary 90-second read

For a long year the radar governed the servant as a tool — named, booked, bonded, waged, sent to reap, funded with a scoped purse, and stood on ground you checked was yours. This morning the vendors change what the agent is: they onboard it as a coworker. The axis turns from how do we govern the tool? to how do we hire, vouch for, and dismiss a colleague who is not a person?

The reality — the agent is being given its own identity, as a coworker, not a tool. On 8 October 2026, at "Gemini at Work 2026," Google Cloud unveiled the Gemini agent, whose "coworker agent" receives its own Workspace account — its own email address, calendar, Drive, and "presence in your company directory" — which colleagues @mention in Chat and Docs, whose edits "appear under its own name in version history," and which "sees only what is shared with it" (Google Cloud; MarkTechPost; AI Weekly, as reported). The underlying model is now an explicitly swappable layer — Google routes each task "across our Gemini family of models and Claude models from Anthropic today," with OpenAI and open-weights promised next (Futurum — "the agent ahead of the model"; Creative Strategies — "bets on governance and model choice," as reported). This is not the livery: the livery put your uniform on your servant so you could tell your hands apart; this gives the hand its own key, its own inbox, its own nameplate on the staff roll. And the framing is now industry-wide — OpenAI's Frontier (Feb 2026) treats agents as "AI coworkers" onboarded and governed like employees, and Anthropic's Claude Frontier Academy (2 Oct 2026) commits $100M to train 10,000 "Frontier Deployed Engineers" by the end of 2027 (OpenAI; Anthropic, as reported).

The collision — a coworker brings obligations a tool never did, and most houses have skipped them. When a worker is a tool, you need only scope and log it. When a worker is a colleague — with its own credentials, its own inbox, its own seat — you need the things that make a colleague accountable: a named human who vouches for it, a role that bounds what it may touch, and a way to dismiss it that actually works. Most houses have handed out the keys and skipped the hiring. Non-human identities now outnumber humans by as much as 45 to 1 and, on other counts, more than 80 to 1; only 10% of organizations say their identity systems are fully equipped to manage them; 41% say no single team is responsible for AI security; only about 22% have identities tied to their agents; and just 32% secure agents with the rigor they give a human hire — while 88% reported a confirmed or suspected agent-related security incident in the past year (Okta, Businesses at Work 2026; CyberArk; as reported). A coworker you cannot find on the roll, cannot trace to a sponsor, and cannot fire is not a colleague. It is an unlocked door wearing a nameplate.

The stakes — onboard the agent like a hire, keep the roll readable, make dismissal a drill. The board move re-uses the whole year's discipline and points it at the HR question the vendors just forced. First, onboard it like a hire, not a tool — every agent gets its own identity (never a borrowed human login or a shared service account), tied to a named human sponsor who is accountable for it, a scoped role that bounds what it may touch, and a lifecycle from provisioning to retirement. Second, keep the staff roll readable — a live inventory you can actually query: who sponsored each agent, what it can reach, when it last acted; the 45:1 ratio means the census is now the hard part. Third, make dismissal a drill, not a theory — a tested, fast deprovisioning path, so you can revoke an agent's key, inbox and nameplate in minutes, and you have practiced it. Do it now, because the vendors are shipping the coworker-with-its-own-account this quarter, and the keys are being cut faster than the roll is being kept.

2 · Allegory of the Day

🖋️ "The New Hire"

Topic: This morning the vendors change what the agent is — from a tool in the house to a coworker on the staff. On 8 October 2026, at "Gemini at Work 2026," Google Cloud unveiled the Gemini agent, whose "coworker agent" receives its own Workspace account — its own email, calendar, Drive and "presence in your company directory," @mentionable in Chat and Docs, with edits "under its own name" — over a swappable model layer (Gemini + Claude today, OpenAI/open-weights next) (Google Cloud; MarkTechPost; Futurum, as reported). OpenAI's Frontier and Anthropic's Claude Frontier Academy ($100M, 10,000 "Frontier Deployed Engineers" by end-2027) make the "AI coworker" framing industry-wide. Yet non-human identities outnumber humans by up to 45:1, only 10% of firms say their systems can manage them, 41% say no single team owns AI security, ~22% tie identities to agents, 32% govern agents like humans, and 88% had an agent incident (Okta; CyberArk, as reported). The new-hire allegory is the radar's own illustration.

There was a great house that had spent a long and careful year learning to govern its servants. It had named every hand and cut it a scoped key, kept one honest book, put the master's name on the bond, asked whose second coin each advising servant took, sent the governed servants to reap, handed each a scoped purse, and looked hard at the ground the whole estate stood on. By any fair reckoning it governed its tools better than any house in the valley — and the master was proud, as he should have been.

Then, this month, the great trading company that supplied the house did a quiet and clever thing. It stopped delivering tools altogether. Where last season it had sent a clever machine the house plugged into its own wall, this season it sent a worker — and the worker arrived with its own things. Its own key to the front door. Its own pigeon-hole in the mail-room, with its own name painted on. Its own place at the long table, its own line in the house's own staff register, so that when a maid wanted a thing done she no longer fetched a tool from the cupboard — she called the worker by name down the hall, and the worker answered, and did the thing, and signed the work in its own hand in the house ledger. "Treat it as a colleague," the company said warmly. "Give it a seat. It will feel like one of your own."

And the master, who had grown so good at governing tools, felt the ground tilt under a question he had never had to ask of a hammer. A tool I switch off and put in a drawer. But this is not in a drawer. This has a key, and an inbox, and a name on the roll. This is a hire. And a hire, he knew — for he had hired people all his life — is a wholly different kind of obligation than a tool. You do not merely scope a person. You vouch for them: someone signs their name beside the new hand's and says, this one is mine, I answer for them. You bound them: a role, a title, a description of what they may and may not touch. And — the part no one likes to plan on the first day — you keep the power to let them go: a clear way to take back the key, empty the pigeon-hole, and strike the name from the roll, the same afternoon, if it comes to that. None of these is a thing you ever did to a tool. All of them are things you must do for a colleague.

So the master walked down to the staff register to see how his new workers had been hired — and here the story turns cold. The register was a shambles. For every human name on the roll there were forty-five others he did not recognize — some said eighty — workers that had been let in the front door over the past year, each with a key and a pigeon-hole, none of them ever properly hired. He asked the steward, "Who vouched for these? Who is their master of record?" and the steward could answer for scarcely one in five. He asked, "Which office does their hiring — who onboards them, who can dismiss them?" and found that no single office owned the task; it had fallen between the butler and the clerk of works and the man who kept the locks, and so had fallen to no one. He asked, "Do we hire these the way we hire a footman — a sponsor, a role, a probation, a way to let them go?" and the honest answer was that barely a third were held to anything like it. And when he asked the plainest question of all — "Has any harm come of this?" — the steward went grey, because very nearly all of them, by now, had been near some mishap or breach, a worker wandering into a room it had no business in, a key that opened a door no one meant it to.

Two roads, as always. The easy road was to keep saying welcome aboard — the workers were useful, they did fine work, and @calling them by name down the hall was a marvel. The wiser road, which the careful houses took, began by refusing the company's warm word. A "colleague" is not a gift you unwrap; it is a hire you are now responsible for. So the careful houses stopped letting workers in the door and started onboarding them. Every worker got its own true name — never a borrowed footman's key, never a shared pass the whole kitchen used, but a name of its own, so the ledger always showed whose hand did the work. Every worker got a human sponsor — a real person who signed beside it and answered for it, as a captain answers for the men he musters. Every worker got a written role — this room, not that one; these errands, not those — so a seat at the table never quietly became a run of the whole house. And every worker got, written down before it ever lifted a finger, the terms of its dismissal — who could let it go, and how fast, and a drill run at least once so the house knew it could take back the key, empty the pigeon-hole and strike the name by supper, not merely believe it could.

Here is the part that should keep a master awake. The thing that made the new workers so pleasant — that they came with their own key, their own inbox, their own name on the roll, so you need not trouble yourself with hiring them — was exactly the thing that made them dangerous. A tool forgotten in a drawer is inert. A worker forgotten on the roll still has a key. The company had not done anything wicked; it had simply made it so easy to take on staff that the house took on a city's worth of them without once doing the small, dull, human work of hiring — the vouching, the bounding, the planning for the parting. And a house that cannot say who vouched for a worker, what it is allowed to touch, and how it would be let go, does not have colleagues. It has strangers with keys.

The moral, stated plainly: the model is a rented stove, now so cheap a bigger one draws a shrug; your knowledge is the larder; the foundation is the ground you must own; the gate is the one door; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks for the roll; the keyring is which key each holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment one slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you put your uniform on your servant; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising servant eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee and not an heir — and the new hire is why, the morning the vendor stops selling you a tool and hands you a colleague with its own key, its own inbox and its own name on the staff roll, you must stop installing and start hiring: give every worker its own true name, a human who vouches for it, a role that bounds it, and a dismissal you have drilled — so that a house which governs its tools superbly does not wake to find it has let in a workforce forty-five times its own number that it never hired, cannot trace to a sponsor, and has never once practiced letting go. A house that gives a worker a seat at the table without first hiring it has not gained a colleague; it has handed a stranger a key, a nameplate and a chair, and called the arrangement a team.

The question it forces: The vendor just handed our newest worker its own email, its own calendar, its own seat in our directory — and told us to treat it as a colleague. So: did we hire it, or merely let it in? For every agent with a key in our house, can we name the human who vouches for it, the role that bounds what it may touch, and the drill by which we would dismiss it this afternoon — or are we among the houses where non-human workers outnumber people forty-five to one, only one in five can be traced to a sponsor, no single office does the hiring, and nobody has ever practiced saying "you're let go"?

3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
  • Did we hire our agents, or merely let them in? The vendors now ship agents with their own identity — Google's coworker agent gets its own Workspace email, calendar, Drive and directory seat (8 Oct). For every agent with credentials in our house, can we name the human who sponsors it, the written role that bounds what it may touch, and who can dismiss it — or do we only know how to scope a tool, not hire a colleague?
  • Can we even find them all on the roll? Non-human identities outnumber humans by as much as 45:1 (80:1 on other counts), only ~22% of firms have identities tied to their agents, and 41% say no single team owns AI security. Can we produce, today, a live, queryable roster of every agent identity, its sponsor, its permitted scope, and its last activity — and if not, who owns closing that gap by when?
  • Could we fire one by lunch? Only ~32% of firms govern agents with the rigor they give human employees, yet 88% report an agent-related incident. Have we ever run the drill — revoked a live agent's identity, inbox and access under time pressure and confirmed its work stopped — or is "we can turn it off" a belief we have never tested?
🏦 Financial Services
  • Identity and access are supervised controls in your world, and agents are now first-class identities with their own logins. Can we show our regulator a single authoritative register of non-human identities — each with a named accountable owner, a scoped entitlement, and a tested revocation path — or have agents been provisioned outside the identity-governance regime we already owe for humans?
  • Third-party and over-permissioning risk is magnified when an agent has its own account and routes across multiple vendors' models (Gemini + Claude today, more to follow). For each agent, do we enforce least privilege and short-lived credentials, and do we know which model is answering on its behalf for each regulated decision?
🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
  • An agent with its own directory identity that "sees only what is shared with it" still accumulates access over time. For agents touching clinical or patient data, do we have a named human sponsor accountable for each, a scoped role reviewed on a schedule, and automatic deprovisioning when a project ends — the same joiner-mover-leaver discipline we apply to clinicians?
  • Validation and attribution matter here. When an agent's edits "appear under its own name," can we reconstruct who authorized the work, under which model, and roll it back — or does an autonomous coworker create an accountability gap in a regulated workflow?
🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
  • You run rigorous contractor onboarding and badge-control for people on the floor; agents are now badged workers too. Have we applied joiner-mover-leaver (sponsor, scoped access, time-boxed credentials, revocation) to every agent identity — or are agents the one "workforce" we let onto the floor without a badge office?
  • Embedded-vendor programs (OpenAI Frontier's forward-deployed engineers; Anthropic's 10,000 Frontier Deployed Engineers) put vendor-trained people and their agents inside your operations. When they leave, do the agents they stood up remain ours, named and governable — or do we inherit orphaned workers no one in-house can sponsor or dismiss?
🛒 Retail / Consumer
  • Agents with their own accounts now act across chat, email, calendar and documents on your behalf. Do we have a live inventory of every customer- or ops-facing agent, its sponsor and its scope — and can we revoke one instantly if it misbehaves during peak trade, or would "which account is that?" cost us the afternoon?
  • Model choice is now the agent's, not yours (routing across Gemini, Claude, soon others). For agents that touch customer data or spend, do we control and log which model answers — or has the "coworker" been given discretion we never granted a tool?
🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
  • Accountability and traceability are statutory duties. Under the EU AI Act high-risk regime (live since 2 August 2026), can we show that every agent acting in a public service has a named accountable human, a bounded role, and a revocation path — the digital equivalent of a vetted, dismissible public servant?
  • Continuity and public trust make an uncounted workforce a governance failure. Do we maintain an authoritative register of agent identities with owners and scopes, and have we tested dismissing one — or have we onboarded non-human workers into public systems that no single office is responsible for?

4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Foundation to the New Hire

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking is this tool named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded, and standing on ground I own? and started asking the question the vendors just forced: this worker has its own key, its own inbox and its own name on the roll — did we hire it, and could we fire it? Every prior control — gate, passport, census, inspector, keyring, locksmith, tide table, provenance, errand, bell, locusts, looking-glass, livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse, foundation — governs the agent as a tool in your house. The new hire is the morning the agent becomes a colleague with an identity of its own — and a colleague carries obligations a tool never did: someone who vouches for it, a role that bounds it, and a dismissal that works. This month made the shift concrete: Google's Gemini agent (8 Oct) gives a "coworker agent" its own Workspace account — email, calendar, Drive, directory presence, @mentionable, edits under its own name — over a model layer it chooses per task (Gemini + Claude now, OpenAI/open-weights next). The point is blunt: the vendors have promoted the agent from tool to coworker, and most houses are still only equipped to govern tools.

  • The reality — the agent now has its own identity, and the model is a swappable layer beneath it. At "Gemini at Work 2026" (8 Oct 2026) Google Cloud unveiled the Gemini agent; its "coworker agent" receives its own Workspace account — email address, calendar, Drive, and "presence in your company directory" — is @mentioned in Chat and Docs, has edits "appear under its own name in version history," and "sees only what is shared with it," while the system "orchestrates across our Gemini family of models and Claude models from Anthropic today," with OpenAI and open-weights promised next; admins reportedly get hard spend caps that can pause an agent mid-task, and each agent has its own audited identity (Google Cloud; MarkTechPost; Creative Strategies; Futurum, as reported; currently a private preview, no public price or GA date). Three engineering facts follow. First, agent identity is now a first-class object, distinct from both the human user and the model — it has credentials, a mailbox, a directory entry and an audit trail of its own. Second, the model is explicitly decoupled and swappable, so "which model acted" is a per-task question you must log, not assume. Third, "sees only what is shared with it" is a starting grant, not a standing guarantee — access accretes, so the governing object over time is the identity's entitlement, not the model's.
  • The collision — a first-class identity demands joiner-mover-leaver, and the numbers say it is missing. Non-human identities now outnumber humans by as much as 45:1 (CyberArk puts machine identities at more than 80:1); only 10% of organizations say their identity systems are fully equipped to manage and secure non-human identities; 41% say no single team is responsible for AI security; only ~22% have identities tied to their agents; just 32% secure agents with the same rigor as human employees; and 88% reported a confirmed or suspected agent-related security incident in the past year (Okta, Businesses at Work 2026; CyberArk, as reported). Two realities cut against comfort. First, provisioning outran governance. It is now trivial to stand up an agent with its own account and far harder to onboard it properly — so the identity count explodes while the sponsor/role/lifecycle discipline lags; the gap between "it has a key" and "someone hired it" is the whole risk. Second, deprovisioning is the untested control. Everyone assumes they can switch an agent off; almost no one has drilled revoking a live agent's identity, mailbox and access under time pressure and confirming the work actually stopped. Emerging standards point the way — proposals to extend SCIM so agents are provisioned and deprovisioned like employees, and Gartner's 2026 guidance to define agent authority before agents scale — but they are early, not adopted (arXiv 2510.25819; Gartner, as reported).
  • The answer — onboard like a hire, keep the roll readable, make dismissal a drill. Give every agent its own identity (never a borrowed human login or a shared service account) bound to a named human sponsor, a written scoped role, and a full lifecycle; maintain a live, queryable register of every agent identity with its owner, scope and last activity; and build and test a fast deprovisioning path that revokes the identity, mailbox and access in minutes — reusing the livery (its own name), the surety (a human answers for it), the errand (a bounded role), the census (a readable roll) and the keyring (revocable keys), now aimed at the agent-as-colleague. This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — sharpened to the HR question the vendors just created: not what the tool is or does, but who hired this colleague, what it may touch, and whether you could let it go today.

The strategic core: the vendors have reframed the agent from a tool you install to a coworker you onboard — and a coworker is a heavier obligation, not a lighter one. The temptation is that the coworker-with-its-own-account feels like a tidy win: give it an email, @mention it, let it sign its own work, and stop thinking about it. The failure mode is exact: a house that governs its tools superbly — named, scoped, logged, funded — and then lets vendors onboard "coworkers" with their own keys, inboxes and nameplates without a sponsor, a bounded role, or a tested dismissal, will pass every tool-governance review and discover, the first time one of 45-to-1 uncounted workers wanders where it shouldn't, that it hired a stranger it cannot name, cannot trace, and has never practiced firing. The board translation: @mention the new hire all you like — but first make sure you could fire it by lunch, and that its name is on the roll.

Why "the new hire" is the discipline that matters now

For a long year the question was whether the tool was named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and standing on ground you owned. This month the vendors changed the category: they stopped selling tools and started onboarding coworkers. Google's coworker agent with its own email and directory seat, OpenAI's and Anthropic's "AI coworker" framing and their armies of deployed engineers — all promote the agent from a thing you install to a colleague you onboard. Tool-governance that stopped at scope-and-log will not hire a colleague. The houses that keep their standing will treat every agent as a hire: its own true name, a human who vouches for it, a role that bounds it, a roll you can read, and a dismissal you have drilled.

How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now sharpened to be able to name, sponsor, bound and dismiss every non-human worker in the house. On a legacy estate the instinct is to welcome the coworker agent the way you welcomed the clever tool: switch it on, give it an account, move on. Under the new hire that is the exact reflex to check — an account without an onboarding is a stranger with a key. The retrofit is hire, roll, dismiss. Hire: give every agent its own identity, a named sponsor, a written scoped role, and a lifecycle — joiner-mover-leaver, the same discipline you owe a human. Roll: stand up a live, queryable register of every agent identity, its owner, scope and last activity, because at 45:1 the census is the hard part. Dismiss: build and test a fast deprovisioning path, so revoking an agent's key, inbox and name takes minutes and you have proven it. 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

  1. Onboard the agent like a hire, not a tool — its own identity, a named sponsor, a scoped role. The vendors now ship agents with their own accounts (Google's coworker agent gets its own email, calendar, Drive and directory seat), and a colleague is a heavier obligation than a tool. This month, write and enforce an agent-onboarding standard: every agent gets its own identity (never a borrowed human login or a shared service account), bound to a named human sponsor who is accountable for it, a written role that bounds what it may touch, and a defined lifecycle — the same joiner-mover-leaver discipline you owe a human. Report one number to the board: the share of production agents that have a named sponsor and a scoped role on record.
  2. Keep the staff roll readable — a live, queryable register of every agent identity. Non-human identities outnumber humans by as much as 45:1, yet only ~22% of firms tie identities to their agents and 41% say no single team owns AI security — the census is now the hard part. This month, stand up (or designate the owner of) a single authoritative register of every agent identity, recording its sponsor, permitted scope, the model(s) it routes to, and its last activity, and name the one team accountable for keeping it. Report the share of known agent identities that appear on the register with an owner and a scope — and the estimated share still uncounted.
  3. Make dismissal a drill, not a theory — a fast, tested deprovisioning path. Only ~32% of firms govern agents with the rigor they give human employees, and "we can switch it off" is almost never tested — while 88% have already had an agent incident. This month, pick one production agent and run the drill: revoke its identity, mailbox and access under time pressure and confirm its work actually stopped; measure the time-to-dismiss and fix whatever made it slow. Report the measured time-to-dismiss and a dated plan to bring every high-privilege agent under a tested revocation path.
