1 · Executive Summary

The most important development of the week is not a model at all: the release cadence crossed the threshold where a rational buyer can no longer keep up, and the market named the condition — "model fatigue."

1. The event — four labs, one week, buyer fatigue. Anthropic Fable 5.1 + Mythos 5.1 (1 Sep), Meta Muse Spark 1.3 (2 Sep), Google Gemini 3.8 Flash (2 Sep, third Flash in six weeks), OpenAI GPT-6 Astra (3 Sep). CNBC (6 Sep) reports buyers spending outsized hours comparing cost and capability only for the comparison to go stale before they finish, with some capping shortlists at ~5 models instead of 10 (CNBC, Startup Fortune, as reported). The cadence itself is now the enterprise's problem.

2. The cause — a sellers' race, not a signal about you. The tempo is set by a share-of-wallet arms race among labs valued near $1T each and racing to public markets; 1,100+ lab employees have petitioned Washington to help slow it (CNBC, as reported). Chasing the frontier release-by-release is a treadmill that burns your scarcest asset — attention — and converts almost none of it into durable advantage.

3. The answer — build the harbor, not a new pier per ship. The winning move is to treat each model as a swappable input behind a stable interface — a model-agnostic gateway — so adopting the newest or cheapest is a routing decision made in a day, not a re-platforming project. ~37% of orgs already run 5+ models in production (up from ~29%); lock-in costs ~19–34% to switch; and the differentiator is proprietary data and process, not the model (McKinsey: ~80% productivity, ~37% EBIT) (industry analyses, McKinsey, as reported).

Bottom line: the week that shipped four frontier models in seven days is telling you the picking will never stop, and any answer you standardise on will be stale within the fortnight. Do two unglamorous things: build one quay any ship can dock at — a model-agnostic gateway and an evaluation harness — so swapping models is routine; and put your real effort into the larder, not the pier — the proprietary data, redesigned process and evaluations that are yours no matter which hull is tied up. You held the locksmith yesterday; today, keep the tide table.

2 · Allegory — The Tide Table

A harbor town had grown rich, slowly, on the sea. In the old days a supply-ship came once a season, and the whole town would rebuild the dock to fit its particular hull — a fortnight of re-cutting the pier to one ship. There was only one ship a season, so the town could afford to make each berth bespoke. Then the sea changed its temper: ships began arriving weekly, each grander than the last, each captain crying that this hull was the finest ever floated. And the town did what it always had — fell on the pier with saws to re-cut the berth — and before the work was half done, a grander ship hove into view and made all the cutting wrong. The carpenters worked themselves grey; the harbormaster's ledger filled with comparisons obsolete before the ink dried; and the merchants who came to fill their larders found no cargo ever landed, because the town was always rebuilding the pier and never unloading the ship.

A few quiet households asked the only question that mattered: what are the ships actually for? To land cargo that fills the larder. And they saw the town had forgotten it — spending its whole strength on the dock and none on the cargo, and worse, wedding each berth so tightly to one captain's whims that a better ship left them hostage. So they built differently: one standing quay any ship could tie up to, so a new arrival was throwing a line, not rebuilding a pier; a tide table — a disciplined record of what came in and a short watch-list, not a ledger of everything going stale by the hour; and above all they moved their labor off the pier and into the larder — the storehouse, the recipes, the knowledge that was theirs no matter which ship was tied up. They judged every ship by one test: what did it land in our larder? When the season of weekly ships came, they were the calm ones — they did not have the newest ship, but they had a harbor that received every ship without being remade by any of them.

The question it forces: Have we built a standing quay any ship can dock at — a layer between our work and any one model, so swapping to the newest or cheapest is throwing a line, not rebuilding a pier — or is every model still a bespoke integration we are hostage to? Do we keep a tide table, or are we drowning in comparisons that go stale before we finish them? And when we ask what all these ships are for, can we say honestly that our strength is going into the larder — proprietary data, redesigned process, evaluations — or into a pier rebuilt weekly for a ship that will be old by Friday? A harbor wed to one hull is a harbor held hostage by the next; the only harbor that thrives on a rising tide is the one built to receive them all.

3 · Technical — From the Locksmith to the Tide Table

The model has become a fast-moving, commoditising input, and any architecture that treats it as a fixed, load-bearing choice is a pier you will be tearing down every week. The event: four frontier models in seven days (Fable 5.1 + Mythos, Muse Spark 1.3, Gemini 3.8 Flash, GPT-6 Astra); CNBC (6 Sep) reports "model fatigue," buyers capping shortlists at ~5. The answer: a model gateway — a vendor-neutral abstraction layer routing every request through one API, so switching needs no application change; ~37% of orgs already run 5+ models in production (up from ~29%). 5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Build the quay: put a model-agnostic gateway between your work and any one vendor. Stand up (or consolidate onto) a model gateway so no application names a model directly, every request routes through a unified vendor-neutral API, and adopting the newest or cheapest model is a config change — with several models already live behind it. Report how long it takes today to move a production workload from one model to another, and the target once the gateway is in place.

2. Keep the tide table: replace release-chasing with a disciplined watch and a switch trigger. Define a short watch-list of the few models worth tracking, a standing evaluation harness that scores them on your own workloads, and a written trigger for when a release warrants a switch — so a launch is a scheduled evaluation, not a fire drill. Report our shortlist, last evaluation date, and the criteria by which the next model would displace the current one.

3. Move your strength off the pier and into the larder. The differentiator is proprietary data and redesigned process, not the model — and 32% of firms already build internally what they used to buy. Rebalance AI budget and engineering hours away from model chasing toward proprietary data, redesigned process, evaluations owned at your gateway layer, and the deployment work that turns access into ROI. Report the share of AI spend and hours going into that larder versus per-model integration — and a target to shift it.
