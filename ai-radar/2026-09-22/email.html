The Foundation

Tuesday, 22 September 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

The model just commoditized in public — a 40%-bigger Grok 4.7 at an unchanged price drew a shrug — while the labs move up a floor, from selling a model to becoming your "core infrastructure." You can swap the stove in an afternoon; can you swap the foundation? Own the floor, keep the larder, light a second fire.

1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from improving the servant to the ground the whole house stands on — because the model just commoditized in public, and the same vendors are racing to become the foundation, where the lock-in lives.

1. The reality — the model commoditized; a bigger stove earns a shrug. On 21 September 2026 xAI shipped Grok 4.7 — a reported ~2.1T parameters (up ~40% from ~1.5T), same $2/$6 per-million-token price and speed, with real benchmark gains — and it was met with "a notable improvement… but late to the AI Frontier Party," with VentureBeat cautioning that high token consumption threatens ROI (Decrypt/Yahoo Tech; Artificial Analysis; DataCamp; VentureBeat, as reported). $2/$6 sits beside Claude Fable 5.1 at ~$10/$50 — intelligence is a rented, swappable stove.

2. The collision — the labs move up a floor, from model to "core infrastructure." OpenAI's "The next phase of enterprise AI" reframes the pitch to becoming the "core infrastructure" (enterprise >40% of OpenAI revenue and on track to consumer parity by end-2026; Codex 3M WAU; APIs >15B tokens/min; Goldman Sachs, Philips, State Farm named), and Microsoft's $2.5B "Frontier" unit embeds 6,000 forward-deployed engineers in clients (OpenAI; Microsoft; CNBC, as reported). You swap the stove; you do not swap the floor.

3. The answer — own the floor, keep the larder, run a live second source. Put an abstraction you own between the estate and any one model; keep your data, context and evals portable; and light a real fallback — because 81% fear vendor dependency, 47% would see a function stop on an outage, and only 6% believe they could switch cleanly (Zapier 2026, as reported).

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests and purse each improved the servant — the foundation is about the ground the house itself stands on. The market proved the model is a cheap, swappable stove; the vendors answered by racing to become the foundation (core infrastructure, data gravity, embedded engineers). Shop stoves ruthlessly — they should be cheap — but never let the stove-seller pour your foundation.

2 · Allegory — The Foundation

A great house had spent a careful year making its servants excellent — named, booked, bonded, waged, sent to reap, funded with a scoped purse. The servants cooked on a rented stove: the master's shrewdest economy, swapped in an afternoon whenever a better one came. This week a merchant wheeled in the newest stove — half again as large — and the cook only shrugged: "It's bigger, it costs the same, and it's late. The one we have is fine." The stove had become a commodity, and that was the master winning the game he'd set out to win.

But the same company that rented the cheap stove had been doing something quieter in the cellar: it had offered — so helpfully, at so fair a price — to pour the foundation the whole house stood on. And the master, delighted to think only about cooking, had said yes, and yes again, not noticing that a stove you swap in an afternoon and a foundation you cannot move without razing the house are not the same kind of thing at all. The careful houses asked a colder question: if this company doubled its price or shut its gates, how many rooms go dark — and could I move? Four in five already lay awake over the dependency; nearly one in two knew a room would go dark; and only six in a hundred believed they could build on another's ground without tearing out something load-bearing. That is not a rented stove. That is a foundation already poured.

So the wise houses learned three things: own the floor (keep the stove a component that plugs into a hearth you built); keep your own larder (your grain, recipes and tests live in your pantry, portable, never only in the company's cellar); and keep a second fire lit (an actual flame you've cooked on, not a slide about one). For while every house happily compared stoves, the companies that rented them were racing to become the ground beneath — and one such company, that season, paused its own great work in the cellar, and every house built on its floor felt the tremor without being consulted.

The question it forces: We can swap the model in an afternoon — the market just proved it, with a shrug at a stove half again as large. But can we swap the vendor our whole estate now stands on? Do we own the floor — an abstraction, our data and recipes in our own larder, a second fire actually lit — or have we let the stove-seller quietly pour the foundation under us, so that we are among the 94 in 100 who could not move?

3 · Technical — From the Purse to the Foundation

Every prior control governs the servant; the foundation is about the ground. The thing that commoditized (the model) is not the thing that locks you in (the foundation) — and confusing the two is how a house that feels thrifty ends up unable to move off a floor it never chose to pour. The reality: capability parity is arriving at wildly different prices (Grok 4.7 $2/$6 vs Claude Fable 5.1 ~$10/$50), so treat models as interchangeable components routed per task behind an interface you own; "bigger" is no longer the story. The collision: as the model commoditized, the lock-in moved to the layers you don't swap in an afternoon — identity, data gravity, proprietary orchestration, embedded engineers; the stove got cheap so the foundation could get sticky, and a fallback you've never lit is not a fallback (81% worried, 47% a function stops, only 6% confident of a clean switch). The answer: own the floor (an abstraction/gateway you control), keep the larder (portable data, context, evals), and light a second fire (a qualified alternate, tested under load).

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Own the floor. The model is a cheap, swappable stove ($2/$6 vs $10/$50), and that is good news you capture only if switching is cheap. Route your top few AI workloads through a gateway/abstraction you control, so each model is a component you plug in and pull out, and route per task on price/quality across at least two vendors. Report the share of critical AI spend behind an abstraction you own versus wired into one vendor's proprietary layer.

2. Keep the larder. The lock-in moved off the model onto the foundation (identity, data gravity, orchestration, embedded engineers); 81% fear that dependency and only 6% believe they could switch cleanly. Inventory where your prompts, context, retrieval data and eval suites live, and move anything load-bearing out of a vendor's proprietary cellar into a form you can export and re-point. Report the share of your AI knowledge assets that are portable by design.

3. Light a second fire. A fallback you've never lit is a foundation, not a stove; 47% say a key function would stop on a primary-vendor outage or price change. Pick one critical AI-dependent workflow and run a timed, under-load cut-over to a second supplier — measure the time-to-switch and what breaks — so the 6% number becomes your tested reality. Report the measured time-to-switch and a dated plan to extend it to the next two.
