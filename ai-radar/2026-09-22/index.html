Tuesday, 22 September 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering

Yesterday, 21 September 2026, xAI shipped Grok 4.7 — a reported ~2.1-trillion-parameter model (up ~40% from Grok 4.6's ~1.5T) at the same $2/$6 per-million-token price and speed, with real benchmark gains — and the market's verdict was a shrug: "a notable improvement… but late to the AI Frontier Party" (Decrypt/Yahoo Tech; Artificial Analysis; VentureBeat, as reported). A bigger stove arrived and no one gasped, because the model has commoditized (Grok 4.7 at $2/$6 beside Claude Fable 5.1 at a reported $10/$50). In the same window, OpenAI's "The next phase of enterprise AI" reframes the pitch from selling a model to becoming your "core infrastructure" (enterprise >40% of OpenAI revenue, on track to consumer parity by end-2026; Codex 3M WAU; APIs >15B tokens/min; Goldman Sachs, Philips, State Farm named). The board question is no longer which model is best or cheapest — the stove is cheap and swappable — it is: while we shop stoves, is one vendor quietly pouring the foundation our whole estate stands on, and could we ever move off it? A Zapier 2026 survey reports 81% of leaders worried about vendor dependency, 47% saying a key function would stop on an outage or price change, and only 6% confident they could switch cleanly (as reported). That is not a rented stove. That is a foundation already poured.

1 · Executive Summary 90-second read

For a long year the radar worked on the servant — name it (the livery), keep one book (the steward), put your name on the bond (the surety), check its wage (the second wage), send it to reap (the two harvests), hand it a scoped purse (the purse). Every one of those disciplines improves the agent. This morning the axis turns from the servant to the ground the whole house stands on. Two developments in the same week make the shift plain, and they point in opposite directions on purpose.

The reality — the model has commoditized; a bigger stove now earns a shrug. On 21 September 2026 xAI released Grok 4.7, a reported ~2.1-trillion-parameter model (up ~40% from Grok 4.6's ~1.5T), at the same $2/$6 per-million-token price and speed, with real benchmark gains (a reported 1657 Elo on AA-Briefcase, +111 over 4.6; 1695 on GDPval; 46.3% on CursorBench 4.0 vs 40.4%; 71.0% on DeepSWE v1.1 at high effort vs 65.2%) — and it was met with "a notable improvement… but late to the AI Frontier Party," after Musk reportedly walked the timeline back at least five times since late July (Decrypt/Yahoo Tech; Artificial Analysis; DataCamp; VentureBeat, as reported). VentureBeat's caveat travels with it: the coding gains pair with high token consumption that threatens real-world ROI. The signal underneath the launch is bigger than the launch: when a 40%-bigger frontier model at an unchanged low price is greeted with a shrug, the model is a rented stove — cheap, plentiful, and swappable (Grok 4.7 at $2/$6 next to Claude Fable 5.1 at a reported $10/$50, DeepSeek and Gemini Flash cheaper still).

The collision — the same labs are moving up a floor, from model to "core infrastructure." In the same window, OpenAI's "The next phase of enterprise AI" reframes the pitch: not a better brain to rent, but the "core infrastructure for AI" an enterprise runs on — reporting enterprise >40% of OpenAI's revenue and on track to reach parity with consumer by end-2026, Codex at 3M weekly active users, APIs at >15B tokens/minute, with Goldman Sachs, Philips and State Farm as named customers, and Microsoft's $2.5B "Frontier" unit embedding 6,000 forward-deployed engineers inside client houses (OpenAI; Microsoft; CNBC, as reported). This is the quiet part: you swap the stove; you do not swap the floor. A Zapier 2026 enterprise survey reports 81% of leaders worried about AI-vendor dependency, 47% saying a key business function would stop on a primary-vendor outage or price change, and only 6% confident they could switch their primary provider without material operational disruption. Concentration is structural — three hyperscalers held ~63% of global cloud-infrastructure spend in early 2026, and the foundation models beneath most enterprise AI trace to a handful of labs (CSA; Development Corporate, as reported) — and it turned concrete when OpenAI (18 Aug 2026) paused reinforcement-learning training on its newest models and put its largest planned frontier run on hold indefinitely: a supplier's internal decision rippling straight into every house built on it.

The stakes — keep the foundation yours, so the cheap stove stays swappable. The board move re-uses the whole year's discipline and points it one level down, at the ground. First, own the floor between you and any one supplier — an abstraction/gateway so the model is a swappable component, not a load-bearing wall; the whole point of a rented stove is that you can change it. Second, keep your knowledge in your own larder — your data, context, evals and integration logic are the estate; if they live only inside one vendor's proprietary layer, you have poured that vendor into your foundation. Third, keep a live second supplier, not a slide about one — the 6% number is the tell: a fallback you have never lit is a foundation, not a stove. And do it now, while the stove market is fiercely competitive and cheap, because the vendors are racing to become the floor precisely while you are distracted comparing stoves.

2 · Allegory of the Day

🖋️ "The Foundation"

Topic: This morning the frame turns from improving the servant to the ground the house stands on. On 21 September 2026 xAI shipped Grok 4.7 — a reported ~2.1-trillion-parameter model, ~40% larger than Grok 4.6, at the same $2/$6 price and speed, with real benchmark gains — and it was met with "a notable improvement… but late to the AI Frontier Party" (Decrypt/Yahoo Tech; Artificial Analysis; VentureBeat, as reported): the surest sign yet that the model has commoditized. In the same window OpenAI's "The next phase of enterprise AI" reframed the pitch from selling a model to becoming an enterprise's "core infrastructure" (enterprise >40% of OpenAI revenue, on track to consumer parity by end-2026; Codex 3M WAU; >15B tokens/min), while a Zapier 2026 survey reports 81% of leaders worried about vendor dependence, 47% saying a function would stop on an outage, and only 6% confident they could switch cleanly (as reported). The foundation allegory is the radar's own illustration.

There was a great house that had spent a long and careful year making its servants excellent. It had named every hand and cut it a scoped key, hired a steward to keep one honest book, accepted that the master's own name stood surety for the servants' work, learned to ask whose second coin each advising servant took, sent the governed servants into the fields to reap, and handed each a scoped purse with a drawstring the master could reach. By any fair reckoning it was a well-run house, and the master was proud of his servants — as he should have been.

Now, the servants cooked on a rented stove. This had been the master's shrewdest economy: he did not build a great furnace and marry it, he rented his fire, and when a better stove came along he swapped it in an afternoon and paid a little less. And this very week a merchant wheeled in the newest stove — a great brass thing, near half again the size of last season's, and the master braced for the usual gasp from his kitchen. But the cook only glanced at it and shrugged. "It's bigger," she said, "and it costs the same, and it's late. The one we have is fine. Wheel it in if you like." The master laughed, because he understood the shrug for what it was: the stove had become a commodity. Fire was fire; it was cheap, it was plentiful, and any stove could be swapped for any other before supper. This was not a defeat. This was the master winning the game he had set out to win.

But here is the thing the master nearly missed, because he was busy admiring stoves. The same great trading company that rented him the cheap stove had, over that same careful year, been doing something quieter in the cellar. It had offered — so helpfully, at so fair a price — to lay the foundation the whole house stood on. To run the water and the drains, to hold the deeds and the ledgers, to pour the very floor. "Do not trouble yourself with the ground," the company had said; "let us be the foundation you build on, and you need only think about the cooking." And the master, delighted to think only about cooking, had said yes, and yes again, and had not noticed that a stove you swap in an afternoon and a foundation you cannot move without razing the house are not the same kind of thing at all.

Two roads, as always. The easy road was to keep saying yes — the company was competent and cheap, the floor was excellent, and why would a man dig up a good floor? The wiser road, which the careful houses took, began with a cold question asked of the estate itself: not "which stove is best?" but "if this company doubled its price tomorrow, or shut its gates, or simply changed its mind about a thing in the cellar, how many rooms of my house would go dark — and could I move?" And when the careful houses counted honestly, the answer frightened them. Four houses in five already lay awake over how much they leaned on their supplier; nearly one in two knew a whole room would go dark the day the supplier stumbled; and only six houses in a hundred believed they could pack up and build on another company's ground without tearing something load-bearing out of their own walls. That is not the arithmetic of a rented stove. That is the arithmetic of a foundation already poured.

So the wise houses learned three plain things, and they cost less to do early than late. The first: own the floor between the kitchen and any one supplier. Keep the stove a component — a thing that plugs into a hearth you built and can be pulled out and replaced — never a load-bearing wall. The whole worth of a rented stove is that you can change it; a stove you cannot change is just an expensive furnace you do not own. The second: keep your own larder. Your grain, your recipes, your knowledge of your own kitchen — your data, your context, the tests by which you know good work from bad — these are the estate, and they must live in your pantry, portable, and never only inside the company's cellar; for a house whose larder is in another man's cellar does not own its dinner. And the third: keep a second fire lit. Not a drawing of a second stove, not a promise from a second merchant — an actual second flame you have cooked on, so that the morning the first goes cold you serve dinner anyway. The six-in-a-hundred are the houses that mistook a slide about a fallback for a fallback.

Here is the part that should keep a master awake. The stove market was loud, cheap, and wonderful — a new stove every few weeks, each bigger and no dearer — and all that glorious noise was, in part, a kindly distraction. While every house in the valley happily compared stoves, the companies that rented them were racing, quietly, to become the ground the houses stood on — for a merchant who has poured your foundation has a hold on you that no stove-seller ever could, and he knows it. One such company, that very season, had paused a great work of its own in the cellar and put another on hold indefinitely — its own affair, entirely — and every house built on its floor felt the tremor without ever having been consulted. The lesson was not that the company was wicked. The lesson was that when you do not own your foundation, another man's ordinary decisions become earthquakes in your house.

The moral, stated plainly: the model is a rented stove, now so cheap and plentiful that a stove half again as large draws only a shrug; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising servant eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap and weigh what it brings in; the purse is why you fund it like an employee and not an heir — and the foundation is why, having made your servants excellent, you must now look down at the ground they stand on and refuse to let the company that rents you the cheap, swappable stove quietly pour itself into the floor you cannot leave: own the hearth so the stove stays a component, keep your grain and recipes in your own larder, and keep a second fire actually lit, so that when the model of the month is superseded — and it will be, by supper — you swap the stove and never re-pour the ground. A house that lets the stove-seller lay its foundation has not been thrifty; it has rented its kitchen and mortgaged its cellar to the same man, and called the arrangement a bargain.

The question it forces: We can swap the model in an afternoon — the market just proved it, with a shrug at a stove half again as large. But can we swap the vendor our whole estate now stands on? Do we own the floor between our house and any one supplier — an abstraction, our data and recipes in our own larder, a second fire actually lit — or have we let the company that rents us the cheap stove quietly pour the foundation under us, so that we are among the ninety-four houses in a hundred that could not move without tearing something load-bearing out of our own walls?

3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
  • Have we confused the swappable stove with the poured foundation? The model just commoditized in public — a 40%-bigger Grok 4.7 at an unchanged price drew a shrug, and $2/$6 sits beside $10/$50 across vendors. For each critical AI capability, can we name what is a swappable component (the model) and what is a load-bearing dependency on one vendor (identity, data gravity, proprietary orchestration, embedded engineers) — and which one is actually holding up the house?
  • If our primary AI vendor doubled its price, went dark, or changed a decision in its own cellar tomorrow, what stops working — and could we move? A reported 47% say a key function would stop on a primary-vendor outage or price change, and only 6% believe they could switch cleanly (Zapier 2026). Have we measured our own version of the 6% — a tested, timed cut-over to a second supplier — or do we only have a slide that says "multi-vendor"?
  • Do we own the floor between us and any one model? Is there an abstraction/gateway we control so a model is a component we plug in and pull out — or is a specific vendor's proprietary layer wired directly into our load-bearing systems, so that swapping the "cheap, swappable" model means re-pouring the foundation?
🏦 Financial Services
  • Concentration is a supervised risk in your world, and it now includes AI: three hyperscalers held ~63% of cloud spend and a handful of labs supply most foundation models (CSA; Development Corporate, as reported). Can we produce, for our regulator and our own board, a concentration-risk map of our AI stack — model, inference, data, orchestration — with a tested exit for each single point of failure?
  • OpenAI paused RL training and put its largest planned run on hold indefinitely (18 Aug 2026) — a supplier's internal choice with external tremors. Have we stress-tested a primary-AI-vendor "stumble" (outage, deprecation, price step, model withdrawal) the way we stress-test a counterparty default — and do our critical workflows degrade gracefully rather than stop?
🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
  • Clinical, safety and regulatory work is validated against a specific model's behaviour; when that model is deprecated or changed, the validation may not carry. When the stove is swapped — and it will be, cheaply and often — do our validated workflows and our evals travel with us, or is our validation effectively a bet on one vendor's foundation staying put?
  • Patient data is the ultimate larder. Is our clinical data, context and knowledge held in a form we own and can move, or does it accrete inside one vendor's proprietary layer such that leaving means leaving the data behind — the truest form of a poured foundation?
🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
  • You already run dual-sourcing and supplier-qualification discipline for physical inputs; AI is now a critical input. Have we applied the same second-source rule to models and AI infrastructure — a qualified, live alternate — or is our AI supply chain single-sourced in a way we would never tolerate for a critical component?
  • Embedded-engineer programs (Microsoft's $2.5B Frontier unit; forward-deployed teams) put a vendor's people inside your operations. Where a vendor's engineers are embedded, does the resulting system, data and know-how remain ours and portable when they leave — or are we building a foundation only their people can maintain?
🛒 Retail / Consumer
  • Your margins depend on cheap, swappable inference — and the good news is that it is now genuinely cheap and swappable. Are we actively arbitraging the commoditized model market (routing to the best-value model per task) behind an abstraction we own — or are we paying a premium and locked to one vendor because switching would touch load-bearing systems?
  • Peak-season reliability is existential. If our primary AI provider had an outage on our biggest sales day, does checkout / search / support degrade to a live second source, or go dark — and have we actually run that failover under load?
🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
  • Public accountability and continuity of service make vendor lock-in a governance failure, not just a cost. Under the duties you already owe (EU AI Act high-risk regime, live since 2 August 2026), can we show that no critical public service is built on a single AI vendor's foundation with no tested exit — and that our data and models are portable by design?
  • Sovereignty and long horizons matter most here. Are we procuring AI as a swappable utility on ground we own (open weights where fit, portable data, an abstraction layer) — or are we pouring a foreign or single-vendor foundation under services citizens will still need in a decade?

4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Purse to the Foundation

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking is this agent named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping and funded? and started asking the one question none of those controls touch: what ground does the whole estate stand on, and could I move it? The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the keyring is which key it holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what it can reach; the bell discloses when it slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery gives it a scoped identity; the steward keeps one office; the surety puts your name on the bond; the second wage asks whose interest it serves; the two harvests sends it to reap; the purse funds it. Every one of those governs the servant. The foundation is about the ground — and this week the market made the distinction impossible to ignore: it proved the model is a swappable stove (a 40%-bigger Grok 4.7 at an unchanged price drew a shrug) in the very week the labs stopped selling stoves and started offering to be your foundation (OpenAI's "core infrastructure"; Microsoft's embedded engineers). The point is blunt: the thing that has commoditized (the model) is not the thing that locks you in (the foundation), and confusing the two is how a house that feels thrifty — swapping cheap stoves — ends up unable to move off a floor it never chose to pour.

  • The reality — the model is a rented, swappable stove, and its commoditization is now public. On 21 September 2026 xAI shipped Grok 4.7 — a reported ~2.1T parameters (up ~40% from ~1.5T), same $2/$6 per-million-token price and speed, with real gains (1657 Elo AA-Briefcase, +111; 1695 GDPval; 46.3% CursorBench 4.0 vs 40.4%; 71.0% DeepSWE v1.1 high effort vs 65.2%) — and was greeted with "a notable improvement… but late to the AI Frontier Party," with VentureBeat cautioning that high token consumption threatens real-world ROI (Decrypt/Yahoo Tech; Artificial Analysis; DataCamp; VentureBeat, as reported). Architecturally, three things follow. First, capability parity is arriving at wildly different prices — Grok 4.7 at $2/$6, Claude Fable 5.1 at a reported $10/$50, DeepSeek and Gemini Flash lower still — so the rational posture is to treat models as interchangeable components routed per task on price/quality (General Input: "the stream of intelligence becoming completely commoditized"). Second, a swappable component must sit behind an interface you own — the value of commoditization is captured only if switching is cheap, which requires an abstraction/gateway between your systems and any one model. Third, "bigger" is no longer the story — the shrug at a 40%-larger model, and the ROI caveat on token consumption, say the frontier that matters to enterprises is cost-per-useful-task and fit, not raw scale.
  • The collision — the labs are moving up a floor, from model to "core infrastructure." OpenAI's "The next phase of enterprise AI" reframes the offer from a model to the "core infrastructure for AI" — reporting enterprise >40% of OpenAI revenue and on track to consumer parity by end-2026, Codex at 3M WAU and APIs at >15B tokens/minute, with Goldman Sachs, Philips and State Farm named; Microsoft's $2.5B "Frontier" unit embeds 6,000 forward-deployed engineers inside client houses; and OpenAI (18 Aug 2026) paused RL training on its newest models and put its largest planned frontier run on hold indefinitely (OpenAI; Microsoft; CNBC, as reported). Two engineering realities cut against comfort. First, the lock-in migrated off the model. As the model commoditized, the durable lock-in moved to the layers you don't swap in an afternoon: identity and auth, data gravity, proprietary orchestration and memory, embedded human expertise. The stove got cheap precisely so the foundation could get sticky. Second, a fallback you have never lit is not a fallback. The Zapier numbers are an engineering verdict, not a survey mood: 81% worried about dependency, 47% a function stops on an outage or price change, only 6% confident of a clean switch — the gap between "we're multi-vendor on a slide" and "we have cut over under load" is the whole risk.
  • The answer — own the floor, keep the larder, run a live second source. Put an abstraction/gateway you control between your estate and any one model (so a model is a component you plug in and pull out); keep your data, context, prompts and evals in a portable form you own (not accreted inside one vendor's proprietary layer); and keep a qualified second supplier actually lit and tested under load — reusing the identity, logging and ownership disciplines you already built for the livery, the steward and the purse, now pointed one level down at the ground. This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now aimed at the sharpest axis of all: not what the agent is, serves, harvests or spends, but whether the ground under the whole house is yours to keep or another man's to change.

The strategic core: the model commoditizing is good news you must actually collect — cheap, swappable intelligence — and you collect it only by owning the floor it plugs into; the danger is that the same vendors, watching the stove commoditize, are racing to become the foundation, where the real lock-in lives. The market makes the temptation concrete: it feels thrifty and modern to swap cheap stoves, and it feels like friction to build your own hearth and light a second fire — so most houses take the cheap stove and let the seller pour the floor. The board translation: shop stoves ruthlessly; never let the stove-seller pour your foundation. The failure mode is exact: a house that names, governs, funds and perfects its servants — and then wires one vendor's proprietary layer into its load-bearing systems while congratulating itself on cheap models — will pass every governance review and discover, the first time that vendor changes a price or pauses a run, that it is one of the 94% that cannot move.

Why "the foundation" is the discipline that matters now

For a long year the worry was whether the servant was named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping and funded. This morning the market moved the question underneath all of that: the model commoditized in public, and the vendors answered by racing to become the foundation. Grok 4.7's shrug and the $2/$6-vs-$10/$50 spread prove intelligence is now a rented, swappable stove; OpenAI's "core infrastructure" pitch and Microsoft's embedded engineers prove the lock-in has moved to the floor. Governance that stopped at the servant will not protect the ground. The estates that keep their standing will collect the good news (cheap, swappable models) by owning the floor those models plug into — and will refuse, deliberately, to let the company that rents them the stove pour the foundation they cannot leave.

How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now sharpened to be able to move the ground your AI stands on. On a legacy estate the instinct, after a hard year of governance work, is to consolidate on the one vendor that already knows the house — it is easier, cheaper this quarter, and the embedded engineers are helpful. Under the foundation that is exactly the reflex to check: consolidation is how a swappable stove quietly becomes a load-bearing wall. The retrofit is own, keep, light. Own the floor: put an abstraction/gateway between your systems and any one model, so models are components. Keep the larder: hold your data, context, prompts and evals in a portable form you control, never only inside a vendor's proprietary layer. Light a second fire: qualify and actually run an alternate supplier under real load, so the 6% number is your reality, not your risk. And keep the whole refrain: the model is a rented stove, now so cheap a bigger one draws a shrug; the knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door; the passport names each agent; the census is the roll; the inspector asks for it; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what an agent can reach; the bell discloses when your own agent slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why your name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising agent eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee — and the foundation is why, having perfected the servant, you must look down at the ground and refuse to let the stove-seller pour it: own the hearth, keep the larder, light a second fire, so the cheap stove stays swappable and no vendor becomes the floor you cannot leave.

7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

  1. Own the floor — put an abstraction between your estate and any one model, and collect the commoditization dividend. The model is now a cheap, swappable stove (Grok 4.7 +40% bigger at the same $2/$6 price drew a shrug; $2/$6 vs $10/$50 across labs), and that is good news you capture only if switching is cheap. This month, route your top few AI workloads through a gateway/abstraction you control, so each model is a component you can plug in and pull out, and start routing per task on price/quality across at least two vendors. Report one number to the board: the share of critical AI spend that sits behind an abstraction you own versus wired directly into one vendor's proprietary layer.
  2. Keep the larder — hold your data, context and evals in a portable form you own. The lock-in has moved off the model and onto the foundation (identity, data gravity, orchestration, embedded engineers), and 81% already fear that dependency while only 6% believe they could switch cleanly (Zapier 2026). This month, inventory where your prompts, context, retrieval data and evaluation suites actually live, and move anything load-bearing out of a single vendor's proprietary cellar into a form you can export and re-point at another model. Report the share of your AI knowledge assets (data, prompts, evals) that are portable by design.
  3. Light a second fire — qualify and actually run an alternate supplier under load. A fallback you have never lit is a foundation, not a stove; 47% say a key function would stop on a primary-vendor outage or price change. This month, pick one critical AI-dependent workflow and run a timed, under-load cut-over to a second supplier — measure the time-to-switch and what breaks — so the 6% number becomes your tested reality, not your unmeasured risk. Report the measured time-to-switch for that workflow and a dated plan to extend it to the next two.
