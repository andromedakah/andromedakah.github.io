The Quarrel

Monday, 19 October 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

You perfected the single agent — named, scoped, grounded. New research says 82% of boards are confident they can govern each agent, but only 26% can resolve a conflict between agents. When two faithful agents give the system opposite orders, who is the magistrate? Appoint a court: a shared control plane, house rules as policy-as-code, a bell that halts a collision in flight.

1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from governing each agent to governing the crowd of them — because the house is no longer running an agent, it is running a staff, and a staff has a failure mode a single agent never had: two of them, each perfectly governed, giving each other contradictory orders.

1. The gap — confident about each agent, unready for the collision. New research from OutSystems with CIO Dive and KPMG (~850 IT decision-makers; fieldwork 16–29 June 2026) reports 82% of executives confident they can govern, integrate and monitor AI agents — but only 26% well prepared to resolve conflicts across agents, systems and automated workflows (as reported). Four boards in five trust the individual agent; barely one in four can settle a quarrel between two.

2. The complication — the newest model's whole pitch is memory. On 12 October Meta shipped Muse Wonder, its first frontier model, built on "active memory" — an agent that remembers and acts on what it is told (self-reported MemoryBench lead; Opus 5.5 still leads the main independent index; $4/$20 per-M tokens, as reported). A memory-bearing agent arrives at every corridor already certain, and is therefore the most likely to collide. Capability fills the corridor; it does not clear it.

3. The answer — appoint a magistrate, post the house rules, hang a bell. KPMG's read: assurance must be "built in from the start," with a shared control plane, policy-as-code and runtime guardrails (as reported). One office over all agents (not a folder of per-agent policies), precedence a machine enforces, and a guardrail that can halt a collision in flight — kept yours and portable, so the 62% who want to keep switching vendors still can.

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each governed one servant — the quarrel is what happens when there are many servants and no court. A 56-point gap (82% vs 26%) sits exactly where the agentic era actually breaks: two agents, each flawless, each obeying orders, undo each other, and the house that audited both has no one to say which wins. Stop adding servants; appoint the magistrate.

2 · Allegory — The Quarrel

A great house had spent a careful season making each of its servants excellent — named, booked, bonded, waged, sent to reap, funded, standing on ground it owned. But the house had changed shape: where once there was a servant, now there were dozens, all working the same corridors at the same hour. One ordinary morning two of them collided. The buyer, told to cut the month's costs, cancelled a standing order. The planner, told to keep the shelves full, had placed that very order an hour before. Each had done exactly as instructed; each was, on its own, blameless. And the house — which had audited the buyer and found him sound, and the planner and found her sound — stood with two faithful servants pointing at each other and discovered it had no one whose office it was to say which order won.

The master had not seen it coming, because every tool he owned looked at one servant at a time. Four masters in five swore their households were in hand; but pressed on the plain case — two of your own servants, each right, each undoing the other, this morning, in your hallway — only about one in four could name who would settle it. The rest had governed every servant and appointed no magistrate. A corridor does not run on good intentions; it runs on precedence — and the buyer and planner were both sensible, which is exactly why neither would yield.

So the wise houses built the office they had skipped, in three parts: a magistrate who sees the whole floor at once (one bench that can see both parties, not a folder of private rulebooks); house rules posted as plain law a servant cannot misread and the magistrate can enforce without summoning the master; and a bell that halts both servants the instant they reach for the same thing, so the house decides before the damage is done. And they knew the market would make the corridor busier, not calmer: the newest, most prized servants are the ones that remember — and a servant with a long memory arrives already certain, the most likely of all to collide.

The question it forces: We can vouch for each of our agents one at a time — most of us are confident we can. But can we name the office that settles it when two of our good agents, each obeying its orders, demand opposite things in the same workflow at the same moment? Do we have a magistrate, house rules a machine enforces, and a bell that halts a collision in flight — or are we among the three houses in four that governed every servant and never built the court?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Quarrel

Every prior control governs a single agent; the quarrel is a property of the system. An agent can be perfectly trustworthy and still produce a catastrophic outcome by colliding with another perfectly trustworthy agent — and no amount of per-agent assurance closes that gap, only a shared adjudicating layer does. The gap is structural: per-agent assurance does not compose (a set of correct agents is not a correct system); conflict is the default, not the exception (cut-cost vs. hold-stock, retain vs. collect), so without arbitration "last write wins" silently becomes your policy; and the hard part is shared state — 35% name data-consistency and context-sharing the hardest hurdle. The remedy is an office, not a better agent: a magistrate (one control plane with sight of every agent), house rules (policy-as-code: precedence and mutual-exclusion a machine enforces), and a bell (runtime guardrails that intercept conflicting actions before they commit) — kept yours and portable. Memory raises the stakes: Meta's memory-first Muse Wonder (12 Oct) makes agents carry context and collide with conviction, and adds a fresh conflict axis — an agent's remembered instruction vs. current policy.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Appoint the magistrate. The gap is structural (82% can govern each agent, only 26% can resolve conflicts between them) because every tool looks at one agent at a time. Name the single office — a person and a control plane — with visibility over every production agent and the authority to adjudicate a conflict, and point it at your highest-traffic multi-agent workflow first. Report the share of production agents under a shared adjudicating layer versus governed only in isolation.

2. Post the house rules. A corridor runs on precedence, not good intentions. Take your top three "two agents want opposite things" cases (cut-cost vs. hold-stock, retain vs. collect, markdown vs. margin), write who yields and why as machine-enforced policy-as-code, version and test it — including the memory case, a remembered instruction vs. current policy. Report the share of your conflict rules that are executable policy, versus documentation no system reads.

3. Hang a bell. A court that rules only after the harm is a historian; 35% already name shared, consistent context as the hardest hurdle, which is where collisions start. Put a runtime guardrail in the commit path that can intercept, hold or arbitrate conflicting agent actions before they execute — on one governed source of truth — and run a drill where two agents collide on purpose. Report whether the bell caught it, and keep the magistrate yours and portable (62%).
