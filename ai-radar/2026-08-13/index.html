The datable signal. On 12 August 2026, Cognition — the AI-coding company behind the Devin agent — was reported (TechCrunch, Bloomberg) to be in talks to raise more than $1 billion at a valuation of at least $40 billion. Do the arithmetic the market is doing: a ~$40B tag on a ~$1B run-rate is an implied ~40× forward-revenue multiple — the market paying today, in full, for a harvest that has not yet been picked.

Why it matters more than one funding round. A 40× forward multiple on a still-young agent company is not a rogue data point; it is the price of the entire agent narrative, written down in one line. The market is valuing agents by their blossom — growth rate, benchmark scores, total-addressable-market story, demo magic — and not by their fruit: audited, repeatable business value in the hands of the enterprises that buy them. And the fruit is still thin: PwC's 2026 Global CEO Survey reports 56% of chief executives saw no measurable AI ROI in the past year, even as 91% use AI and roughly 65% raised AI budgets (median +22%). The gap between what the market pays for agents and what buyers can prove they return is about as wide as it has ever been — which is exactly the condition in which a board overpays.

2 · Allegory of the Day

🌸 "The Blossom Price"

Topic: On 12 August 2026, Cognition (maker of the Devin coding agent) was reported to be in talks to raise more than $1B at a valuation of at least $40B — up from a ~$26B valuation three months earlier — on a revenue run-rate only now approaching $1B, an implied ~40× forward-revenue multiple, with the valuation roughly doubling in a single quarter. It lands against a hard adoption backdrop: PwC's 2026 Global CEO Survey reporting 56% of CEOs saw no measurable AI ROI in the past year even as 91% use AI. Meanwhile the trust layer is being poured — the AITSC launched 12 August, and the EU AI Act's transparency duties have been enforceable since 2 August. The lesson: the market is pricing AI agents by their growth and promise (the blossom) rather than their audited, realized return (the fruit) — so the board's discipline is to underwrite agents, vendors and its own deployments by the harvest actually picked.

Picture an orchard town in a year when the whole valley went mad for apples. For as long as anyone could remember, an orchard had been valued the plain way: by the fruit in the barn — how many bushels it threw off in autumn, how reliably, at what cost to work it. A buyer walked the rows in October, counted the crates, checked the ledger of past harvests, and named a price a sober man could defend. An orchard was worth its yield.

Then, almost overnight, the town began to price orchards by the blossom instead. A new kind of buyer came through in spring — not autumn — and bid on the trees while they were still in flower. They did not count crates; they counted blooms. They looked at how fast a young orchard had spread its canopy, how white and thick the blossom hung, how loudly the neighbors talked about it, and they paid — in full, in advance — for the harvest the flowers promised. And because blossom is beautiful and the valley was giddy, the prices did a strange thing. One young orchard, barely of bearing age, was bid at a price in one spring; and by the very next spring — before it had shipped a single extra barrel worth speaking of — it was bid at nearly double, on nothing but a heavier bloom and a better story. The price had come unmoored from the barn. Men were no longer buying apples; they were buying the promise of apples, at forty times what the trees had ever actually yielded.

Here is what the giddy buyers forgot, and the shrewd one did not. Blossom is a promise; fruit is a fact, and a long, uncertain season stands between the two. Frost can take the flower in a night. The bloom can set thin. Pests, drought, a bad picking — a hundred things drop between the branch and the barn, and every one of them turns a glorious spring into a lean autumn. Worse, the whole valley had just lived through exactly that lesson and refused to hear it: for all the white blossom of the last few springs, more than half the orchard-owners could not yet point to a single extra coin of profit from the mania — the flowers had come, and the fruit, mostly, had not. And even where fruit did set, a new question had arrived at the market gate that spring: the assessors now demanded to know whose orchard this really was, whether its apples were labeled and sound, and whether its books would survive an audit — because a barrel you cannot vouch for cannot be sold into the good houses at any price.

So the town split into two kinds of buyer. The blossom buyers paid the spring price, told themselves the story of the coming harvest, and renewed their bids each year on a heavier bloom — a beautiful ledger of flowers and a barn that never quite filled. The fruit buyer did the harder, duller thing. She still walked the rows in flower, and she was glad to; but she priced the orchard by the yield she could actually verify — bushels picked, sold, and paid for, at a cost she could name — and she treated the bloom as what it was: a reason to watch the tree, not a reason to pay for it. She asked the yield questions out loud at the gate: What has this orchard actually harvested, not promised? What stands between this blossom and my barn — frost, drop, a season that turns? If the valley's giddiness passes and prices snap back to the barn, what am I left holding? And can I even bring this fruit to the good houses — is it mine, is it labeled, will its books stand? She paid less, she owned more, and when the frost finally came — as it always eventually does to a valley that has confused flowers with food — she still had a barn full of apples while her neighbors had a hillside full of petals.

The moral: when a whole market starts pricing things by their bloom, the discipline is to keep pricing by the barn. Blossom is growth, and demo, and total-addressable-market, and a valuation that doubles on a story; fruit is audited, repeatable value in the hands of the buyer, sold and paid for. Do not pay the blossom price. Underwrite the agent — the vendor you buy and the project you build — by the harvest it has actually delivered, price the long season that stands between promise and profit, and make sure every barrel is one you can label, vouch for, and defend — because the season the world paid forty times the yield for the flower was not the season the careful got poorer; it was the season they quietly out-bought everyone by refusing to.

The question it forces: The market is pricing AI agents by their blossom — growth rate, benchmark, TAM, a valuation that doubles in a quarter on a promise. Are we pricing the agents we buy and the ones we build the same giddy way — or are we underwriting them by the fruit actually in the barn: realized value per workload, the vendor's durability if the valuation resets, and whether the output is one we can audit, label and defend under the trust rules now live?

🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
  • Are we valuing AI — our vendors and our own deployments — by the blossom or the fruit? When an agent company doubles to ~$40B in a quarter at an implied ~40× forward revenue, that is the market pricing the promise. For each of our material AI bets, can we name the fruit — audited value per workload, sold and paid for — or are we renewing on growth-rate and demo romance?
  • PwC reports 56% of CEOs saw no measurable AI ROI last year, yet budgets rose ~22%. What share of our AI spend is tied to a measured, repeatable return — and what share is us paying the blossom price on a harvest we have not yet picked?
  • If the valley's giddiness passes and agent valuations snap back to the barn, what are we left holding? Which of our workflows would break if a richly-valued vendor reset, tightened, or was acquired — and have we priced that exit?
🏦 Financial Services
  • You underwrite forward multiples for a living. Would our own credit or equity desk lend against a ~40× forward-revenue, cash-burning counterparty without covenants — and if not, why are we embedding one in a core workflow without the same discipline?
  • Concentration risk has a new face: a single, richly-valued agent vendor woven through the front office. Have we stress-tested the failure or repricing of that vendor the way we stress-test any large, illiquid, long-dated exposure — and do we hold the audit trail the EU AI Act and our regulators now expect?
🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
  • Clinical and discovery agents are being sold on the bloom — benchmark scores and demos — where the fruit is hardest to verify. Before a multi-year commitment, can we name the measured clinical or research outcome per dollar, not the vendor's growth story?
  • Transparency is now law, not courtesy. Under the EU AI Act, are our patient-facing AI interactions clearly disclosed as AI, and our AI-generated content labeled — and can our books on any agent touching patient care survive an audit?
🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
  • You price a plant, a line, a fleet by payback and utilization, never by the supplier's valuation. Are we applying that same barn-not-blossom rigor to agent platforms — payback period, realized throughput, residual value — rather than buying the growth narrative?
  • A 40×-priced vendor is a durability question. If a core automation supplier is unprofitable and priced for perfection, what is our continuity plan for the line if their season turns?
🛒 Retail / Consumer
  • Agent economics live or die at volume. After the per-transaction agent cost, is our margin per order still positive at peak — or does the blossom-priced tool quietly eat the sale?
  • Consumer disclosure is now mandatory. Do our AI chat and voice agents disclose they are AI at the start of the interaction, and is AI-generated marketing content labeled — before a regulator or a customer makes the point for us?
🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
  • Public money is being asked to buy agents from richly-valued, sometimes-unprofitable vendors. Are we procuring for the fruit — verifiable outcomes, competition, exit rights — or paying the blossom price on a promise with citizens' money?
  • Sovereignty and trust are now procurement clauses. With the EU AI Act live and the US gating frontier-model access, do we have the transparency, audit rights and alternatives to stay accountable if our chosen vendor is repriced, restricted, or acquired?

4 · Technical Deep-Dive — Price the Fruit, Not the Blossom

Read the stack as three layers, priced in three very different ways. At the roots is the brain — the rented, commoditizing model, cheaper every week. Just above it is the plumbing — the standardizing rails agents run on (MCP and friends), which are becoming free infrastructure. And at the top, in full bloom, are the agents — the layer the market is pricing at 40× forward revenue on the strength of its flower. The engineering point is that value and price are moving in opposite directions down the tree: the roots and rails are commoditizing toward zero, while the blossom is being bid toward the sky. The board's job is to price the top layer by the fruit the bottom layers can actually produce.

  • The roots — the brain, commoditizing. Opus 5, GPT-5.6, Gemini 3.6 Flash, and open-weight leaders — Kimi K3 sitting at the top of open leaderboards, with DeepSeek V4 Flash quoting roughly $0.14 per million input / $0.28 per million output tokens as the price floor keeps falling. The raw brain is cheap, swappable, and getting cheaper. It is not where the $40B is.
  • The rails — the plumbing, standardizing. The Model Context Protocol's 2026-07-28 specification went stateless (dropping the initialize handshake and session IDs so any server instance can answer behind ordinary HTTP), added cacheable list results and multi-round-trip requests, hardened authorization, and stabilized Enterprise-Managed Authorization for SSO across connected servers. Adoption is now infrastructure-scale: ~500M SDK downloads a month, with the TypeScript and Python SDKs each past 1 billion total. Rails this standard do not command a premium; they enable one above them.
  • The blossom — the agents, priced for the harvest. This is where the money is. Cognition/Devin in talks at ≥$40B (implied ~40× forward revenue, doubled in a quarter); Salesforce Agentforce at ~$1.2B ARR, +205% YoY (Agentforce + Data 360 ~$3.4B; 3.8B agentic work units; 28.6T tokens processed, as of Q1 FY27); Microsoft's AI run-rate ~$37B with roughly one in three GitHub pull requests now involving an agent (as reported). The agent layer is being valued as though the harvest is already in the barn.

The strategic core: the roots and rails are becoming free; the blossom is being priced as if the fruit is guaranteed. For a month the misread was "buy the cleverest model and you have deployed AI." The sharper read this week is: you are not buying a model, you are buying an agent — and the market is pricing that agent by its bloom. "It grew 200% and tops the benchmark" is not the answer to "what did it actually harvest for us, what does it cost us if its valuation resets, and can we audit and disclose its output"; "we priced it by realized value per workload, by vendor durability, and by provable trust" is the answer.

Why pricing the fruit beats chasing the bloom

Every layer of the stack this month presumed the brain was cheap and swappable — and it is; the roots and rails are commoditizing toward zero. That is precisely why the money, and the mispricing, have moved to the agent on top. A cleverer model does nothing about a 40× forward multiple you have embedded in a core workflow. The reason blossom and fruit belong in the same picture is that they are the same tree seen in two seasons: the bloom is the growth story the market pays for in spring; the fruit is the audited value your own business books in autumn — and only the second one is money. Read them together and you can answer the CFO's real question. The correct read of this week is not "an AI startup is worth $40B" but "agents are being priced by the blossom — so bring harvest discipline, or pay for flowers."

How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — be deliberate about what you own, rent and finance. On legacy estates the danger is buying agents the way the market prices them: on the bloom. The retrofit is an underwriting exercise. Build a one-page "harvest sheet" for every agent — vendor-bought and internally-built — recording the fruit, not the flower: realized value per workload, cost per successful task, production reliability (not demo pass-rate), and payback period. Price vendor durability: for any agent supplier priced at a steep forward multiple, model the reset — funding tightening, a down round, an acquisition — and the cost to your workflow if their season turns. Price your exit and switching cost, because a "swappable" agent is sticky once it is wired into production. And wire trust in from the start — the EU AI Act's disclosure and labeling duties are live, the AITSC is forming the enterprise standard, and an agent whose sessions you cannot audit or whose output you cannot label is not deployable at board scale regardless of its benchmark. Keep the roots swappable underneath — re-benchmark Kimi K3, DeepSeek V4 Flash and the next open-weight release freely — because the brain is a commodity and the agent is the priced, sticky, trust-bound commitment.

The clean mental model: The brain is the roots — cheap, swappable, cheaper every quarter. The rails are the plumbing — MCP and its kin, standardizing toward free. The agent is the blossom — the layer the market prices at 40× forward on the strength of its flower. Blossom is growth, demo and TAM; fruit is audited value in your barn, sold and paid for, and provable under the trust rules now live. Price the fruit, not the blossom — realized value per workload, vendor durability, and trust you can defend — or pay the blossom price and own a beautiful spring and an empty barn.

7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

  1. Build a "harvest sheet" for every agent — price the fruit, not the blossom. For each material AI agent you buy or build, put one page in front of the board that records the harvest, not the flower: realized business value per workload, cost per successful task, production reliability (not demo pass-rate), and payback period. Report a single number: what share of our AI spend is tied to an audited, repeatable return? PwC's 56%-no-ROI figure is what an org looks like when nobody keeps a harvest sheet — and the size of the "unmeasured" pile is the wake-up call.
  2. Underwrite vendor durability as coldly as any 40×-forward counterparty. For every agent supplier priced for perfection, model the reset: a down round, tighter funding, an acquisition, a repricing. Answer three questions — our exposure (how deeply is this vendor wired into core flows?), our exit (switching cost, egress, re-integration — the stickiness behind a "swappable" agent), and our continuity (what breaks, and how fast can we recover, if their season turns?). Avoid concentrating a critical workflow on a single, richly-valued, cash-burning counterparty without the covenants you'd demand anywhere else.
  3. Wire trust in now — it is part of the return, and it is already law. The trust gate is live: turn on the audit trail over agent sessions (compliance/eDiscovery), disclose AI to customers where the EU AI Act now requires it, and label AI-generated content machine-readably. Track the AITSC standard as it forms and adopt what fits. Treat provable trust not as overhead but as part of the fruit — because an agent you cannot audit, disclose or defend cannot be deployed at board scale, and its benchmark score will not save you in front of a regulator or an auditor.
