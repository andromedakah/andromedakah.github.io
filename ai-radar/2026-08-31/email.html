The Census

Monday, 31 August 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

All week the radar built a workforce of agents — one door, owned knowledge, an identity, an open tongue, a conductor, an ear, a repertoire. This morning the board hits the question that comes after: how many of these digital workers do we actually employ — and can we name a single one? Most cannot. Gartner projects 150,000+ agents per Fortune 500 by 2028 (from fewer than 15 in 2025), reframed this week (SAP, CIO) as "agent sprawl is now a board-level issue." Only ~13% believe their governance is right, and fewer than half can inventory the agents they run (SAP LeanIX). If we are about to employ a hundred thousand tireless digital workers, do we have a census — or are we running a city whose population we have never counted?

1 · Executive Summary

For a week this radar assembled the parts of an agentic workforce. This morning the governing question is no longer how do I build one agent well? It is "now that every team can spin up agents faster than anyone can track them, do I even know how many I have, who owns them, and what they are allowed to touch?" The answer, across this week's board-level coverage, is uncomfortable: most enterprises are scaling the agent population far faster than they are counting or governing it.

1. The population — uncounted. Gartner projects the average Fortune 500 enterprise will run 150,000+ AI agents by 2028 (up from fewer than 15 in 2025) — recirculated this week (SAP, CIO, Reworked) as a governance alarm. Only ~13% believe their agent governance is right; per SAP LeanIX, fewer than half can produce an inventory of the agents they already run. Gartner's own tell: "organizations don't intentionally create agent sprawl… it is an after-effect of successful AI adoption."

2. The register — the fix everyone is building. By mid-2026 Microsoft, AWS and Google Cloud had each shipped agent registry, identity, governance and lifecycle tooling — a single source of truth for every agent, sanctioned, third-party and shadow. Okta ("AI Agents at Work 2026," 200+ executives): 91% of organizations use agents, but only ~10% have a mature strategy for the non-human identities they carry. You cannot govern what you have not first counted and named.

3. The bill — why the board cares now. A three-way squeeze (SAP): the workforce wants more permissions, the CFO says token spend is too high, the CEO wants results. Google Cloud's ROI report: 74% of production deployers claim first-year ROI, yet ~22% of deployments show negative ROI at 12 months and only ~12% of CEOs report both revenue gain and cost reduction. An uncounted agent is an uncounted cost — and an uncounted liability.

Bottom line: the week built the workforce; this morning the board must count it. When an enterprise is heading toward 150,000 agents it cannot yet inventory, "we're scaling agents fast" is not a strategy — it is an unmeasured liability, because you cannot govern, budget for, audit, or stand behind a workforce you have never counted. Count before you scale: take the census, build the register — every agent named, owned, scoped and retirable — because the sprawl is coming whether or not you are counting.

2 · Allegory — The Census

There was a city that fell in love with hiring. Its workers were tireless — they never slept, never tired, and could be conjured by any household that wanted one — so quarter by quarter every household hired more. "We are the fastest-growing city in the province," the mayor boasted. But one autumn the treasurer made a terrible admission: asked how many workers the city now employed, she could not say. Each household had hired on its own, and no one had ever kept a roll. There was no register of who worked where, who owned them, which doors their keys opened, or which had finished their task months ago and simply kept drawing pay. The city had a hundred thousand workers and could not name one.

And the danger stopped being abstract. A worker no one remembered hiring still held a key to the treasury; a task finished in spring still had three workers on it in autumn, costing wages no one budgeted; and when something went wrong, no one could say which worker did it or who was answerable. The city was not badly run; it was uncounted — and an uncounted city cannot be governed, taxed honestly, or answer for itself. The households that panicked froze all hiring and watched the work stop. The ones that kept their footing did the dull, decisive thing: they took the census — walked every ward and wrote down every worker, a name, an owner, the doors its key opened, its task, and the date to let it go — and ruled that no new worker could start until it was on the roll with an owner. They hired more than the panickers, not fewer; they simply never again let a worker exist that the roll did not know.

The question it forces: we are about to employ a hundred thousand tireless digital workers. So — how many do we run right now? Can we produce, this afternoon, a single roll that names every agent, the human who owns it, the systems its credentials can reach, and the date it should be retired? If we cannot, we are not governing a workforce — we are hoping one governs itself. Are we counting our agents, or only admiring how fast they multiply?

3 · Technical — Counting the Digital Workforce Before It Counts You

The population: Gartner — ~150,000+ agents per Fortune 500 enterprise by 2028 (from <15 in 2025); ~40% of enterprise apps ship a task-specific agent by end of 2026 (from <5%); Microsoft — ~80% of the Fortune 500 already run active agents. Creation is democratized to every team and app, so the growth is decentralized — which is why no single team holds the roll. The bill: a three-way squeeze (permissions vs. token spend vs. results); Google Cloud ROI — 74% claim first-year ROI, ~22% of deployments negative at 12 months, ~12% of CEOs see both gain and cut. The uncounted agent is the one running up token spend on a finished task and holding a key no one watches.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Take the census. Stand up one source of truth listing every agent — sanctioned, third-party and shadow — mapped to a human owner, the systems and data its credentials can reach, and the task it was created for. Reconcile it against reality by walking the estate. Report one number to the board: how many agents we actually run today, and how many we could name an owner for. If the honest answer is "we don't know," that is the finding.

2. Make registration a gate. Require that every new agent be registered, owned, and scoped to least privilege before it can touch production — treat an unregistered agent like an unbadged employee with a master key. Wire identity and Article-12 logging into every agent so any action resolves to a named agent and a named human. The register is only real if nothing can exist outside it.

3. Prune the roll. Assign a lifecycle owner, review the register on a schedule, and retire or de-provision agents that finished their work or went idle — and measure per-agent cost and value so negative-ROI agents get pruned, not accumulated. Report two numbers next quarter: the share of agents on a governed register with a named owner, and the share you retired this quarter.
