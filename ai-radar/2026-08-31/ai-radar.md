## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Census"

*Topic: This week the enterprise-AI conversation pivoted from building agents to governing the sheer number of them. Gartner's forecast that the average Global Fortune 500 enterprise will run more than 150,000 AI agents by 2028 (up from fewer than 15 in 2025) was recirculated across the trade press (SAP News, CIO, Reworked) as the framing "AI agent sprawl is now a board-level issue." The alarm is a counting gap: only about 13% of organizations believe they have the right agent governance in place, and per an SAP LeanIX survey fewer than half can even produce an inventory of the agents they already run. In response, Microsoft, AWS and Google Cloud have each shipped agent registry, identity and lifecycle tooling; Okta's "AI Agents at Work 2026" report finds 91% of organizations using agents but only ~10% with a mature strategy for the non-human identities they carry. This follows the week's editions on the gate, the larder, the passport, the lingua franca, the conductor, the ear and the repertoire. The lesson: after you learn to build one agent well, the governing problem becomes counting and accounting for all of them — you cannot govern a workforce you have never counted. The census allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There was a city that fell in love with hiring. Its workers were tireless — they never slept, never tired, and could be conjured by any household that wanted one — and so, quarter by quarter, every household hired more. The mayor was proud of it. "We are the fastest-growing city in the province," he told the council, and the council nodded, because everywhere you looked there was work being done: messages carried, ledgers kept, bargains struck, goods moved through the night.

But one autumn the treasurer came to the council with a simple, terrible admission. Asked how many workers the city now employed, she could not say. Not roughly. Each household had hired on its own — through the front gate, through the back, through a cousin who knew a cousin — and no one had ever kept a roll. There was no register of who worked where, who had hired them, which doors their keys opened, or which of them were still needed and which had finished their task months ago and simply kept drawing breath and pay. The city had a hundred thousand workers and could not name one of them. It had grown too fast to count itself.

And here the danger stopped being abstract. A worker no one remembered hiring still held a key to the treasury. A task finished in spring still had three workers assigned to it in autumn, quietly costing wages no one had budgeted. When something went wrong — a message misdelivered, a ledger altered, a door left open — no one could say which worker had done it, or who was answerable, because no one owned the roll. The city was not badly *run;* it was **uncounted,** and an uncounted city cannot be governed, cannot be taxed honestly, and cannot answer for itself when a magistrate asks who did this.

The households that panicked did the loud thing: they froze all hiring, and watched the work stop. But the households that kept their footing did the dull, decisive thing — they **took the census.** They walked every ward and wrote down every worker: a name, the household that owned it, the doors its key opened, the task it was hired for, and the date it should be let go. They built a single roll that the whole city could read, and a rule that no new worker could start until it was written into the roll and given an owner. They did not hire fewer workers than the panicking households — in the end they hired far more — but they could always answer the treasurer's question, because they never again let a worker exist that the roll did not know.

The moral is not that the workers were the problem — they did prodigious work, and a city that refused to hire them fell behind. It is that **a workforce you have not counted is not an asset; it is an exposure** — an unbudgeted cost, an ungoverned key, an unanswerable question when something breaks. The census is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It is the one thing that lets a city keep hiring at scale *without* losing the ability to govern, pay for, and stand behind the work being done in its name.

**The moral, stated plainly:** the model is a rented stove; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door; the passport names each agent at that door; the lingua franca is the tongue it speaks; the conductor keeps the ensemble in time; the ear hears whether the performance is good; the repertoire is the music only your house can play — and **the census is the roll that knows how many players you have, who owns each, what door their key opens, and when to let them go.** Count the workforce before it counts you. Build the register, assign every agent an owner, scope what it can touch, and retire it when its task is done — because the population is arriving whether or not you are keeping the roll.

**The question it forces:** *We are about to employ a hundred thousand tireless digital workers. So — how many do we run right now? Can we produce, this afternoon, a single roll that names every agent, the human who owns it, the systems its credentials can reach, and the date it should be retired? If we cannot, we are not governing a workforce — we are hoping one governs itself. Are we counting our agents, or only admiring how fast they multiply?*

## 3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

### 🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
- **Can we produce an agent census today?** Gartner projects 150,000+ agents per Fortune 500 by 2028, yet fewer than half of firms can inventory the agents they run now (SAP LeanIX). **If the CEO asked this afternoon "how many agents do we operate, and who owns each," could anyone answer — or would we be a city with no roll?**
- **Is "we're scaling agents fast" an achievement or an exposure?** Only ~13% of organizations believe their agent governance is adequate. **An uncounted agent is an unbudgeted cost and an ungoverned key — so where is our register, who maintains it, and what stops a new agent from existing before it is written into the roll with an owner?**
- **Who is answerable when an agent we can't name does something we can't explain?** The engine is rented; the accountability is not. **When an agent alters a record or opens a door, can we trace it to a named agent, a human owner, and an Article-12 log — or does the trail end at "one of our agents, we're not sure which"?**

### 🏦 Financial Services
- Every unregistered agent with a live credential is model risk, access risk and audit risk in one. **Do we hold a single register mapping each agent to an owner, a scope of accounts and systems it may touch, and a retirement date — or are there agents holding keys to positions and client data that no inventory knows about?**
- The regulator will ask "who authorized this and who is accountable." **Can we answer at the level of a named agent and a named human — with reconstructable logs (Article 12) — or only at the level of "the platform did it"?**

### 🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
- Agents touching patient records, trial data and care pathways cannot be allowed to accumulate uncounted. **Is every clinical or research agent on a governed roll — named, owned, scoped to the minimum data it needs, and retired when its study or workflow ends — or are finished-task agents still holding live access months later?**
- Accountability for a patient-facing decision must resolve to a person. **When an agent acts, can we name the agent, its human owner and its audit trail — or have we deployed a workforce we cannot account for to a regulator or an ethics board?**

### 🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
- Agents on the line, in the supply chain and across OT/IT multiply per site and per team — the definition of sprawl. **Do we run one register across plants, or does every site hire its own untracked workers with keys to control systems no central roll can see?**
- Idle agents are silent cost and silent risk. **Do we know which agents finished their task and should be retired — and who is accountable for pruning the roll — or are we paying wages and holding open doors for workers no one remembers hiring?**

### 🛒 Retail / Consumer
- Agents across storefront, pricing, service and logistics are exactly where sprawl hides behind "it's working." **Can we inventory every customer-facing and back-office agent, its owner and its data scope — or is our agent estate growing faster than our ability to see it, one team's quick win at a time?**
- Token spend and negative-ROI agents drain margin quietly. **With ~22% of agent deployments reportedly showing negative ROI at 12 months, do we measure per-agent value and retire the losers — or does the roll only ever grow?**

### 🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
- Agents acting on citizen records and statutory process must be countable, owned and auditable by definition. **Is there a public-grade register of every agent in service — name, owner, scope, retirement — with Article-12-style reconstructable logs, or are we running ungoverned digital workers over the public's data?**
- Accountability is not delegable to a vendor. **When an agent makes or supports a decision affecting a citizen, can we name the responsible agent and official — or have we lost the roll and, with it, the ability to answer for the state's own actions?**

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — Counting the Digital Workforce Before It Counts You

Read the stack once more as the parts of an agentic workforce this radar has assembled all week — but now step past *building one agent well* to the question every enterprise hits the moment it has built more than a handful: **do you actually know how many you are running, and can you name each one?** The gate admits them; the passport identifies one at the door; the lingua franca lets them speak; the conductor coordinates them; the ear hears their errors; the repertoire is the data they perform. **The census is the layer above all of it — the register that turns an uncounted crowd of agents into a governed workforce.** The engineering point is blunt: **an agent that is not on a register, owned by a human, scoped to least privilege and given a retirement date is not a capability — it is an unmanaged non-human identity with a live credential, and at 150,000 per enterprise that is not an edge case, it is the default.**

- **The population — arriving faster than the headcount.** **Gartner: ~150,000+ AI agents per average Fortune 500 enterprise by 2028, up from fewer than 15 in 2025; ~40% of enterprise apps will ship a task-specific agent by end of 2026 (up from <5% in 2025); Microsoft: ~80% of the Fortune 500 already run active AI agents.** Read architecturally: agent creation has been democratized to every team and every app, so the growth is *organic and decentralized* — which is precisely why no single team has the roll. Treat every agent as a first-class, registered non-human identity from creation, not an afterthought.
- **The register — the control plane everyone is now building.** **Microsoft, AWS and Google Cloud each shipped agent registry / identity / lifecycle tooling by mid-2026; the design pattern is a single source of truth for every agent (sanctioned, third-party and shadow), each mapped to an owner, a permission scope and a lifecycle state.** Okta ("AI Agents at Work 2026," 200+ executives): **91% use agents, but only ~10% have a mature non-human-identity strategy** — and *agentic readiness is identity readiness.* The census is not a spreadsheet; it is enforced provisioning: no agent runs unless it is registered, owned and scoped.
- **The bill and the liability — why the board is counting now.** **SAP frames a three-way squeeze: the workforce demands more agent permissions, the CFO flags token spend, the CEO demands results. Google Cloud ROI report: 74% of production deployers claim first-year ROI, yet ~22% of deployments reportedly show negative ROI at 12 months and only ~12% of CEOs report both revenue gain and cost reduction.** The uncounted agent is the one running up token spend on a finished task and holding a key no one is watching — the census is how you find and retire it.

The strategic core: **when the agent population is heading to six figures per enterprise and fewer than half of firms can inventory what they already run, "we're scaling agents" describes an exposure, not a moat — the durable capability is the register that lets you scale the workforce while still being able to count, own, scope and retire every member of it.** For a week the frame moved from "own the right layer" to "oversee without custody" to "build the gate" to "own the knowledge" to "name the agent" to "speak the open tongue" to "conduct the ensemble" to "keep the ear" to "own the repertoire"; this morning it lands on the discipline that makes all of it governable at scale: **count the workforce.** "We deploy agents everywhere" is not an answer to "how many do you run and who owns them"; ***"every agent is on one register — named, owned, scoped to least privilege, logged to the Article-12 standard, and retired when its task ends"*** is the answer.

### Why "the census" is the artifact that matters

For a year the reflex was to celebrate the *number* of agents as proof of progress — more agents, more automation, more pilots shipped. This week the trade press reframed that number as a liability: **an enterprise heading to 150,000 agents it cannot inventory is not ahead, it is exposed.** What makes the population governable is not another capability layer but a *count* — the register that maps every agent to an owner, a permission scope and a lifecycle. The most valuable move this week is not deploying one more agent; it is being able to answer, honestly and instantly, how many you already run and who is accountable for each.

### How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — **be deliberate about what you run, own and can account for.** On a legacy estate the temptation is to let every team stand up its own agents against the systems they already touch, because it is fast and it works — and that is exactly how sprawl accretes. The retrofit is **count first, then scale. Count:** build one register now, before the population is six figures, and reconcile it against reality — walk the estate and write down every agent, its owner, its credentials and its task. **Enforce:** make registration a gate — no agent runs in production unless it is on the roll, owned, scoped to least privilege, and given a review-and-retire date. **Account:** wire identity and Article-12-style logging into every agent so any action resolves to a named agent and a human, and prune the roll on a schedule so finished-task agents lose their keys. And remember the whole refrain: the model is a rented stove, the knowledge is the larder, the gate is the one door, the passport names each agent, the lingua franca is the score, the conductor keeps time, the ear listens to the real room, the repertoire is the music only your house can play — and *the census is the roll that lets you employ a hundred thousand of them and still answer for every one.*

The census is the roll above all of it — the register that knows how many agents you run, who owns each, what its key opens, and when to let it go. Count the workforce before it counts you.*

## 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. **Take the census — produce one register of every agent you run.** You cannot govern what you have not counted. **This month, stand up a single source of truth that lists every agent — sanctioned, third-party and shadow — mapped to a human owner, the systems and data its credentials can reach, and the task it was created for.** Reconcile it against reality by walking the estate, not by trusting anyone's memory. Report one number to the board: how many agents we actually run today, and how many of them we could name an owner for. If the honest answer is "we don't know," that *is* the finding.
2. **Make registration a gate — no agent runs unless it is on the roll.** Sprawl is what happens when creation is easy and counting is optional. **Flip it: require that every new agent be registered, owned, and scoped to least privilege before it can touch production — treat an unregistered agent the way you would treat an unbadged employee with a master key.** Wire identity and Article-12-style logging into every agent so any action resolves to a named agent and a named human. The register is only real if nothing can exist outside it.
3. **Prune the roll — retire the agents whose task is done.** A census you never update becomes another stale spreadsheet, and a finished-task agent still holding a key is pure downside. **Assign a lifecycle owner, review the register on a schedule, and retire or de-provision agents that have finished their work or gone idle — and measure per-agent cost and value so the negative-ROI agents get pruned, not just accumulated.** Report two numbers next quarter: the share of agents on a governed register with a named owner, and the share you retired this quarter. **The model is rented, the knowledge is the moat, and the census is what lets you employ a hundred thousand digital workers and still answer, instantly, for every one.**
