The Scullery Hand

Saturday, 10 October 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

The cheap model tier just did two things at once — it collapsed in price and, for the first time, got good at real work. So the costly question is no longer "which model?" but "which tier, for which task?" Are we paying a master's wage to scrub our pots? Match the hand to the task.

1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from owning the ground the model stands on to the wage you pay each task — because the cheap tier just collapsed in price and, for the first time, got good at the plain work.

1. The event — the small tier collapsed in price and got good. On 7 October 2026 Anthropic shipped Claude Haiku 5.5 — "our fastest, cheapest, and most capable small model yet… designed for high-volume, cost-sensitive work" (Anthropic, primary) — at a reported $0.10 / $0.50 per million input/output tokens for prompts ≤100K, about one-tenth of Haiku 4.5's $1 / $5, with vendor-reported computer-use (OSWorld 2.1 offline) leaping 15.7% → 72.4%. The cheap hand finally got good at the plain work.

2. The shift — the costly question is the tier, not the model. Most enterprise token-volume is routine, high-volume work (classify, extract, route, first-draft); running it on a frontier model is paying a master's wage for scullery work. And the AI bill is now a board number: Gartner puts 2026 worldwide AI spend at $2.59 trillion, up 47% (19 May 2026, as reported).

3. The answer — match the hand to the task. Tier your work (scullery vs master), route each to the cheapest capable tier, and measure cost-per-useful-task, not the per-token sticker — honest that "~75% cheaper" is blended (a new tokenizer counts ~30% more tokens) and the benchmarks are vendor-reported.

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each improved the servant or the ground it stands on — the scullery hand is about the wage you pay each task. The stove-seller handed the kitchen a cheap, fast hand (a tenth of last year's wage) finally skilled enough for the plain work. Hire the master for mastery — but never pay a master's wage to scrub a pot.

2 · Allegory — The Scullery Hand

A great house had done everything right by its servants — named, booked, bonded, waged, sent to reap, funded with a scoped purse, and at last learned to own the very ground the kitchen stood on. But the master had a habit he had never questioned: whatever the task — carving the feast-day roast or scrubbing the breakfast pots — he sent his finest cook to do it, and paid a master's wage for every hour of it, and thought himself well served because the pots were very clean.

This week a hiring-agent brought a scullery hand — young, quick, tireless, asking roughly a tenth of the master cook's wage. The master had turned such hands away before, because in years past the cheap hands scorched the simple things and had to be done over. But this one did the plain, high-volume work not passably but well — where last year's cheap hands got it right scarcely one time in six, this one got the ordinary tasks right most of the time, at a tenth of the wage. The cheap hand had, at last, become good enough for the plain work.

So the wise houses learned three things: sort the work (scullery vs master, each to the least costly hand that does it well); weigh the true cost, not the sticker (the hand's wage was quoted by a new clock that ticked faster, so a "tenth the wage" was nearer a quarter saved — pay by the finished dish); and don't take the agent's word for the skill (try the hand on your own pots, at the effort you will actually use, before you trust it with dinner). This is the mirror of last month: there, don't let the cheap stove pour your foundation; here, don't run the whole estate on a master's wage.

The question it forces: The cheap hand just got good — a tenth of last year's wage, finally skilled for the plain work. Are we matching the hand to the task, or paying a master's wage to scrub our pots? Have we sorted our AI work into scullery and master, routed each to the cheapest hand that does it well across a floor we own, and do we pay by the finished task — or are we running the whole estate on our most expensive model and calling the clean pots a success?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Scullery Hand

Every prior control governs what the agent is, may do, serves, produces or stands on; the scullery hand is about the wage you pay each task. The thing that changed is not that the frontier got better — it is that the bottom got cheap and capable at once, so the expensive mistake is running routine work on a premium model, and the fix is routing, not model-shopping. The event: Haiku 5.5 at a reported $0.10/$0.50 per M tokens (~1/10) with computer-use 15.7%→72.4% — the cheap tier is now good enough for the mass of real work. The caveats: "~75% cheaper" is blended (a new tokenizer counts ~30% more tokens; the API default is medium effort; no independent lab yet), so measure the bill, not the sticker, and prove it on your own tasks. The answer: tier your work (scullery vs master), route each to the cheapest capable tier with escalation to the frontier only when needed, and measure cost-per-useful-task on the abstraction you already own.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Sort the work. The small tier just fell ~tenfold and got capable (Haiku 5.5: ~$0.10/$0.50 per M; computer-use 15.7%→72.4%), so routine work no longer belongs on a frontier model. Inventory your top AI workloads and classify each task as scullery (high-volume, verifiable) or master (judgement, difficulty). Report the share of your AI token-volume that is scullery work still routed to a frontier-priced model.

2. Route it — and measure the bill, not the sticker. The dividend is captured only by routing, and the honest cost is fully-loaded (the "~75% cheaper" headline is blended; the API defaults to medium effort; the tokenizer counts ~30% more tokens). Route one high-volume workload to the cheap tier behind the abstraction you own, escalate hard cases to the frontier, and prove quality on your own tasks. Report before/after cost-per-task and the quality delta.

3. Keep the master for mastery. Over-provisioning is the default waste, and with AI spend at $2.59T in 2026 (+47%) the leak compounds. Set a routing default of "cheapest capable tier, escalate on need," make model-tier choice a reviewed decision per workload, and schedule a quarterly re-tiering as prices and capabilities move. Report the projected annual saving from right-sizing your top workloads.
