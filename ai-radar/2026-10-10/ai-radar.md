**Saturday, 10 October 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering**

> You spent a long year learning that the model is a cheap, swappable stove, and that the wise estate owns the floor, keeps the larder and lights a second fire. This morning the stove-seller does something sharper than drop the stove's price — it hands you a **scullery hand**. On **7 October 2026 Anthropic shipped Claude Haiku 5.5**, which it calls "our fastest, cheapest, and most capable small model yet… designed for high-volume, cost-sensitive work" (Anthropic, primary). Gartner puts 2026 worldwide AI spend at **$2.59 trillion, up 47%** (Gartner, 19 May 2026): the estate that routes the bulk of that spend to the cheapest capable hand, and reserves the master only for mastery, keeps far more of it.

**The event — the small tier collapsed in price and, for the first time, got good.** On **7 October 2026 Anthropic released Claude Haiku 5.5**, "our fastest, cheapest, and most capable small model yet… designed for high-volume, cost-sensitive work" (Anthropic, primary). **The shift — the expensive question moved from "which model?" to "which tier, for which task?"** When intelligence was scarce and dear, you sent every task to the best brain you could afford. Now the brain is cheap at the bottom and the bottom is capable: the mistake is running the whole estate on a master's wage — routing routine, high-volume work (classification, extraction, routing, retrieval, first-pass drafting) to a frontier model when a hand at a tenth of the cost does it well. This is where the spend actually is. Gartner forecasts **worldwide AI spending of $2.59 trillion in 2026, up 47% year-over-year, rising toward ~$3.49 trillion in 2027**, with AI infrastructure the single largest segment (Gartner, 19 May 2026, as reported). The estate's AI bill is now a board-level line, and most of it rides on plain, repeatable tasks.

## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Scullery Hand"

*Topic: The series moved from the servant to the ground the house stands on; this morning it turns to the wage paid for each task. On 7 October 2026 Anthropic shipped Claude Haiku 5.5 — "our fastest, cheapest, and most capable small model yet… designed for high-volume, cost-sensitive work" (Anthropic, primary) — at a reported $0.10/$0.50 per million tokens, roughly a tenth of the prior small model, with the cheap tier's computer-use score leaping from 15.7% to 72.4% (vendor-reported, as reported). Gartner puts 2026 worldwide AI spend at $2.59 trillion, up 47% (19 May 2026, as reported). The honest caveats — a blended "~75% cheaper," a new tokenizer that counts ~30% more tokens, vendor-reported benchmarks — are kept in view. The scullery-hand allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There was a great house that had, over a long and careful year, done everything right by its servants. It had named every hand and cut it a scoped key, kept one honest book, put the master's own name on the bond, asked whose second coin each advising servant took, sent the governed servants out to reap, handed each a scoped purse, and learned at last to look down and own the very ground the kitchen stood on. The master was, justly, proud. And the kitchen cooked on a rented stove that grew cheaper and better every season — his shrewdest economy.

Now, the master had a habit he had never thought to question. **Whatever the task — be it carving the feast-day roast or merely scrubbing the breakfast pots — he sent his finest cook to do it.** His finest cook was a marvel, and expensive, and the master reasoned, not unreasonably, that the best hand could do any work best. So the master cook spent her morning scrubbing pots, fetching water, sorting lentils, and copying out the day's orders in a fair hand — and the master paid a master's wage for every hour of it, and thought himself well served because the pots were, indeed, very clean.

This week a hiring-agent came to the gate with a **scullery hand** — young, quick, tireless, and astonishingly cheap, asking roughly **a tenth** of the master cook's wage. The master had turned such hands away before, for a simple reason: in years past the cheap hands could not be trusted with real work. They scorched the simple things and had to be done over, and a hand you must follow and correct is dearer than one you pay well. But the agent said, *"Try this one at the plain work — the scrubbing, the sorting, the fetching, the fair copying."* And to the master's surprise, the scullery hand did the plain, high-volume work of the kitchen not passably but *well* — where last year's cheap hands had got it right scarcely one time in six, this one got the ordinary tasks right most of the time, and at a tenth of the wage. The cheap hand had, at last, become good enough for the plain work.

Here is where two roads opened, as they always do. The easy road — the road the proud, well-run house was already on — was to shrug and keep sending the master cook to scrub the pots, because she was the best and the house could afford her and changing the arrangement was a bother. The wiser road began with a cold question the master had never asked of his own kitchen: *not "who is my best cook?" but "for each task in this kitchen, what is the least skilled hand that can do it well — and am I paying a master's wage for scullery work?"* And when the careful houses counted honestly, they found the answer expensive: the great bulk of a kitchen's labour is plain, repeatable work — scrubbing, sorting, fetching, fair copies — and they had been paying their very best, at a master's wage, to do nearly all of it. The whole valley's kitchen-bill was swelling by near half again this year; most of that coin was being spent on exactly the plain work a cheap, capable hand could now do.

So the wise houses learned three plain things, and each saved more than the last. **The first: sort the work before you assign it.** Divide the day's tasks into scullery work — plain, high-volume, easy to check — and master's work — the carving, the saucing, the judgement calls — and send each to the least costly hand that does it well. **The second: weigh the true cost of the hand, not the sticker on the door.** A cheap hand that scorches the dish and must be done over is dearer than it looks; and this particular hand's wage, the master noticed, was quoted cleverly — it counted its hours by a new clock that ticked faster, so a "tenth the wage" was nearer a quarter saved once the day's bill was tallied honestly. A wise master pays by the finished dish, not the advertised rate, and checks the bill himself. **And the third: do not take the hiring-agent's word for the hand's skill.** The agent's own testimonials glowed, but no neighbour had yet watched the hand work a full week; so the wise house tried it on its own pots, at the ordinary effort it would actually use day to day, before it trusted it with the dinner.

And here is the part that should steady a master's nerve, because it is the mirror of last month's warning. **Last month the danger was letting the cheap stove quietly pour the foundation you could not leave; this month the danger is the plain, daily waste of running the whole estate on a master's wage.** They are the two faces of the same coin: when intelligence is cheap, the house that thrives is not the one that always buys the best, nor the one that always buys the cheapest, but the one that **matches the hand to the task** — hires the master for mastery, sets the scullery hand to the scullery, keeps the floor it routes them across in its own hands, and pays, always, by the finished work. The proud house that sends its master cook to scrub every pot will have the cleanest pots in the valley and the thinnest purse, and will never understand why.

**The moral, stated plainly:** the model is a rented stove, now cheap enough that a cheap, capable hand comes with it; your knowledge is the larder; the foundation is the ground the house stands on; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks for the roll; the keyring is which key each holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery names every hand; the steward keeps one book; the surety puts your name on the bond; the second wage asks whose bread it eats; the two harvests sends it to reap; the purse funds it like an employee; the foundation keeps the ground yours — and **the scullery hand is why, now that the cheap tier is at last skilled enough to do the plain work, you must stop paying a master's wage for scullery work: sort the day's tasks, send each to the least costly hand that does it well, pay by the finished dish and not the advertised rate, and hire the master only for mastery.** A house that sends its finest cook to scrub every pot has not been thorough; it has simply never counted what the scrubbing costs.

**The question it forces:** *The cheap hand just got good — a tenth of last year's wage, and finally skilled enough for the plain work. So: are we matching the hand to the task, or paying a master's wage to scrub our pots? Have we sorted our AI work into scullery and master, routed each to the cheapest hand that does it well across a floor we own, and do we pay by the finished task — honest about the new clock and the hiring-agent's glowing testimonials — or are we running the whole estate on our most expensive model and calling the clean pots a success?*

## 3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

### 🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
- **Are we paying a master's wage for scullery work?** The small tier just fell ~tenfold in price and got genuinely capable (Haiku 5.5, 7 Oct: a reported $0.10/$0.50 per M tokens; computer-use 15.7%→72.4%, vendor-reported). **For our top few AI workloads, can we show what fraction of token-volume is plain, high-volume, verifiable "scullery" work still being routed to a frontier-priced model — and what moving it to the cheapest capable tier would save?**
- **Do we measure cost-per-useful-task, or per-token sticker price?** The headline "~75% cheaper" is blended — a new tokenizer counts ~30% more tokens, so the per-task saving is smaller than the per-token cut. **Do we track the fully-loaded cost of a completed task (including retries and the tokenizer), or are we comparing advertised rates on a slide?**
- **Have we built the routing, or just picked a model?** With AI spend at $2.59T in 2026, up 47% (Gartner), tiering is now a margin lever. **Is there an abstraction/gateway we own that routes each task to the right tier and escalates only genuine difficulty to the frontier — or does every request hit the same expensive model by default?**

### 🏦 Financial Services
- High-volume, well-specified work (transaction classification, KYC document extraction, first-pass dispute triage, reconciliation) is the archetypal scullery task. **Have we quantified how much of our AI inference bill rides on such tasks, and modelled routing them to a small, cheap tier with the frontier reserved for judgement calls — under the same model-risk and validation controls?**
- A new, vendor-reported model is not a validated one. **Before we route regulated workflows to the cheaper tier, do we re-run our own validation at the effort level we will actually use in production (the default is medium, below the max-effort launch benchmarks), rather than trusting the launch table?**

### 🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
- Much clinical and operational AI work is high-volume triage, coding, and summarisation — plain work where a cheap, capable tier now excels. **Can we tier our clinical-adjacent tasks so routine, verifiable work runs on the cheap hand while diagnostic-grade judgement stays on the frontier — and is each tier validated for its specific use, not assumed from the headline?**
- Cost-sensitive, high-volume settings are where a tenfold price cut matters most. **Does switching the bulk of our documentation and intake workload to the small tier free budget for the harder clinical problems — and have we proven the cheap tier's quality on our own data before trusting it with patients?**

### 🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
- You already match the tool to the job on the shop floor; AI is the same discipline. **Have we applied right-sizing to our AI stack — a cheap, fast model for high-volume sensor-log parsing, inventory and first-pass quality notes, with the frontier reserved for genuinely hard planning — or are we over-provisioning model capability like buying a precision machine to drive a nail?**
- Agentic, computer-use work (navigating interfaces, filling systems) just got cheap and capable (OSWorld 15.7%→72.4%, vendor-reported). **Where we automate routine system-to-system tasks, can the cheap tier now do them at a fraction of the cost — and have we tested it on our actual interfaces?**

### 🛒 Retail / Consumer
- Your margins live and die on cost-per-interaction, and the bulk of retail AI work (product tagging, review triage, search, first-line support) is scullery work. **Are we routing that high-volume work to the cheapest capable tier behind an abstraction we own — and reserving the frontier for the hard, high-value conversations — or paying premium rates for routine traffic?**
- Peak-season volume multiplies any per-task overpayment. **If we moved routine support and catalogue work to the small tier before the peak, what would it save at peak volume — and have we load-tested the cheap tier's quality so savings don't cost us the customer experience?**

### 🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
- Public budgets make overpaying for routine automation a stewardship failure. **Can we show that high-volume, low-complexity public-service tasks (form processing, routing, FOIA triage) run on the most cost-effective capable tier, with the frontier reserved for genuine complexity — and that the choice is documented and defensible?**
- Procurement must right-size, not gold-plate. **Are we procuring AI capability tiered to the task (a cheap tier for the mass of work, a frontier tier for the hard cases), on a floor we own — under the duties already owed (EU AI Act high-risk regime, live since 2 August 2026) — or buying one expensive model for everything?**

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Foundation to the Scullery Hand

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking *is this agent named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and standing on ground I own?* and started asking the question that decides the AI bill: *for each task, what is the least costly hand that does it well — and am I paying a master's wage for scullery work?* Every prior control governs what the agent is, may do, serves, produces, or stands on. **The scullery hand is about the wage you pay each task — and this week the market made the question unavoidable, because the cheap tier both collapsed in price and, for the first time, got good.** The point is blunt: **the thing that changed is not that the frontier got better; it is that the bottom got cheap and capable at once, so the expensive mistake is now running routine work on a premium model — and the fix is routing, not model-shopping.**

- **The event — the small tier collapsed in price and crossed into real capability.** **On 7 October 2026 Anthropic released Claude Haiku 5.5, "our fastest, cheapest, and most capable small model yet… designed for high-volume, cost-sensitive work" (Anthropic, primary) — a reported $0.10 / $0.50 per million input/output tokens for prompts up to 100K (about one-tenth of Haiku 4.5's $1 / $5; $0.50 / $2.50 above 100K; batch ~$0.05 / $0.25; cache reads ~$0.01), a 1M-token context, and vendor-reported capability jumps where agents live: OSWorld 2.1 offline computer-use 15.7% → 72.4%, SWE-bench Multilingual 67.4% → 83.7% (Anthropic launch table, via DataScience/ComputingForGeeks/VKTR, as reported).** Three engineering realities follow. First, **the cheap tier is now good enough for the mass of real work** — the computer-use leap (15.7%→72.4%) is the signal: last year's small model failed routine agentic tasks ~5 times in 6; this one largely succeeds, so the economic case for keeping routine work on a frontier model has collapsed. The strategic core: **the cheap tier getting both cheaper and capable is good news you only collect by routing — tier the work, send the scullery work to the cheap hand, keep the master for mastery, and pay by the finished task.** The market makes the temptation concrete: it is easy and safe-feeling to route everything to the best model and never think about it again, and it feels like fiddly engineering to tier and route — so most houses quietly run their whole estate on a master's wage. The board translation: **hire the master for mastery; never pay a master's wage to scrub a pot.** The failure mode is exact: **a house that names, governs, funds, perfects and grounds its agents — and then routes every routine task to a frontier model while congratulating itself on quality — will pass every governance review and discover, when the AI bill lands, that it has been paying its finest cook, by the hour, to scrub the breakfast pots.**

## 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. **Sort the work — tier every AI task into "scullery" and "master."** The small tier just fell ~tenfold and got genuinely capable (Haiku 5.5, 7 Oct: a reported $0.10/$0.50 per M tokens; computer-use 15.7%→72.4%), so the mass of routine work no longer belongs on a frontier model. **This month, inventory your top AI workloads and classify each task as scullery (high-volume, well-specified, cheaply verifiable) or master (ambiguity, judgement, genuine difficulty).** Report one number to the board: the share of your AI token-volume that is scullery work still being routed to a frontier-priced model.
2. **Route it — send scullery work to the cheapest capable tier, and measure the bill, not the sticker.** The dividend is captured only by routing, and the honest cost is fully-loaded (the "~75% cheaper" headline is blended; the API defaults to medium effort; the tokenizer counts ~30% more tokens). **This month, route one high-volume workload to the cheap tier behind the abstraction you already own, with explicit escalation to the frontier for hard cases, and prove the quality on your own tasks at the effort you will actually run — then measure cost-per-useful-task, retries included.** Report the measured before/after cost-per-task and the quality delta.
3. **Keep the master for mastery — and make tiering a standing discipline.** Over-provisioning is the default waste; a single premium default is how a kitchen pays a master's wage to scrub every pot, and with AI spend at $2.59T in 2026 (+47%, Gartner) the leak compounds. **This month, set a routing default of "cheapest capable tier, escalate on need," make model-tier choice a reviewed decision per workload, and schedule a quarterly re-tiering as prices and capabilities move.** Report the projected annual saving from right-sizing your top workloads, and a dated plan to extend tiering to the next three. **The model is a rented stove; the knowledge is the larder; the foundation is the ground; the census is the roll; the inspector asks for it; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay; the provenance is the papers; the errand bounds the reach; the bell discloses the slip; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is the honest outside light; the livery names every hand; the steward keeps one book; the surety is your name on the bond; the second wage is whose bread it eats; the two harvests sends it to reap; the purse funds it; the foundation keeps the ground yours — and the scullery hand is why, now that the cheap tier is skilled enough to do the plain work, you must match the hand to the task: sort the work, route each to the cheapest hand that does it well, pay by the finished dish, and hire the master only for mastery.**
