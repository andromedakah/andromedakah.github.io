The Turnkey Household

Sunday, 11 October 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

The agent-governance disciplines the radar spent a year arguing boards must build by hand just shipped as one product: at Gemini at Work (8 Oct) Google Cloud launched a single universal enterprise agent with per-agent attested identity, action-level audit, a one-policy "Agent Gateway," real-time spend caps and Smart Routing. It vindicates the thesis — and perfects the lock-in. Take the drilled staff; keep the roll, the book and the door.

1 · Executive Summary

For a year the radar built the governed house by hand — name every agent (the livery), count and credential them (the census, the passport), log every act (the bell), guard one gate, cap each purse, keep the ground your own (the foundation). This week a vendor shipped almost the whole curriculum as one turnkey product — the strongest validation the thesis has had, and the sharpest new version of the trap.

1. The event — governance you were told to build now ships as a product. On 8 October 2026, at Gemini at Work, Google Cloud (CEO Thomas Kurian) launched a single universal "Gemini" enterprise agent (chat, knowledge work, media, code). It arrives with each agent getting its own cryptographically attested identity (coworker agents get their own Workspace identity/email, acting as themselves), every action logged and tied to the agent not the person, an "Agent Gateway" Google calls an "AI network firewall" applying one policy to every agent, isolated sandboxes, real-time spend caps and Smart Routing to the cheapest capable model (Google Cloud; Futurum; VentureBeat; TechCrunch; The Register, as reported). The livery, passport, bell, gate and purse — move-in ready.

2. The collision — turnkey is the stickiest foundation. The least-portable facts about your estate — who your agents are (identity), what they did (audit), what they may touch (policy) — now live inside one vendor's console. Testers On, Shopify, PayPal named; ~$21–$30/seat plus pay-as-you-go reported (TechCrunch; coworker.ai, as reported). Last week: don't let the stove-seller pour your foundation; this week: the stove-seller offers to staff the whole house.

3. The answer — keep the roll, the book and the door. Keep an exportable agent registry you own; stream the per-agent audit to your own store; put a portable seam (MCP) or gateway you control in front, and test re-homing one workflow — the MCP 2026-07-28 revision and the late-August roadmap's agent-identity priority are the portable rails (Model Context Protocol; agentgateway.dev, as reported).

Bottom line: the livery, census, passport, bell, gate, purse and foundation were a year of disciplines a house was told to build by hand. This week a vendor shipped the whole staffed household at once — and it is the most elegant lock-in yet, because it arrives as relief. Take the drilled staff; they are better than what you had. Just keep the roll (who your agents are), the book (what they did) and the door (a seam you own) in your own hand, so the house stays the house's, not the agency's.

2 · Allegory — The Turnkey Household

A great house had spent a careful year learning to run its own servants — each in named livery with a scoped key, a roll of every hand, papers to show at the door, one honest book of every errand, a single manned gate, a capped purse with a drawstring the master could reach. Hard, unglamorous work, done well. Then a grand staffing agency arrived with an offer that felt like rescue: "Do not trouble yourself. We supply the whole staff — already named, liveried and papered, each with a purse you may cap, all passing through one gate our porter mans, and we keep the book of everything they do. They start tomorrow." And the staff were superb — better-drilled than the house's own.

The easy road was to sigh, sign, and hand over the household — and most houses did. But the careful master asked one cold question: whose servants are these, really? The crisp livery bore the agency's thread; the papers were filed in the agency's hall; the gate was manned by the agency's porter; and the book — the record of everything done in these very rooms — was kept in the agency's hand. The house could read it, so long as the agency was willing to show it. But it did not hold it. A turnkey household, for all its polish, is the stickiest foundation of all: the agency holds the identity of everyone in your rooms, the record of what they did, and the only keys to your one gate.

So the wise houses learned three things: keep your own roll (a copy of every servant's name, scope and papers in the house's strongbox, so the house — not the agency — can say who works here); keep your own copy of the book (every errand written onto the house's own shelves too, because a record only the agency holds is the agency's record); and keep the door (re-badge a handful of servants out of the agency's livery and into the house's own thread, as a daylight drill, to prove it can be done). The relief is the mechanism: a year's discipline lifted off your shoulders by a staff that governs itself better than you did — so of course you hand it all over, until one ordinary morning the agency changes a term and you find the governance you outsourced was the governance of your own house.

The question it forces: A vendor just offered to staff our whole governed house — attested identities, per-agent audit, one policy gate, capped purses — move-in ready, and better-drilled than we built. Do we take the staff while keeping our own roll, our own copy of the book, and our own door (a seam we control, with one workflow re-homed to prove we can leave) — or have we, in our relief, handed the agency the staff and the roll and the book and the only keys?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Turnkey Household

The thing that validates the radar's whole year (governance is now productized) is the same thing that locks you in hardest (identity, audit and policy are the least-portable layers), and confusing "we are governed" with "we govern" is the trap. The event: Gemini at Work ships per-agent attested identity, action-level audit, a one-policy "Agent Gateway," sandboxes, real-time spend caps and Smart Routing — the right primitives, better-wired than most houses manage. The collision: the audit you cannot export is the vendor's audit, and an attested identity minted inside one vendor's directory is the hardest thing to re-home — so the most durable lock-in has moved up to the governance layer itself. The answer: adopt the turnkey governance, but hold three things yourself — an exportable agent registry, the per-agent audit streamed to your own store/SIEM, and a gateway/portable seam (MCP) you control with one workflow re-homed to prove you can leave.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Keep the roll. Adopt the turnkey bundle — it is genuinely good — but the deepest lock-in is who your agents are. Stand up (or export to) your own registry of every agent's identity, scope and entitlements, held in a store you control and reconciled against the vendor's console, so you can say on your own authority who works in your house. Report the share of production agents whose identity and entitlements you can export and reproduce outside the vendor's console.

2. Keep the book. Action-level logging tied to the agent is exactly right; a record only the vendor holds is the vendor's record. Wire the turnkey stack's per-agent audit into your own log store or SIEM in the same stroke it writes to the vendor's console, and confirm you can produce a complete, independent, immutable account without asking the vendor. Report the share of agent actions whose audit you hold independently, and the time to produce a full report from your own shelves.

3. Keep the door. A household you cannot leave is the agency's, not yours. Put a gateway or portable seam you control (MCP, or your own policy/identity layer) in front of the stack, and run a timed, daylight drill that re-badges one agentic workflow off the vendor's identity and audit onto your own — measure what breaks and the time-to-re-home. Report that measured time and a dated plan to extend it to the next two workflows.
