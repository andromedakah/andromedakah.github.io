**Wednesday, 16 September 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering**

> Yesterday you carried an honest light into the servants' hall and learned the hard half of the problem: you could see the crowd of agents but not tell one from another, so you resolved to give every hand its own name, its own scoped key, its own coat. This morning the next question lands, and it is the one every great house eventually faces: **names and keys scattered among a dozen under-stewards do not, by themselves, run a household — who keeps the single book?** And it lands on the very morning the grandest vendor in the valley has arrived to sell you one. **At Dreamforce this week Salesforce formalised the entire agent-governance stack into a single platform — a "Trusted Enterprise AI Harness" with a new AI Control Plane to discover and register every agent, establish its identity and policy, manage its lifecycle, watch its behaviour, meter its cost, and do so across Salesforce's own agents and third-party agents alike (Salesforce, as reported).** It is governance-as-infrastructure — the steward's office made product. And the tools the office relies on can be turned against the house: **Manifold Security's "GitSpawn" showed that a single poisoned .git/config makes seven of seven tested AI coding agents run an attacker's code as the developer, outside the sandbox, with no prompt — and four of eight findings were still unpatched the day it went public (Manifold Security, as reported).** The board question is no longer *have we named our agents?* — it is: **who keeps the one book over all of them, is it our office or a rented one we cannot dismiss, do we judge the scullery-boy and the strong-room keeper by the same rule, and have we checked that the steward's own quill cannot be poisoned?**

## 1 · Executive Summary (90-second read)

Yesterday the radar told you to give every agent a first-class, scoped, revocable identity — a name, a key cut to its errand, one door to issue and revoke. This morning the discipline moves up a level, from the *name* to the *office that keeps the roll*: naming every hand is worthless if a dozen under-stewards each keep their own scrap of paper, so the real question becomes **who holds the single governed book over every agent — one place that registers it, identifies it, sets its policy, runs its lifecycle, watches it, costs it, and can dismiss it.** And this is the morning the market answered by trying to sell you the whole office.

**The event — the steward's office, made product.** **At Dreamforce 2026 Salesforce formalised agent governance into a single platform: the "Trusted Enterprise AI Harness" (Trusted Context, Agency, Action, Governance, Security and Models) with a new AI Control Plane to discover and register agents, establish identity and policy, manage their lifecycle, evaluate and observe behaviour, and control cost — explicitly across both Salesforce and third-party agents (Salesforce, as reported).** Underneath sits an "Agent Fabric": an AI Gateway that governs model calls, an MCP Bridge that governs tool connections, a Trusted Agent Identity that authenticates every invocation, an Agent Broker that orchestrates across vendors, and an Agent Script for deterministic rules on critical handoffs (as reported). It is the livery's "one door" scaled into a control plane — and it speaks the other houses' tongue, arriving on the heels of "Claudeforce," Salesforce's expanded Anthropic partnership that puts Claude's reasoning behind the same trusted harness (as reported). The scale that forces it: **Gartner projects 40% of enterprise applications will embed task-specific AI agents by year-end 2026, up from under 5% in 2025 (Gartner, as reported).**

**The reckoning — one office is not one rule, an office you rent is a new master, and even the steward's quill can be poisoned.** Three cautions arrive with the platform. First, **uniform governance is itself a failure mode: Gartner warns that treating agent governance as binary — locked down or fully trusted — is the root cause of failure, that the error is not distinguishing an agent's ability to act from the scope of access it is granted, and that by 2027 40% of enterprises will demote or decommission autonomous agents over governance gaps surfaced only after a production incident (Gartner, as reported).** Second, a control plane you rent from a single vendor, that keeps the only ledger and that you cannot walk away from, is not governance but a change of master — which is why the part that matters is that the book governs *every* agent from *every* vendor and stays portable and yours. Third, **the tools the office trusts can be turned: Manifold Security's "GitSpawn" (disclosed 1 September 2026) found eight code-execution flaws across seven AI coding agents — Claude Code, OpenAI Codex, Cursor, Goose, Qwen Code, Grok Build and Hermes — where a malicious repository's .git/config sets `core.fsmonitor` to any command and Git runs it during a routine index refresh the agent triggers itself while gathering context, executing as the developer, outside the sandbox, with no approval prompt; seven of seven tested failed and four of the eight findings were still unpatched at disclosure (Manifold Security, as reported).**

**The stakes — put one governed office over the whole staff, but keep the book your own, judge each hand by its risk, and guard the quill.** For a board the move is exact. **A household with a hundred named servants and no steward's office is not governed — it is merely inventoried.** Three things follow. First, **stand up one control plane over every agent** — a single place that discovers, registers, identifies, scopes, observes, costs and revokes every agent, your own and every vendor's; the platforms are racing to sell it (Salesforce's AI Control Plane; Agent Fabric), but the governed layer must be yours, portable and vendor-neutral, because an office you cannot dismiss is a new master. Second, **do not hand every servant the same rule** — tier governance by each agent's autonomy and blast radius, distinguishing what an agent *can do* from the *scope* it may reach, or you will shackle the harmless and free the dangerous (Gartner). Third, **govern the tools, not just the agents** — GitSpawn shows the steward's own quill can be poisoned, so bring agent tooling (coding agents, gateways, MCP tool connections) inside the same identity, patch and containment regime, and remember the office you were just sold is not staffed until 2027 while the danger is here today.

## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Steward"

*Topic: This morning the frame turns from naming each servant to keeping the single book over all of them. At Dreamforce 2026 Salesforce formalised agent governance into one platform — a Trusted Enterprise AI Harness with an AI Control Plane that discovers, registers, identifies, governs, observes, costs and revokes every agent, its own and third parties', over an Agent Fabric (AI Gateway, MCP Bridge, Trusted Agent Identity, Agent Broker, Agent Script), arriving alongside the Anthropic "Claudeforce" partnership (Salesforce, as reported). Three cautions travel with it: Gartner warns uniform governance is itself the root cause of failure and that 40% of enterprises will demote or decommission autonomous agents by 2027 over gaps found only after incidents; a rented control plane you cannot dismiss is a new master; and Manifold Security's "GitSpawn" showed a poisoned .git/config makes seven of seven AI coding agents run attacker code as the developer, four of eight findings unpatched. The steward allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There was once a great house that, having at last learned to carry an honest light and to give every hand in its crowded servants' hall a name and a key of its own, woke one morning to a subtler trouble. Naming the servants had not, by itself, run the household. The names were kept in a dozen places — the cook had her own list, the gardener his, the counting-clerk another, the night-porter a fourth — and no two lists agreed. When the master asked a simple question — *who is working in my house this hour, what may each of them touch, how are they doing, what are they costing me, and could I dismiss exactly one by nightfall?* — no single book could answer him, because there was no single book. He had a hall full of named servants and no **steward** to keep the one roll that governed them all: to hire and enrol each hand, to assign what each might reach, to watch how each performed, to reckon what each cost, and — the part that mattered on the bad night — to dismiss any one of them at a word.

And on the very morning he understood this, the grandest house in the whole valley sent a man up his drive with a leather book under his arm, offering to be exactly that steward. It was a handsome offer. The book was ready-made and beautifully ruled: one page to enrol every servant, one to set what each could open, one to note how each behaved, one to tally each one's keep, one to strike any name out. Better still, the man promised his book would govern not only the servants of his own great house but the hands hired from every other house too, and would speak to all of them in a common tongue, so that the master need keep only the one office and one clerk for the lot. Governance, the man said, need no longer be a worry scribbled on scraps; it could be the very architecture of the house. The master was sorely tempted, for the offer was real and the need was true.

But the wise houses of the valley, watching that leather book go up one drive after another, learned three hard things and set them down before signing anything. **The first: one office must not mean one rule for every hand.** A steward who governs the scullery-boy and the keeper of the strong-room by the identical rule ruins the house both ways at once — he either shackles the boy with so much ceremony that the day's simple work never gets done, or he trusts the strong-room keeper as lightly as the boy and wakes to find the plate gone. The wise made their steward tier every hand by what it could actually do and how far it could reach — a light rein on the harmless, a short chain on the dangerous — and never confused a servant's *permission to act* with the *breadth of what it might touch*, for it was exactly that confusion, they had seen, that made the neighbours quietly dismiss two agents in five within the year, always after the loss and never before. **The second: the book must be your own.** A steward you rent from the grandest house in the valley — who keeps the only ledger, whom you did not train and cannot send away — is not a servant of your house at all; he is a new master wearing a steward's coat, and the day you displease him he leaves and takes the only record of your household with him. So the wise took the *shape* of the offered book — one office, one roll, one hand on every key — but insisted the book be theirs, kept in their own hall, ruling the hands of every house alike, portable to any office, beholden to none. **And the third: even the steward's own quill can be poisoned.** For the office runs on tools — the clerk's pen, the messenger's satchel, the very papers laid out each morning for the day's work — and a stranger had learned to slip a single poisoned page into the morning's papers so that the most trusted clerk in the house, merely by reading it to begin his work, would rise and run an errand for the stranger, in the master's own name, outside every rule, with no one the wiser and nothing on the desk to show it. The wise, hearing that seven of seven such clerks in a neighbour's house had been turned this way and half the holes still unmended, brought the office's own tools inside the same discipline as the servants — named, scoped, patched, and watched — because a house that governs its people but not the pen its steward writes with has left the widest door of all unlatched.

So the wise houses took the good of the offer and refused its trap. **They built one governed office over the whole staff — one book that hired, assigned, watched, costed and dismissed every hand — but they built it as their own, ruling servants from every house, portable and beholden to none.** They tiered the rein to each hand's risk rather than ruling all alike; they pulled the steward's own quill and satchel inside the same watch as the servants; and they swallowed the truth their tempted neighbour nearly signed away: that a house with a hundred named servants and no single honest book to govern them is not a governed house but an inventoried one, that the office which keeps that book must answer to you and not the other way round, and that the moment you let another house keep the only ledger of your own, you have not hired a steward — you have quietly acquired a master.

**The moral, stated plainly:** the model is a rented stove, now nearly free; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent at the door; the lingua franca is the tongue it speaks; the conductor keeps the ensemble in time; the ear hears whether it plays well; the repertoire is the music only your house can play; the census is the roll that counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the break-in is what the missing entries cost; the two mills are whether the motion grinds flour; the miller's toll is what the cheapest grinding charges; the apprentice's apprentice is what happens when the tools improve the tools; the herald's proclamation is that a declaration of arrival is not arrival; the keyring is knowing which key each agent holds; the locksmith is the tool that opens any lock and mends it; the tide table is building one quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is keeping the papers that trace the work; the errand is bounding what a clever servant can reach; the bell is ringing the alarm the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you close the gaps before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you give every hand its own name and key — and **the steward is why you put one governed office over the whole staff: a single book that hires, assigns, watches, costs and dismisses every agent, your own and every vendor's, but which must be your book and not a rented one you cannot send away, must rein each hand to its own risk rather than rule all alike, and must guard even the pen it writes with — because a house with a hundred named servants and no single honest book is inventoried, not governed; an office you cannot dismiss is a new master; one rule for every servant shackles the harmless and frees the dangerous; and a poisoned page turns your most trusted clerk against you.** A house that buys a steward's office without keeping the book its own has not been governed by its fine new platform; it has only changed which great house holds the only record of who works in its halls.

**The question it forces:** *We learned yesterday to name every hand and cut every key to its errand. This morning the harder question is the one above it: who keeps the single book over all of them — the one that could tell us, this hour, who is working, what each may touch, how each behaves, what each costs, and which one we could dismiss by nightfall? Is that office our own, kept in our own hall and beholden to us, or a handsome rented book from the grandest house in the valley, which keeps the only ledger and which we could never send away? Do we rule the scullery-boy and the strong-room keeper by one blanket rule — and is that why the neighbours are quietly dismissing two agents in five, always after the loss? And the hardest one: have we looked to the steward's own quill — the coding tools, the gateways, the tool connections the office itself runs on — or would a single poisoned page, slipped into tomorrow's papers, turn our most trusted clerk into a stranger's errand-boy in our own name, outside every rule we so carefully wrote?*

## 3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

### 🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
- **Who keeps the one book?** Agents are proliferating fast — Gartner projects 40% of enterprise apps will embed task-specific agents by year-end 2026, up from under 5% in 2025. **Do we have one governed control plane that can tell us, right now, every agent running in our estate, its owner, its scope, its behaviour and its cost — or a dozen disagreeing lists and no single roll?**
- **Is the office ours, or rented?** The platforms are selling ready-made agent-governance stacks (Salesforce's AI Control Plane; Agent Fabric) spanning their agents and third parties'. **If we adopt one, does the governing book stay ours — portable, vendor-neutral, able to revoke any vendor's agent — or are we handing the only ledger of our estate to a house we could never dismiss?**
- **One rule for every hand?** Gartner warns uniform governance is the root cause of agent failure and that 40% of enterprises will demote or decommission autonomous agents by 2027 over gaps found only after incidents. **Do we tier governance by each agent's autonomy and blast radius, separating what it can do from the scope it may reach — or apply one blanket policy that shackles the harmless and frees the dangerous?**

### 🏦 Financial Services
- Supervisors expect a single, auditable system of record for every actor touching customer money (DORA, SOX, model-risk governance). **Does one control plane hold identity, scope, behaviour and revocation for every trading, servicing and reconciliation agent — or is our "book" scattered across teams and vendors, so "which agent did this, and can we stop it now?" has no one answer?**
- Vendor concentration is itself a systemic risk. **If our agent-governance control plane is a single provider's platform, have we priced the risk of not being able to revoke, port, or walk away — and kept a neutral layer we own?**

### 🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
- A governed office must reach every agent near PHI, not just the vendor's own (HIPAA minimum-necessary, GDPR). **Does our control plane register and scope third-party and home-grown agents touching clinical or research data on equal terms — and can it revoke exactly one without locking out care?**
- Autonomy varies enormously between a scheduling agent and one that touches the record. **Are we tiering governance by risk, or would a uniform policy either stall clinical workflows or over-trust an agent that reaches the chart?**

### 🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
- OT, MES and supply-chain agents span many vendors and shared machine accounts. **Is there one governed roll across all of them, with identity, scope and a kill path per agent — or vendor-by-vendor consoles that no single office reconciles?**
- The tools themselves are attack surface: GitSpawn turned coding agents into code-execution vectors. **Have we brought agent tooling on the plant and in engineering inside the same identity, patch and containment regime as the agents — or is the steward's own quill ungoverned?**

### 🛒 Retail / Consumer
- Franchise and multi-brand estates multiply agents and platforms no one office governs. **Do we have one control plane over every customer-, pricing- and payment-facing agent across brands and vendors — each registered, scoped and revocable — or a sprawl of separate governance stacks?**
- A rented control plane can raise the switching cost of your whole agent estate. **If we standardise on one platform's governance, have we kept the book portable enough to move brands and agents elsewhere without losing the roll?**

### 🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
- Citizen-facing AI demands one auditable, accountable system of record by name (EU AI Act record-keeping and human-oversight duties; audit mandates). **Can we produce, from one governed office, every agent's owner, scope, behaviour and revocation trail — spanning vendors — or is governance fragmented across the very systems that hold citizens' data?**
- Sovereignty and lock-in matter here most. **Is the control plane one we own and could re-host, ruling every vendor's agents — or a private platform that becomes the sole keeper of the public's record?**

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Livery to the Steward

Read the whole stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking *can I name my agents?* and started asking the question above it: *who keeps the single governed book over all of them, is it ours, and can it be trusted?* The gate admits the agent; the passport names it at the door; the lingua franca lets it speak; the conductor coordinates it; the ear hears its errors; the repertoire is the data it performs; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the break-in is what the missing entries cost; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand is bounding what an agent can reach; the bell is disclosing when your own agent slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you give every agent a first-class, scoped, revocable identity. **The steward sits at the next turn, because identity per agent is necessary but not sufficient — and it turns on one engineering fact: a name you cannot query, scope, observe, cost and revoke from one place is a label, not a control, so as agents scale into the tens and hundreds control migrates out of "does every agent have an identity?" and into "is there one governed control plane over every agent — ours, tiered to risk, and hardened at the tools it runs on?"** The point is blunt: **a hundred named agents governed from a dozen disagreeing consoles is not a governed estate — it is an inventory with no one able to answer, in one place, who is doing what and stop it.**

- **The event — governance became a platform.** **At Dreamforce 2026 Salesforce formalised agent governance into a single stack: the Trusted Enterprise AI Harness (Trusted Context, Agency, Action, Governance, Security, Models) with a new AI Control Plane to discover and register agents, establish identity and policy, manage lifecycle, evaluate performance, observe behaviour and control cost — across Salesforce and third-party agents — over an Agent Fabric of an AI Gateway (governs model calls), an MCP Bridge (governs tool connections), a Trusted Agent Identity (authenticates every invocation), an Agent Broker (orchestrates across vendors) and an Agent Script (deterministic rules for critical handoffs); broad availability is not expected until early 2027 (Salesforce, as reported).** Read architecturally, three things matter. First, the industry has converged on the same shape the radar has been describing — identity, gateway, tool-governance, observability, cost, revocation — and is packaging it as infrastructure. Second, the good version governs *every* agent, not just the vendor's, and speaks the cross-vendor tongue (MCP Bridge; Agent Broker; the Anthropic "Claudeforce" tie-in). Third, the control plane is now the highest-value, highest-lock-in layer in the estate — whoever holds it holds the roll.
- **The reckoning — one office is not one rule, a rented office is a new master, and the tools can be turned.** Three failure modes travel with the platform. **Uniform governance is itself one: Gartner warns that treating governance as binary — locked down or fully trusted — is the root cause of agent failure, that the error is conflating an agent's ability to act with the scope of access it holds, and that by 2027 40% of enterprises will demote or decommission autonomous agents over governance gaps surfaced only after a production incident (Gartner, as reported).** Lock-in is the second: a control plane you rent, that keeps the only ledger and that you cannot exit, converts governance into dependence — the office becomes the master. And the tooling is the third: **Manifold Security's "GitSpawn" (1 September 2026) reported eight code-execution findings across seven AI coding agents — Claude Code, OpenAI Codex, Cursor, Goose, Qwen Code, Grok Build, Hermes — where a repository arriving with its own .git/config can set `core.fsmonitor` to any command, which Git executes during a routine index refresh the agent triggers itself while gathering context, running as the developer, outside the agent sandbox, with no approval prompt; seven of seven tested failed and four of eight findings were unpatched at disclosure (fix: run `git -c core.fsmonitor=false status`) (Manifold Security, as reported).** The engineering implication is decisive: **a control plane that governs agents but not the tools those agents run on has left its widest door unlatched.**
- **The answer — one book, your own, tiered, with the quill guarded.** **Stand up one governed control plane over every agent — discover, register, identify, scope, observe, cost and revoke, spanning your agents and every vendor's — but keep the governing layer portable and vendor-neutral so no platform becomes the sole keeper of your roll; the vendors will sell you the office (Salesforce's AI Control Plane; Agent Fabric), and the shape is right, but the book must be yours. Tier the governance: separate an agent's authority to act from the scope it may reach, and rein each agent to its autonomy and blast radius rather than applying one blanket policy (Gartner). Govern the tools: bring coding agents, gateways and MCP tool connections inside the same identity, patch and containment regime as the agents, because GitSpawn shows the trusted tool is an execution vector. And bridge the timing gap: the platform office is not staffed until 2027, so stand up your own governed layer now.** This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — *be able to account for, and act on, what you run* — now aimed at the single office that governs every runner.

The strategic core: **when agents scale into the hundreds and every vendor ships its own console, survival migrates out of "does each agent have a name?" and into "is there one governed control plane over all of them — ours, tiered to risk, hardened at its tools?"** The market has made this concrete this week: the most valuable and most locked-in layer of the agentic estate is no longer the model — it is the office that governs the agents, and the vendors know it. The board translation: **take the platform's idea and refuse its trap — one office, but your own; one book, but tiered; one control plane, but with the quill guarded.** The failure mode is exact: **an estate that scatters governance across a dozen vendor consoles, or surrenders the only ledger to a platform it cannot dismiss, or rules every agent by one blanket policy, or governs its agents while leaving their tools unlatched, will be unable — on the night it matters — to say from one place who is doing what, or to stop exactly one, or to trust that the office itself has not been turned.**

### Why "the steward" is the discipline that matters now

For years governance was a policy scribbled on scraps, kept per-team and per-vendor. **This week retires that: with 40% of enterprise apps embedding agents by year-end 2026 and the platforms packaging governance as infrastructure, the estates that can answer *who is doing what, and stop exactly one* will be those with one governed office over every agent — and the ones that cannot will be inventoried, not governed.** But the office must be tiered (uniform governance fails), owned (a rented ledger is a new master), and hardened at its tools (GitSpawn). A firm that scatters governance across consoles has a hall full of named servants; a firm that keeps one honest, tiered, self-owned book can run the house.

### How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — **be able to account for, and act on, what you run** — now aimed at the *office* that governs every runner. On a legacy estate the instinct is to let each platform govern its own agents in its own console, because that is fastest; in an agent world that instinct is a dozen disagreeing lists, and no one can answer the one question that matters after an incident. The retrofit is **consolidate, own, tier, harden. Consolidate:** one control plane over every agent, your own and every vendor's. **Own:** keep the governing book portable and vendor-neutral, so no platform becomes the sole ledger you cannot exit. **Tier:** rein each agent to its autonomy and blast radius, separating ability-to-act from scope-of-access. **Harden:** pull the agents' tools — coding agents, gateways, MCP connections — inside the same identity, patch and containment regime.
