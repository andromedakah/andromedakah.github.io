1 · Executive Summary

For a month this radar has argued the model is a commodity, so the durable advantage is the layer you own around it. This week made it concrete with controls — the airlock, the keyring, the proving ground, the waterworks, the loading dock. Today the story turns from access to identity: not which doors an agent may open, but whose authority it acts under — and whether your ledger can tell the agent's deeds from your own.

1. The most convenient identity for an agent is your own — the trap. On 30 July Google gave Gemini Spark the run of your real desktop Chrome, using the accounts you are signed into and the passwords saved in Chrome's manager, to run errands on your behalf. Google's docs: in local mode the agent "has access to all the same sites that you do, including sites you are signed into." Convenient — and the agent is no longer acting as itself. It is acting as you.

2. Attribution is what you cannot buy back later. The hardest agent problem is telling which actions were the agent versus the user. Solve it at issuance — strike each agent its own seal — or you never solve it. On 28 July at Black Hat 2026, Snowflake launched its Cortex AI Gateway on the Natoma acquisition, enforcing identity, policy and audit at the tool-call level, with seven identity partners. Only ~19% of orgs classify an agent as a human-equivalent insider; ~80% have seen agents act beyond scope.

3. The engine keeps commoditizing. Opus 5, Gemini 3.6 Flash, GPT-5.6 Sol, Kimi K3, DeepSeek V4-Flash-0731 (MIT) and Alibaba's open-weight Qwen3.8-Max make the brain cheaper by the week. The durable advantage is not which brain you rent but the signet office — the identity, scope, log and revocation — you build around it.

Bottom line: the layer around the commodity model grows a new wall — the one between your identity and your agents'. Rent the brain; own the signet office — where every agent gets its own stamp, scoped and revocable, and every act is written to the agent that did it — because a business that hands its fleet the master's ring has not deployed AI, it has signed a blank cheque in its own hand and called it delegation.

2 · Allegory — The Signet Ring

Picture a great house in an age before signatures, when a man's authority travelled in a ring. The master's signet — a carved stone that pressed his mark into hot wax — was the master in every place he could not stand himself: a letter sealed with it was his word, a door opened to it. The whole house understood the terrible simplicity: whoever holds the signet is the master. Then the house hired a thousand errand-runners and met a fork. The fast way to give them authority was to press the master's own ring into each runner's hand. It worked — and it was the moment the house lost the one thing it cannot lose: the power to say who did what. The daybook filled with deeds in the master's wax the master never made, and when a runner was turned and sealed a ruinous bargain, the ledger said only, in the master's own wax, the master agreed.

So the wise houses built a signet office — a plain room, a stern clerk, a rack of blank rings — and made a law: no runner carries the master's ring; every runner is struck its own. A distinct mark, cut for the errands it is trusted with, stamped with an expiry, written in the daybook. Now every deed bears the hand that made it; if a runner strays, the clerk melts that one seal in a heartbeat, and the master's ring never leaves the master's hand. The runners are rented and grow cheaper by the week — so precisely because the brain is rented, the signet office is the part that is yours.

The question it forces: Our agents have started acting on our behalf — signing, submitting, transacting in our name. Do they each press their own seal — a distinct, scoped, logged, revocable identity we issued and can melt alone — or have we handed them the master's ring for convenience, so that nothing our machines do can ever be told apart from what we did ourselves?

3 · Technical — Rent the Brain, Own the Signet Office

The runner — the rented brain: Opus 5, Gemini 3.6 Flash, GPT-5.6 Sol, Kimi K3, DeepSeek V4-Flash-0731 (MIT) and Alibaba Qwen3.8-Max (open weights, frontier-parity) — cheaper every week; a cleverer runner wearing your identity just fills your ledger with deeds you cannot account for. The signet — the identity you issue (where the new advantage sits): the consumer trap is Gemini Spark (30 Jul) driving your real Chrome with your logins and saved passwords; the enterprise answer is Snowflake's Cortex AI Gateway (28 Jul, Black Hat 2026) on the Natoma acquisition, enforcing identity, policy and audit at the tool-call level with seven identity partners. A real signet office does four things: scope (least privilege), stamp (the agent's own mark, never a human's), log (every deed written to the agent), and revoke (melt one seal alone). The ledger — attribution: telling which action was the agent versus the user is decided at issuance; fold an agent into a human's identity and no SIEM can un-mix the wax.

Agents borrow human logins, sessions and saved passwordsEach agent carries its own distinct, issued identity.
An agent's deeds are indistinguishable from a person'sEvery deed is attributable to the agent that did it.
Revoking an agent means rotating a human's credentialsMelt one seal alone — no human, no other agent disturbed.
Attribution reconstructed (and lost) after an incidentAttribution decided at issuance and logged at the tool call.

On legacy: same seam — own what doesn't commoditize (airlock … loading dock, and now the signet). Inventory every agent and the identity it acts under; issue each its own first-class non-human identity (provision, scope, expire, revoke) through a gateway that enforces identity at the tool-call level; scope by least privilege; log every action back to the agent; and wire revocation you can pull on one agent alone. A bigger rented brain is not an identity strategy.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Inventory which identity each of your agents acts under. Map, per agent and per team, whether it acts under its own issued identity or borrows a human's or a shared service account — logins, saved credentials, live sessions, standing tokens. Report one number to the board: how many of our agents act on human or shared identities today, so their actions cannot be attributed to the agent. The size of that number is the case for a signet office, made before an incident makes it for you.

2. Issue every agent its own scoped, revocable seal — never lend it a human's. Stand up (or adopt) an agent-identity gateway that enforces identity, policy and audit at the tool-call level (Cortex/Natoma-style, or your own), and give each agent a first-class non-human identity with a real lifecycle: scope it (least privilege), stamp it distinctly, log every action back to the agent, and wire revocation you can pull on one agent alone. Treat pointing an agent at your own Chrome, logins and saved passwords as the anti-pattern it is: fast today, unaccountable forever.

3. Stack the controls, and keep the brain swappable. Wire the signet to the same control points you fitted this month so one dashboard shows, per agent, who it is (signet), what rooms it may enter (keyring), what leaves (airlock), what it did in pre-prod (proving ground), what it costs (waterworks) and what it touched on the web (the dock). Then demand the same of every vendor and model you rent — and re-benchmark the runner freely as Qwen3.8-Max and the next open-weight release reset the price. The brain is a commodity; the identity you issue is the moat.
