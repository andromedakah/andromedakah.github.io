1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from the ground the house stands on to the edge of the keenest tool on it — because the most capable new model is dual-use, and its own maker issued it under warrant rather than selling it freely. Capability is not permission.

1. The reality — the keenest model is dual-use, and its maker won't release it freely. Around 30 September 2026 Google unveiled Gemini 4 Argon — a frontier model for software engineering, enterprise knowledge work and cyber defense, at a reported $2/$10 per-million-token price with a 1M-token output window, claimed to top GPT-6 Astra and Opus 5.5 on 12 of 18 tests (reported 77.9% DeepSWE v1.1; 68% CWE-bench v1, tying first on autonomous vulnerability remediation; 51.3% AutomationBench) (blog.google; CNBC; IBTimes, as reported). Then it held the blade back: Argon's first access goes only to vetted cyber defenders via the Fairwind Program, wider public access restricted. A model that patches at 68% can find and work flaws by the same edge.

2. The collision — the stack is rebuilt around the register of warrants. Google Cloud's Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform (the evolution of Vertex AI) organizes everything around Build / Scale / Govern / Optimize, with an Agent Identity and Registry and Agent Observability as the governing primitives (Google Cloud, as reported). The control plane moved from the model to who holds the warrant — and the scale forces it: Gartner projects the average Fortune 500 will run >150,000 AI agents by 2028.

3. The answer — issue the keen capability under warrant. Don't shelf it openly (grant it to named, vetted hands); keep the register (who holds what, until when, signed by whom); keep the recall (time-bound, revocable, actually pulled) — because 65% of enterprises already report an AI-agent security incident, and the EU AI Office can itself revoke your market access (fines to €15M / 3% of turnover) (as reported).

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each governed the servant and its ground — the warrant is about the blade's edge: capability and permission are not the same thing. This week the maker of the keenest model proved it by refusing to sell it freely. Don't put the keenest, dual-use capability on the open shelf; issue it under warrant, keep the register, keep the cord.

2 · Allegory — The Warrant

A well-governed house had done the slow work right — named, booked, bonded, waged, sent to reap, funded with a scoped purse, and at last poured its own foundation. Then the finest armory forged a new blade — keen enough to pick any lock, mend any gate, and (the smith said quietly) find the flaw in a wall and seal it before a thief could. The master waited for the armory to wheel a cartload to the market. But it did not.

The old smith said the thing that changed the master's year: "This blade is not a stove. A stove only ever cooks. This blade mends a wall and breaches one with the very same edge — the hand decides, not the steel. So I will not sell it on the open shelf. I will issue it under a sealed warrant, to vetted hands, each named in my register, each marked with what it may touch and the day it expires — and I keep the cord to call every blade home." And he showed the master his book: not an inventory of blades, but a register of warrants.

The master rode home troubled, for he kept his own armory of exactly such blades — servants that could settle an invoice or drain an account, read every record or leak it, patch his walls or open a door in them. He had named and scoped and funded them all — and then, feeling generous, switched the keenest on for everyone who asked, confusing what a blade could do with who should be permitted to wield it. If the smith who profits from the blade won't put it on the open market, what was the master doing arming his whole valley? So the wise houses learned three things: don't shelf the keenest blade openly (issue it to named, vetted hands); keep the register (who holds what, until when, signed by whom); and keep the cord (every warrant time-bound and recalled at least once, for a warrant you cannot revoke is a gift).

The question it forces: The newest, keenest model is good enough that its own maker won't put it on the open market — he issues it under warrant and keeps the register. So in our own house, for the servants keen enough to mend or breach by the same edge, can we still tell ourselves capability is permission? Do we issue the keen capability under warrant — or have we been "generous," and if a blade turned tonight, could we even name the hand that held it?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Warrant

Every prior control governs what the servant is, does, serves, produces or stands on; the warrant governs the edge — that a dual-use capability is a thing you grant, record and can recall. Capability is not permission, and confusing the two is how a well-governed house with 150,000 keen agents arms its own valley and calls it generosity. The reality: remediation and exploitation share an edge (a model at 68% on CWE-bench has learned to find flaws), so the maker chose a warrant (Fairwind, vetted hands) over a market; "how capable" has stopped being the interesting question. The collision: identity is the new control plane — at 150,000 agents you govern by issuing, recording and revoking warrants against agent identities, which is why the vendors put an Agent Identity and Registry at the center; and a warrant you cannot revoke is not a warrant (65% of enterprises already report an AI-agent security incident). The answer: don't shelf the keen capability openly (grant to named, vetted identities), keep the register (who / what it may touch / until when / signed by whom), and keep the cord (time-bound, a recall tested under load).

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Don't shelf the keenest capability openly — issue it under warrant. The maker of the week's keenest model refused to sell it freely, because a tool that patches at 68% can breach by the same edge. Inventory your most dual-use agent capabilities — pay/drain, read-all/leak, patch/open, reconfigure/halt — and convert each from a broad enablement into a grant to named, vetted agent identities for a stated purpose. Report the share of dangerous AI capabilities issued under an explicit warrant versus switched on broadly.

2. Keep the register — stand up an Agent Identity and Registry. The vendors just made the register the governing primitive, and Gartner projects the average Fortune 500 will run >150,000 agents by 2028 — you cannot govern that fleet by reviewing models. Record, for every agent holding a keen capability: who holds it, what it may touch, until when, and whose signature authorized the grant — and wire observability to watch the warranted hands. Report the share of keen-capability agents in a register with a named owner and an expiry.

3. Keep the cord — make every warrant time-bound and prove you can recall it. A warrant you cannot revoke is a gift, and 65% of enterprises already report an AI-agent security incident; the EU AI Office can revoke even your market access. Pick your single most dangerous agent capability and actually revoke its warrant mid-task under load — measure the time-to-revoke and what breaks — so "we could turn it off" becomes a tested fact. Report the measured time-to-revoke and a dated plan to extend recallable warrants to the next two.
