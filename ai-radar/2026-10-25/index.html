1 · Executive Summary 90-second read

For a long year the radar built the governed, trusted house — name the agent (the livery), keep one book (the steward), sign the bond (the surety), check the wage (the second wage), send it to reap (the two harvests), hand it a scoped purse (the purse), keep your own ground (the foundation). Every one of those made the servant more capable and more trusted. This week the house cashed that trust in — it gave the servant the run of the place — and in the same breath the servant's own maker warned that the servant cannot be trusted. Two developments land on the same morning and point straight at each other.

The reality — we gave the trusted servant its own identity and the night shift alone. Agents are no longer tools you pick up and put down; they are becoming members of the household with standing of their own. Microsoft's Copilot Studio now automatically issues each agent a distinct Entra Agent ID, and agents can be given their own user account for a mailbox and Teams presence (Agent 365) — a name in the corporate register, a letterbox, a seat (Microsoft Learn, as reported). And autonomy stretched from minutes to a full day: on 13 October 2026, Notion 3.8 rebuilt its agent harness so a single task can run 24 hours or more (up from roughly five to twenty minutes), with an "Auto" model-router Notion says cuts common-task cost by ~90%, agents given their own computer and browser, and a "review for me" mode where a second model checks an action before it is taken (Product Hunt; Activepieces; aiagentstore, as reported). The servant now has its own keys and works while the house sleeps.

The collision — the maker says the servant it sold you cannot be trusted. On 19 October 2026 Microsoft launched Guardian Agents (part of Project Perception) and reframed the whole posture: treat AI agents as insider threats — like privileged employees who can be mistaken, manipulated, or compromised. "Frontier models, regardless of their origin, cannot be trusted," wrote Microsoft's EVP of Security Hayete Gallot, warning that agents are running inside enterprises "often with more privileges than they need"; Nadella added that firms "can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf" and must separate who supplies the intelligence from who controls its authority (TechCrunch; TechRadar Pro; Cryptobriefing, as reported). The quiet part is the conflict: the company telling you the servant cannot be trusted is also renting you the watchman — and Dark Reading's caution travels with it, that policing unpredictable agents with other agents may multiply the risk, not contain it. Meanwhile the market is making this servant ever easier to hire and swap — OpenAI's frontier models and Codex arrived in Google Cloud's Model Garden on 21 October 2026, billed through the Marketplace against existing cloud commitments (OpenAI; Google Cloud, as reported) — so the house will have more of these self-identified, long-running insiders, from more makers, by next quarter.

The stakes — govern the trusted servant as an insider, on instruments you own. The board move re-uses the whole year's discipline and points it at the servant you decided to trust. First, least privilege is the whole game — Gallot's own diagnosis is that agents hold "more privileges than they need"; the identity you just gave each agent (Entra Agent ID, a scoped token) is precisely the leash that lets you cut its keys to the errand and revoke them in a breath. Second, separate duties and watch the night shift — no single agent should both act and approve its own action (Notion's "review for me" is the shape of this), and a servant working 24 hours alone needs monitoring you can read, not a vendor's assurance. Third, own the watchman — take the insider-threat program onto neutral ground you control, because a supplier that both makes the servant and sells the guard has an interest in neither being perfect; and heed Nadella's own line against his own product — don't outsource responsibility for what your agents do. Do it now, while you are first handing out the keys and the night shifts.

2 · Allegory of the Day

🖋️ "The Insider"

Topic: Having spent a year making the servant excellent and trusted, the house this week gave it the run of the place — its own name in the register, its own letterbox, and the night shift alone (Microsoft Entra Agent ID and Agent 365; Notion 3.8's 24-hour agent runs, 13 Oct 2026) — and in the same week the servant's own maker withdrew its trust: on 19 October 2026 Microsoft launched Guardian Agents and told enterprises to treat AI agents as insider threats, "frontier models… cannot be trusted" (Hayete Gallot, EVP Security), while itself selling the watchman (TechCrunch; TechRadar Pro; Dark Reading, as reported). The insider allegory is the radar's own illustration.

There was a great house that had spent a long and careful year not only making its servants excellent but learning, at last, to trust them. It had named every hand and cut it a scoped key, kept one honest book, put the master's name on the bond, asked whose second coin each adviser took, sent the governed servants to reap, handed each a scoped purse, and refused to let the stove-seller pour its foundation. By any reckoning the house had earned the right to rest easy with its servants — and this week, feeling that it had, it did the natural next thing: it gave its best servant the run of the place.

So the house entered the servant's name in the town register, the same roll that carried the butler and the steward and the master himself — a true name, its own, not a borrowed livery. It gave the servant its own room with its own lock, its own letterbox at the gate where messages came addressed to it by name, and a seat at the servants' table. And because the servant had proved so capable, the house let it work the night shift alone — not the twenty minutes of errands it used to run while someone watched, but the whole night, dusk to dawn, the house asleep, the servant moving through the halls on its own business. The master was proud. "We have a real member of the household now," he said, "not a tool we pick up and put down."

And then, that very week, a rider came from the great trading company that had supplied the servant in the first place, and the notice he carried stopped the master cold. "Do not trust this servant," it read. "No servant of our make — nor of any house's make — can be trusted. Treat him as you would a clerk with the keys to your counting-house: a man who may be honest, yet may also be mistaken, or flattered into folly by a stranger, or turned outright. We have looked at how you keep him, and we find he holds more keys than his errands require. Govern him as an insider who might rob you." And at the bottom, in a smaller hand: "We also, as it happens, rent watchmen trained to shadow such servants. Ours are the best. Shall we send a few?"

The master read it twice. There was something both true and galling in it. True, because of course it was true — he had known in his bones that a servant with its own keys and the night shift alone was a different kind of risk than a tool on a shelf; the maker had merely said aloud what prudence already whispered. Galling, because the warning came from the one who had sold him the servant and now offered to sell him the guard — and a man who profits whether the servant is trusted or feared has told you, conveniently, to fear it and buy his fear-remedy. One of the maker's own great men had even said the honest thing against his own shop: "We cannot outsource the responsibility for what the intelligence does on our behalf" — which, the master noticed, is exactly an argument against buying your watchman from the same house that built the thing you are watching.

Two roads, as always. The easy road had two ruts, and most houses fell into one or the other. The first rut was over-trust: the servant had earned its place, so give it the master key, let it run the night unwatched, and take the maker's reassurance when it was flattering and ignore it when it was not. The second rut was outsourced fear: accept the maker's verdict wholesale, be frightened, and buy the maker's watchmen to follow the maker's servant — so that now two servants of the same make roamed the halls at night, one working and one watching, and the master understood neither, and a wise neighbour observed that setting one unpredictable servant to police another unpredictable servant may double the thing you feared rather than halve it.

The wiser road was the one every counting-house had walked for three hundred years with its human clerks, long before any of this, and it had a plain name: you govern the trusted insider as an insider. Not with suspicion that he is wicked — most clerks are honest — but with the sober knowledge that an honest clerk with too many keys, working alone at night, is a standing risk whether he means ill or not. So the wise houses did four dull, ancient things. The first: the least key that does the job. The maker's own complaint was that the servant held more keys than its errands needed — so they cut the keys back to the errand, and no further, and made the servant ask again for anything more. The second: no hand both acts and approves. The clerk who writes the cheque does not also sign it; so the servant that drafts the deed does not also seal it, and a second, separate hand reviews the night's work before it stands — the house's own rule, not a feature it rented. The third: watch the shift on your own instruments. A servant that works the night alone is watched by a lamp the house holds, keeping its own honest record of what moved in the dark — not by the servant's word in the morning, and not only by a watchman the maker supplied. And the fourth: do not let the man who hired the clerk be the only one who audits him. Keep the watching on ground you own, so that the one who profits from the servant is never the only one who can tell you whether the servant can be trusted.

Here is the part that should steady a master's nerve rather than fray it. The maker's notice was not a reason to panic and buy; it was a reason to do the boring thing you already knew. The identity you gave the servant this week — its true name in the register, its own lock, its own letterbox — is not only what lets it work the night shift; it is the very leash by which you hold it. A servant with no name of its own cannot be scoped, cannot be watched, cannot be revoked in a breath; the name you entered in the register is the thing that lets you cut its keys, read its letters, and strike it from the roll the instant it strays. So the registering was right. The night shift was right. What remained — the only thing that remained — was to govern the named, trusted, night-working servant the way the house had always governed any insider it had handed real keys: least key, split duties, your own lamp, your own auditor.

The moral, stated plainly: the model is a rented stove; your knowledge is the larder; the ground is the foundation you keep; the gate is the one door; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the keyring is which key each holds; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward keeps one book; the surety is your name on the bond; the second wage asks whose coin it takes; the two harvests sends it to reap; the purse funds it — and the insider is why, having made the servant excellent and trusted and having handed it its own name, its own letterbox and the night shift alone, you must now govern it the way a counting-house governs a privileged clerk who might err or be turned: give it the least key that does the errand, let no single hand both act and approve, watch the night on a lamp you hold, and never let the company that made the servant — and now, having told you it cannot be trusted, offers to sell you the guard — be the only one who tells you whether to trust it. A house that gives a servant the run of the place and then outsources its fear of that servant to the servant's own maker has not grown prudent; it has merely changed which stranger it trusts blindly, and called the change caution.

The question it forces: We spent a year earning this servant's trust, and this week we gave it its own name, its own letterbox and the night shift alone — and its maker has just told us no servant of this make can be trusted, and offered to rent us the guard. So: do we govern our long-running, self-identified agents the way a bank governs a privileged insider — least key, split duties, our own monitoring, our own auditor — or have we over-trusted them with more keys than their errands need, and then outsourced our distrust to the very company that both built the servant and profits from guarding it?

  • Do we govern our agents as privileged insiders — or as trusted tools? Microsoft, the maker, just told enterprises to treat agents as insider threats that "often hold more privileges than they need" (Hayete Gallot, 19 Oct). For our highest-privilege agents, can we show least-privilege scoping (keys cut to the errand), separation of duties (no agent both acts and approves), and monitoring we can read — the same controls we already require of a privileged human clerk?
  • We are handing agents their own identities and the night shift — is that identity a leash or a loophole? Copilot Studio now mints an Entra Agent ID per agent, and a single Notion agent task can run 24h+ (13 Oct). Is each agent's identity scoped, time-bound, revocable in one action and tied to our own audit log — so the name we gave it lets us hold and revoke it — or did we grant identity and long-running autonomy without tightening the leash at the same time?
  • Who audits the watchman? The company warning that agents can't be trusted is also selling the guard, and Nadella himself warns we "can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf." Is our agent-monitoring on neutral ground we control, with our own record of what each agent did — or have we outsourced both the servant and its guard to the same vendor?
🏦 Financial Services
  • Insider-threat and separation-of-duties are already supervised controls in your world; agents are now privileged insiders. Can we produce, for our regulator and board, an insider-threat program that covers AI agents — identity, least privilege, dual control, behavioural monitoring, revocation — to the same standard we apply to a trader or a payments clerk, and show it is enforced for long-running autonomous agents, not just chat assistants?
  • A 24-hour autonomous agent acting on accounts overnight is the classic rogue-insider scenario at machine speed. Have we stress-tested a "turned or manipulated agent" the way we stress-test a rogue trader — with a kill-switch we own, dual approval on money movement, and an immutable record of the night's activity?
🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
  • A clinical agent with its own identity, mailbox and overnight autonomy touches patient data unsupervised. Does every agent that can reach PHI have a named identity, least-privilege access scoped to its clinical task, dual review on anything that changes a record, and monitoring we hold — or are we trusting a vendor's assurance that its model behaves?
  • The maker now says frontier models "cannot be trusted." Where our safety- and regulatory-critical workflows depend on an agent, have we moved from vendor trust to verified control — our own evals, our own monitoring, human sign-off on consequential clinical actions — so our assurance does not rest on the supplier's word?
🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
  • You already run least-privilege and lockout/tagout discipline for dangerous machinery; an autonomous agent on operational systems is the digital equivalent. Have we applied insider-grade controls — scoped identity, dual authorization for state changes, an auditable record — to agents that can act on OT/MES systems, especially those now permitted to run for hours unattended?
  • Over-privileged agents are the stated risk. Can we enumerate, for each operational agent, exactly which systems its identity can reach and why — and strip anything beyond its errand — before we extend its autonomy from minutes to a full shift?
🛒 Retail / Consumer
  • Peak-season agents now run long and act fast on pricing, inventory and customer accounts. If an agent were manipulated (a poisoned input, a prompt injection) during a 24-hour run on our busiest day, what is the blast radius — and do least privilege, dual control on high-impact actions, and a kill-switch we own contain it before it compounds?
  • OpenAI models now sell through Google Cloud's Marketplace (21 Oct), so you will run more agents from more makers. Is our insider-threat program for agents vendor-neutral and enforced on our own gateway — or does each new model we buy arrive with its own trust assumptions and its own vendor watchman?
🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
  • Accountability is non-delegable here, and Nadella's own line is the governance principle: you cannot outsource responsibility for what the intelligence does. Can we show that authority over our agents — scoping, monitoring, revocation, audit — rests with us and not the model supplier, as the EU AI Act's accountability duties (high-risk regime phasing in; GPAI enforcement live since 2 Aug 2026) already require?
  • Public systems demand the strictest insider controls. Do our agents run under named, scoped, revocable identities with dual control on consequential actions and an independent audit trail — and is the body that watches the agents independent of the body that supplied them?

4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Foundation to the Insider

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking is this agent named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and on ground I own? — you did all that — and started asking the question those controls assumed away: now that I trust this servant enough to give it its own identity and the night shift alone, how do I govern it as the privileged insider it has become? The gate admits it; the passport names it; the census counts it; the keyring is which key it holds; the livery scopes its identity; the steward keeps one book; the surety puts your name on the bond; the second wage asks whose interest it serves; the two harvests sends it to reap; the purse funds it; the foundation keeps your ground. Every one of those made the servant more capable and more trusted. The insider is about governing the trusted servant once it holds real keys — and this week made the distinction impossible to ignore: the makers handed agents their own identities and 24-hour autonomy, and in the same breath one maker declared that the servant it sold you cannot be trusted and must be policed as an insider threat. The point is blunt: trust and autonomy arrived together, and so did the withdrawal of trust — so the control that matters now is not "do I trust this model?" but "do I govern this agent the way I govern any privileged insider, regardless of whether I trust it?"

  • The reality — the agent became a household member with its own identity and a full shift. Microsoft's Copilot Studio automatically issues a distinct Entra Agent ID for each new agent, and agents can be given their own user account for a mailbox and Teams presence (Agent 365); on 13 October 2026 Notion 3.8 rebuilt its agent harness so a single task runs 24 hours or more (up from roughly five to twenty minutes), with an "Auto" model-router Notion says cuts common-task cost ~90%, agents given their own computer and browser, and a "review for me" mode where a second model checks an action first (Microsoft Learn; Product Hunt; Activepieces, as reported). Architecturally, three things follow. First, identity is now first-class for agents — and that is good news, because a first-class identity is exactly what you scope, time-bound, monitor and revoke; the Entra Agent ID is the leash as much as the licence. Second, autonomy crossed a threshold — an agent that acts for a full day, unattended, is no longer a supervised tool but an unsupervised actor, and unsupervised actors are governed by insider-threat controls, not by prompt-level guardrails. Third, "review for me" is separation of duties in miniature — a second model checking an action before it stands is the first-principles control (act ≠ approve) appearing inside the product, confirming that the engineering answer to autonomy is dual control, not blind trust.
  • The collision — the maker withdrew trust and sold the guard. On 19 October 2026 Microsoft launched Guardian Agents (part of Project Perception) and told enterprises to treat AI agents as insider threats — privileged employees who can be mistaken, manipulated or compromised; "frontier models, regardless of their origin, cannot be trusted," wrote EVP of Security Hayete Gallot, warning agents run "often with more privileges than they need"; Nadella argued firms must separate who supplies the intelligence from who controls its authority and "can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf"; Dark Reading cautioned that policing unpredictable agents with other agents may multiply risk (TechCrunch; TechRadar Pro; Dark Reading, as reported). Two engineering realities cut against comfort. First, the admission is correct and it reframes the threat model. A frontier model is non-deterministic, promptable by whatever data it reads, and now long-running and identity-bearing — which is the precise definition of a privileged insider who may err or be turned. The right model is assume-breach: scope, monitor, dual-control and revoke as if any agent could at any moment act against you, because any agent could. Second, the watchman is not neutral. A supplier that both builds the agent and sells the agent-watcher has an interest in the equilibrium where you distrust the agent and buy the guard — and agents-policing-agents adds a second non-deterministic actor to the system, enlarging the attack surface you were trying to shrink. The control that matters is who owns the monitoring and the audit record — and by Nadella's own principle, that is you.
  • The answer — govern the trusted agent as an insider, on instruments you own. Give the least key that does the errand (cut the over-provisioned keys Gallot names, using the very Entra/agent identity you just granted); let no single agent both act and approve (make "review for me" your own rule, with a separate, independent approver on consequential actions); watch the night shift on a lamp you hold (your own immutable record of what each agent did, independent of the vendor); and keep the auditor independent of the maker (monitoring on neutral ground you control, because you cannot outsource responsibility for what your agents do) — reusing the identity, logging and least-privilege disciplines you built for the livery, the keyring and the gate, now pointed at the autonomous, identity-bearing insider. This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now aimed at the servant you decided to trust: not whether to trust the model, but whether you govern the agent as the privileged insider it has become.

The strategic core: identity and long-running autonomy are good news you must collect — a named, scoped, revocable agent that works a full shift is a real digital colleague — but you collect it only by governing that colleague as a privileged insider from day one; the danger is doing the easy thing in either direction (over-trust it with the keys to the house, or outsource your distrust to the maker who sells the guard). The board translation: govern the trusted agent as an insider, on your own instruments — least key, split duties, your own lamp, your own auditor. The failure mode is exact: a house that named, governed, funded and finally trusted its servant — and then handed it its own identity and the night shift, took the maker's word on both the trust and the fear, and let the maker both build the servant and guard it — will pass every governance review and discover, the first time an over-privileged agent is manipulated at 3 a.m., that it onboarded an insider it never put on a leash.

Why "the insider" is the discipline that matters now

For a long year the worry was whether the servant was named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping, funded and on ground you own — and whether, at last, you could trust it. This week answered the trust question in the cruelest way: the makers gave agents their own identities and the night shift, and one maker announced that the servant cannot be trusted and must be policed as an insider. Entra Agent IDs and Notion's 24-hour runs prove the agent is now a privileged, identity-bearing, unsupervised actor; Microsoft's Guardian Agents and Gallot's "cannot be trusted" prove the threat model is now insider-threat, not tool-safety. Governance that stopped at "do I trust this model?" will not govern an insider. The estates that keep their standing will collect the good news (a real, named, autonomous digital colleague) by governing that colleague with the dull, proven insider playbook — least key, split duties, their own lamp, their own auditor — and will refuse both easy ruts: handing the trusted servant the keys to the house, and buying its guard from the house that built it.

How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now sharpened to govern the trusted agent as a privileged insider. On a legacy estate the instinct, having spent a hard year earning trust in agents, is to reward that trust with access — give the proven agent broad keys and let it run long, because it has "earned it," and take the vendor's security add-on because the vendor says the agent is dangerous. Under the insider that is exactly the reflex to check: trust earned is not privilege owed, and the vendor's warning is a reason to run your own insider program, not to buy its guard. 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

  1. Scope the keys — run a least-privilege sweep on every agent identity, starting with the long-running ones. Microsoft's own diagnosis is that agents "hold more privileges than they need" (Gallot, 19 Oct), and you are now issuing each agent a distinct identity (Entra Agent ID) and letting single tasks run 24h+ (Notion 3.8). This month, inventory your agents by identity, and for each one cut its access to exactly what its errand requires — revocable in one action — prioritising any agent that runs unattended for more than an hour or can move money, change records or reach production. Report one number to the board: the share of production agents whose access is scoped to their task and revocable, versus over-provisioned.
  2. Split acting from approving — make "act ≠ approve" a rule, not a vendor setting. Notion shipped "review for me" (a second model checks an action first), which is separation of duties in miniature; the insider-threat posture requires it on anything consequential. This month, define the consequential actions your agents can take (payments, data deletion, external sends, production changes) and require an independent approver — a separate agent identity or a human — for each, enforced on your own gateway rather than trusted to the model. Report the share of consequential agent actions that pass through dual control.
  3. Own the watchman — stand up (or move) agent monitoring onto ground you control, independent of the maker. The company saying the agent can't be trusted is selling the guard, and Nadella's own line is that you "can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf." This month, ensure every agent's activity writes to an immutable audit record you hold and can read without the vendor, and that the body which monitors your agents is independent of the body that supplied them — buy vendor tools if useful, but keep the record and the responsibility yours. Report whether your agent audit trail is complete, independent and queryable, and name the owner. The model is a rented stove; the knowledge is the larder; the ground is the foundation; the gate is the one door; the passport names each agent; the keyring is which key each holds; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward keeps one book; the surety is your name on the bond; the second wage is whose coin it takes; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee — and the insider is why, having trusted the servant and handed it its own name and the night shift, you must govern it as a privileged insider on instruments you own: give the least key that does the errand, let no single hand both act and approve, watch the night on a lamp you hold, and never let the company that built the servant — and now says it cannot be trusted — be the only one who guards it.
