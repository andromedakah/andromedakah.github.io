Wednesday, 11 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering

On 8 October 2026, at Gemini at Work, Google unveiled a universal "Gemini agent" — now in private preview — that assigns each job to the model it judges best, drawing on Google's own Gemini and Anthropic's Claude families today, with OpenAI and open-weight models to follow; it sends routine work to cheaper models, pays frontier rates only where they earn it, and can shepherd a task for days (Futurum — "puts the agent ahead of the model"; Runtimewire — "can route jobs to Claude and run for days"; completeaitraining, as reported). It is, functionally, the routing the radar told you to run and own — now offered to you, finished and free, by one of the companies it was meant to protect you from. And the commoditization that made routing worthwhile keeps deepening across vendor lines: on 9 November 2026 Anthropic shipped Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2, cutting Fable's price from a reported $10/$50 to $8/$40 per million tokens (~30% faster, Mythos gated), and shipping it inside Google Cloud and Microsoft Foundry — its own newest model now just one carrier on someone else's roster. The board question is no longer which model, nor even do we own an abstraction — it is: who runs the dispatch that assigns the work to the models — us, or the model-seller that just offered to run it for free and send us to its rivals as proof of good faith?

1 · Executive Summary 90-second read

For a long season the radar built the servant and then, in September, looked down at the ground — the foundation — and gave one instruction above the rest: own the floor. Make the model a swappable component by owning the abstraction between you and any one vendor — the gateway, the router, the logic that decides which model does which job. This morning the market has taken that exact instruction and turned it into a product to sell you. Two developments, one window, pulling in opposite directions on purpose.

The move — the model-sellers now run the dispatch itself, and book their rivals to prove it. On 8 October 2026, at Gemini at Work, Google unveiled a universal "Gemini agent" — now in private preview — that assigns each job to the model it judges best for the task, across Google's own Gemini and Anthropic's Claude model families today, with OpenAI and open-weight models promised to follow; it sends routine work to smaller, cheaper models and pays frontier rates only where they earn it, and it can shepherd a single task for days (Futurum — "Google Gemini Agent puts the agent ahead of the model"; Runtimewire — "can route jobs to Claude and run for days"; completeaitraining, as reported). Read plainly: the routing the radar told you to run and own is now offered to you, finished and free, by one of the companies it was meant to protect you from — and the presence of a rival's model on the roster (Claude, today) is the proof-of-fairness that makes handing over the dispatch feel like a gain.

The reality underneath — the commoditization that made routing worth doing keeps deepening. On 9 November 2026 Anthropic shipped Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2, cutting Fable's price from a reported $10/$50 to $8/$40 per million tokens, ~30% faster, with Mythos 5.2 gated to vetted organizations, launched ahead of a reported IPO — and available through Google Cloud and Microsoft Foundry as well as Anthropic's own platform (The New Stack; SD Times; 9to5Mac; Fortune, as reported). It follows OpenAI's GPT-6.1 Sol (DevDay, late September) at a reported $2/$10 per million tokens — about one-fifth of flagship GPT-6 Astra — at near-flagship agentic-coding and computer-use quality (aiweekly; The Next Web; Unite.ai, as reported). Capable models are now cheap, plural, and increasingly sold through each other's platforms — exactly the conditions that make a dispatcher valuable, and exactly why running that dispatcher yourself matters.

The stakes — own the dispatch, or the model-seller owns your choice among models. The whole point of the September floor was that you decide which model does each job. A vendor's routing product inverts that: it decides, it logs every job, it learns your traffic and your price tolerance, and it sets the default winner — and it can quietly favor its own family while still, technically, sending you to anyone. So keep three things yours. First, own the dispatch — the assignment of work to models runs on infrastructure and logic you control, not inside a model-seller's agent. Second, keep the logbook — the record of which job went to which model, the evals that define "best," and the traffic history live in your larder, not the dispatcher's. Third, keep a bypass route — at least one path to a model that runs through no vendor's dispatch, so you can always send work the dispatcher cannot see or steer.

2 · Allegory of the Day

🖋️ "The Dispatcher"

Topic: In September the radar's instruction was "own the floor" — build and own the abstraction that lets you route among swappable models. This autumn the model-sellers answered by shipping that routing as a product: on 8 October 2026 Google unveiled a universal Gemini agent (now in private preview) that assigns each job to the model it judges best — its own Gemini and Anthropic's Claude today, OpenAI and open-weights to follow — cheap models for routine work, frontier rates only where earned, running for days (Futurum; Runtimewire; completeaitraining, as reported). Days later, on 9 November, Anthropic cut Claude Fable 5.2 to a reported $8/$40 and shipped it through Google Cloud and Microsoft Foundry (The New Stack; SD Times, as reported) — its newest model now just one carrier on someone else's roster. The dispatcher allegory is the radar's own illustration.

There was a great house that had learned, at long last, the lesson of the foundation. It rented its fire rather than marrying a furnace, it kept its grain in its own larder, and it had even set up, in the yard, a modest dispatch of its own — a roster of carriers and a trusted clerk who, for each errand, chose which carrier to send: the baker's cart for bread, the smith's wagon for iron, the cheapest cart for a light load, the master carrier only for freight that deserved him. This was the house at the top of its game. It owned the dispatch, so no single carrier could ever hold it; when one grew dear or slow, the clerk simply struck him from the day's roster and sent another. The master was proud of that little dispatch, and he was right to be.

Now, the grandest trading company in the valley had watched all of this with great interest, and one morning it arrived at the door bearing a gift of surpassing beauty. It was a dispatch — but such a dispatch as the house had never dreamed of running. It already held a standing arrangement with every carrier in the county. It could hear an errand spoken plainly and, cleverer by far than the house's own tired clerk, send it off in an instant to whichever carrier best suited the load — a cheap cart for a small job, a master only where mastery earned its fee — and it would shepherd a long haul for days without being asked twice. And here was the stroke of genius: the company's dispatch booked the company's own rivals. "See," said the company, "we even send you to the carriers we compete with. Do not trouble yourself with the roster; let our dispatch do the choosing; what could possibly be fairer than a dispatch that books everyone?"

And the master, who had worked so hard to run his own dispatch, looked at this magnificent free thing in arrangement with all the world — even with the company's rivals — and felt that to refuse it would be mere stubbornness. So he thanked the company, and let its splendid dispatch replace his own, and dismissed the tired clerk who used to keep the roster.

Two roads, as always, and the easy one is always the one already paved. The easy road was to keep the gift: it was free, it was clever, it booked everyone, and it worked. The wiser road, which the careful houses took, began with a cold question asked not of the dispatch but of the dispatcher: who runs this yard — and what does the running-of-it let them do? And when the careful houses thought it through, the answer chilled them. The dispatcher who runs the yard hears every job — learns what the house ships, how often, and what it will pay. The dispatcher who runs the yard sets the default — which carrier wins when the choice is close, and nothing stops him from tilting the close calls toward his own cousins while still, with a perfectly straight face, sending the house to any carrier it names outright. And the dispatcher who runs the yard can re-order the roster overnight — raise a rival's rank, bury a carrier at the bottom of the list, or quietly drop a competitor the morning it suits him — and the house would never know, because the house no longer runs the dispatch. The rival carriers the company had boasted of were not the house's freedom. They were the bait that made surrendering the dispatch feel like gaining the world.

So the wise houses learned three plain things, and they cost less to keep than to recover. The first: own the dispatch. The decision of which carrier takes each errand — the assignment itself — must run on a desk you own and a logic you set, even a humble one; let the carriers compete for your work, never for the dispatcher's favor. A clever dispatch you do not run is not a convenience; it is a toll-keeper you installed in your own yard and handed the ledger. The second: keep the day-book. The record of which errand went to which carrier, the measure by which you judge one "best," the history of your own shipping — these are the estate, and they must live in your hall, not the dispatcher's office; for a house whose shipping-book lives in another man's yard has told that man everything about itself and kept nothing. And the third: keep one route that bypasses every dispatch. Not a second dispatch from a second company — an actual standing road to one good carrier that runs through no dispatcher at all, so that on the morning the clever roster is re-ordered against you, you can still send a load that no dispatcher can see, re-rank, or refuse.

Here is the part that should keep a master awake. The genius of the gift was never the cleverness of the dispatch; it was the rivals on the roster. A dispatch in arrangement with only the company's own carriers, the master would have refused in an afternoon — that is plainly a trap. But a dispatch that books the company's competitors disarms the very suspicion that would have saved him, because it looks like the opposite of lock-in. It looks like choice. And that is the finest trick in the valley: to capture a man's choice, do not take his carriers away — take over the clerk who chooses among them, and leave every carrier sitting right there on the roster where he can see them and never quite reach past your hand to hire one himself. The company did not want to sell the house one carrier. It wanted to own the house's choosing, which is worth more than any carrier, because the choosing is the whole relationship, and whoever owns the choosing owns the house.

The moral, stated plainly: the model is a rented stove, now so cheap a bigger one draws a shrug; the knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising servant eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; the foundation is why you own the ground it stands on — and the dispatch is the one desk on that ground from which all the models are assigned, so whoever owns the dispatch owns the house, however many rival carriers it offers to hand your work to: own the dispatch so the choosing is yours, keep the day-book in your own hall so your traffic is yours, and keep one standing road that bypasses every dispatcher, so that no clever free dispatch, in arrangement with all the world, can ever quietly decide for you which world you get. A house that lets the grandest company run its dispatch — because the dispatch books the company's rivals too — has not won its freedom; it has handed over the one desk that freedom was made of, and mistaken a dispatch that books everyone for a dispatch that is its own.

The question it forces: We were told to own the floor — the routing that lets us treat models as swappable and choose the best one for each job. Now a model-seller offers to be that dispatcher, for free, and sends us to its rivals to prove it means us no harm. Do we own our dispatch — the assignment policy, the day-book, a bypass route — or have we handed the one desk that was meant to keep us free to the very company it was meant to keep us free from, and told ourselves that a dispatch that books everyone must surely be a dispatch that is ours?

3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
  • Who runs the dispatch between us and the models — us, or a model-seller? A vendor's agent that "assigns each job to the best model, including our competitors'" is selling the routing layer the radar told you to own. For our top AI workflows, does the decision of which model does each job run on infrastructure and logic we control — or inside a vendor's agent that also sells one of the models it's choosing among?
  • A dispatch that books our rivals' models can still be a trap. What does the dispatcher get? If we adopt a vendor's model-routing agent, can we name exactly what that vendor then sees (our prompts, our traffic patterns, our price tolerance), decides (the default winner on close calls), and can change unilaterally (the ranking, a competitor's availability) — and are we comfortable that one model-seller holds all three?
  • Where is our dispatch day-book, and could we re-point it tomorrow? Do the records of which job went to which model, the evals that define "best," and our traffic history live in a form we own and could carry to a different router in a week — or do they accrete inside one vendor's dispatch, so that leaving means leaving our own choosing behind?
🏦 Financial Services
  • Best-execution and venue-neutrality are concepts you already live by. Is our model routing "best execution" — are we choosing the model for each task on our own measured criteria, with an auditable record — or have we outsourced the assignment to a counterparty that is also one of the venues, with no independent log of why each job was placed where it was?
  • Concentration and third-party-dependency rules already bite, and the EU AI Act high-risk regime has been live since 2 August 2026. If the vendor running our routing agent re-ranked its own models, degraded a competitor's line, or had an outage, which critical workflows would silently shift or stop — and would we even detect it, given the dispatcher owns the logs?
🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
  • Clinical and safety workflows are validated against a specific model's behaviour. If a vendor's dispatch silently reassigns a validated workflow to "the best model for the task," does our validation still hold — or have we let an opaque routing decision move the ground under a regulated process without a change-control trail we own?
  • Patient data is the ultimate larder, and a dispatcher hears the job. When a model-seller's agent routes our clinical queries, what leaves our walls to reach the dispatch — and is the routing operator now holding a stream of PHI-adjacent traffic we would never hand to a single vendor deliberately?
🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
  • You already own your production scheduling rather than letting one supplier sequence your line. Have we applied the same rule to model routing — owning the "which model, which job" dispatcher ourselves — or has a vendor's agent become the foreman that assigns the work, with our rivals' tools on the rack only to make the arrangement look open?
  • Dual-sourcing is second nature for physical inputs. Do we keep at least one model path that bypasses every vendor's dispatch entirely — a route we can run if the clever free dispatcher is re-ordered against us — the way we keep a qualified second supplier for a critical part?
🛒 Retail / Consumer
  • Cheap, plural models are a margin gift (Fable 5.2 at $8/$40; GPT-6.1 Sol ~$2/$10; routing can cut bills ~30–50%, as reported) — but only if you capture the arbitrage. Are we assigning work per task on value through a dispatch we own, pocketing the spread — or is a vendor's dispatcher capturing that choice (and the data about our traffic) while we pay for the privilege of looking multi-model?
  • Peak-season reliability is existential. If our routing runs inside one vendor's agent and that vendor has an outage or re-ranks its models on our biggest sales day, does checkout / search / support fall back to a carrier we control — or does it go wherever the dispatcher sends it?
🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
  • Procurement neutrality and avoiding single-vendor capture are governance duties, not preferences. Can we show that the component choosing which AI model serves each public task is vendor-neutral and ours — not a routing agent sold by one of the model suppliers, however many competitors it lists?
  • Sovereignty and decade-long horizons matter most here. Are we procuring the routing layer — the dispatch — as something we own and can run on open or portable infrastructure, or are we letting a single company run the yard that assigns all our models, where the deepest and least visible lock-in now lives?

4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Foundation to the Dispatcher

In September the radar's floor-level instruction was concrete: own an abstraction/gateway between your systems and any one model, so models become swappable components routed per task. That advice was correct — and this autumn the market did the thing that always happens to correct advice about a valuable layer: a vendor productized it. Google's Gemini agent (unveiled 8 October 2026 at Gemini at Work, in private preview) is, functionally, the dispatcher the radar told you to run — it hears a job, assigns the model it judges best (Google's Gemini and Anthropic's Claude today; OpenAI and open-weights promised), sends routine work to cheaper models and reserves frontier rates for work that earns them, and shepherds long-horizon tasks for days (Futurum — "puts the agent ahead of the model"; Runtimewire; Techgenyz; completeaitraining, as reported). The engineering question is no longer should we route among models — the market has settled that, and multi-model is already the default (average models per enterprise account rose from a reported ~2.1 to ~4.7 in a year; IDC's 2026 FutureScape projects 70% of top AI-driven enterprises on advanced, dynamic multi-model routing by 2028, as reported). The question is who runs the dispatcher — and therefore the three powers that come with it: observation, default, and re-ranking.

  • The move — the router is now a vendor product, and its neutrality is the selling point. Google's Gemini agent assigns each job across Gemini and Claude today (OpenAI and open-weights to follow), choosing cheap models for routine work and frontier models only where they earn it, running for days (Futurum; Runtimewire, as reported). Three engineering facts travel with any dispatcher you do not run. First, the dispatcher sees everything — every prompt, every job type, the frequency and the cost tolerance of your traffic pass through it; whoever operates it holds that telemetry. Second, the dispatcher sets the default — on close calls, some policy decides the winner, and if that policy is the vendor's, the vendor's own models are the ones on the scale. Third, the dispatcher's roster is the vendor's to re-order — model rankings, a competitor's availability, price-performance thresholds can be altered unilaterally and invisibly, because the control plane is theirs. The presence of a rival's model (Claude) on Google's roster does not neutralize any of the three; it only makes them easy to overlook.
  • The reality — commoditization keeps feeding the dispatcher, now across vendor lines. Anthropic cut Claude Fable 5.2 to a reported $8/$40 per million tokens on 9 November (~30% faster, Mythos 5.2 gated), shipping it through Google Cloud and Microsoft Foundry as well as its own platform; OpenAI's GPT-6.1 Sol runs ~$2/$10, about one-fifth of GPT-6 Astra, at near-flagship agentic-coding and computer-use quality (The New Stack; SD Times; 9to5Mac; aiweekly; The Next Web, as reported). The telling detail is not the price cut — the radar has logged those for months — it is that a frontier lab now ships its newest model as a selectable carrier inside its rivals' platforms. The model layer is so thoroughly commoditized that the models themselves flow through each other's dispatches; which means the durable value, and the durable lock-in, is no longer the model and no longer even the gateway — it is the routing control plane that assigns, observes, and defaults.
  • The answer — own the control plane; let models compete for your work. Run the assignment on infrastructure and policy you control (open-source model-routing/gateway layers, your own evals as the definition of "best"); keep the routing logs, the per-task model-choice history and the evaluation suites in a portable store you own; and maintain at least one model path that bypasses every vendor's routing product — a direct route you can run if a vendor's roster is re-ordered against you. This is the September floor, made one level more precise: it is not enough to treat the model as swappable; you must own the thing that does the swapping. A gateway you own with a vendor's dispatcher bolted on top is still a vendor-owned decision — the swap has just moved up a layer.

The strategic core: multi-model routing is the right architecture and the inevitable one — and precisely because it is inevitable, the model-sellers are racing to own the dispatcher rather than merely supply carriers to it. Owning the dispatcher is worth more than owning any model, because the dispatcher captures the one thing a single model never can: your choice among all of them, plus the telemetry of how you choose. The market makes the temptation concrete and gentle: the vendor's dispatcher is free, clever, genuinely good, and — the masterstroke — booked to the vendor's own competitors, so adopting it feels like embracing openness rather than surrendering it. The board translation: treat the routing layer as a core system you own, not a convenience you accept. The failure mode is exact: a house that owns its data, keeps a second supplier, and even calls itself "multi-model" — while letting one model-seller's agent make every routing decision and hold every routing log — has not escaped lock-in; it has relocated lock-in to the one layer that governs all the others, and gift-wrapped the move as choice.

Why "the dispatcher" is the discipline that matters now

In September the lock-in had migrated off the model and onto the foundation. This autumn it migrated one layer higher still — onto the dispatcher that assigns work among models — and the model-sellers moved to own it first, dressing the capture as openness. Google's Gemini agent routing to Claude, and Anthropic's Fable 5.2 selling through Google Cloud, are the same signal read two ways: the models are now so commoditized they flow through each other's dispatches, so the value is the dispatch. A multi-model strategy that does not own its own routing control plane has not defeated lock-in; it has handed one model-seller the desk from which every model is assigned — and been grateful for the rivals listed on the roster.

How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now sharpened to own the layer that decides what you run. On a legacy estate, the instinct after a hard year of governance is to accept the vendor's free, clever dispatcher, because building and running your own router sounds like undifferentiated heavy lifting — and the vendor's dispatch even books your rivals, so it feels safely open. Under the dispatcher that is exactly the reflex to check: the routing control plane is not undifferentiated; it is the single most leveraged layer you own, because it governs cost, data exposure, and model choice all at once. The retrofit is own, log, bypass. Own the dispatch: run the assignment on an open gateway and a policy you control, with your own evals as the definition of "best." Log in your own hall: keep the per-task model-choice records, the traffic history and the evals in a portable store, never only in a vendor's agent. Bypass: keep one direct model path that runs through no vendor's dispatch. And keep the whole refrain: the model is a rented stove, now so cheap a bigger one draws a shrug; the knowledge is the larder; the foundation is the ground you must own; the gate is the one door; the passport names each agent; the census is the roll; the inspector asks for it; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what an agent can reach; the bell discloses when your own agent slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why your name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each advising agent eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; the foundation is why you own the ground — and the dispatcher is why, having built the floor that lets you swap models, you must own the desk that assigns them: own the dispatch, keep the logbook, keep one bypass route, and never let a model-seller be your dispatcher, however many rival carriers it offers to hand your work to.

7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

  1. Own the dispatch — run the assignment on a layer you control, and make the models compete for your work. Multi-model routing is the right architecture and already the default (average models per account ~2.1 → ~4.7 in a year; IDC projects ~70% of top firms on dynamic routing by 2028), but the value is captured only if you run the dispatcher, not a model-seller. This month, route your top few AI workloads through an open gateway/router you operate, with your own evals as the definition of "best," and explicitly decline to let any one model vendor's agent make the assignment for you. Report one number to the board: the share of critical AI jobs whose model is chosen by a layer you own versus by a vendor's routing product.
  2. Keep the logbook — hold your routing records, evals, and traffic history in your own larder. The dispatcher hears everything — every prompt, job type, and price tolerance — so whoever holds its logs holds a map of your business. This month, ensure the per-task model-choice records, the evaluation suites that define "best," and your routing/traffic history are stored in a portable form you own, never only inside a vendor's agent, and could be re-pointed to a different router in a week. Report the share of your routing telemetry and evals that are portable by design and held outside any single model-seller's control plane.
  3. Keep a bypass route — maintain one model path that runs through no vendor's dispatch. A yard you can be shut out of is not yours; the 6% who could switch cleanly are the ones who kept a route open. This month, stand up and actually exercise one direct model path that bypasses every vendor routing product — a route you can run under load if a vendor's roster is re-ordered, degraded, or dropped against you — and measure the time to fail over to it. Report the measured time-to-bypass and a dated plan to extend it.
