The Inspector

Tuesday, 1 September 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

Yesterday the radar took the census — the roll that counts every agent you run. This morning the figure it was preparing for walks up to the door: the inspector from the capital, asking you to produce the roll and prove it. The EU AI Act's general-purpose-AI obligations became enforceable on 2 August, and on 29 August 2026 the Commission's AI Office sent its first formal Requests for Information to frontier model providers (reported: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) on model security, external evaluations, and post-market monitoring — backed by fines up to 3% of global revenue. When the inspector asks to see our books — not our intentions, our records — can we produce them this afternoon, agent by agent and log by log, or do we discover in the worst possible room that the census was never taken?

1 · Executive Summary

For weeks this radar assembled an agentic workforce and, yesterday, learned to count it. This morning the governing question changes hands: it is no longer do we know how many agents we run? but "can we prove it to someone with the power to fine us?" The answer arrived this week as a hard date.

1. The inspector is at the door. The EU AI Act's GPAI rules became enforceable on 2 August 2026, and on 29 August the Commission's AI Office issued its first formal Requests for Information to frontier providers (reported: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) on model security, independent external evaluations and post-market monitoring. Fines run up to 3% of global revenue; legacy models must comply by 2 August 2027. Compliance is now a letter with a deadline (as reported; several primary pages relayed via search summaries).

2. The books must exist before they are asked for. Article 12 requires reconstructable logs; Article 50 requires disclosure of AI interaction and labeling of AI-generated content. The census (yesterday) is what lets you answer agent-by-agent; the inspection is what tests whether you actually took it. The US moved in the opposite posture — a June 2026 executive order set a voluntary frontier framework and a separate order challenging state AI laws — leaving multinationals managing to the stricter rule, Brussels.

3. The pressure that makes it bite — record spend, flat returns. McKinsey's new State of AI (released ~25 Aug): 40% of $1B+ organizations now scaling AI agents (up from 27%), yet only ~6% attribute significant earnings impact to AI and ~37% any EBIT — "about the same" as 2025. Gartner puts 2026 AI spend at ~$2.59T (+47%). An avoidable fine lands on a P&L with no cushion.

Bottom line: the week that built and counted the workforce ends with the authority that will hold you to it. When the inspector asks for your records — not your roadmap, your logs — "we're committed to responsible AI" is not an answer; the register, the reconstructable log, and the named owner per agent are. Keep the books before they are asked for — because the exam is graded on the records you have, not the intentions you describe.

2 · Allegory — The Inspector

The city had, at last, taken its census — every worker named, every owner known, every key logged. For a season the roll sat in the treasury, complete and quiet, and the city told itself it was ready. Then one grey morning a rider came from the capital. She carried no army, only a writ. She was the inspector, and her question was not are you a good city but "Show me your books. For the three workers who touched the granary last month, produce the record — who owned each, what door each key opened, and what each one did. You have until the assizes." She wanted the ledger, opened to the page, on demand — not the mayor's speech about responsible governance.

The city that had taken the census laid three names, three owners, three logs on the table before noon; the inspector nodded and rode on. But the city that had only admired its growth stood in the great hall with nothing to hand her — not because it had done wrong, but because it could not show that it hadn't. Its intentions were spotless and entirely unprovable, and an inspector does not grade intentions. She set the fine at a share of everything the city earned. And the cruelty was the timing: that same city's harvest had come in thin — it had spent more than ever and its granaries were barely fuller — so a fine it could have avoided for a clerk's wages landed on a purse with nothing spare. The households that kept their footing had done the quiet thing long before the rider was on the road: they kept the books as they went. You do not pass an inspection you prepare for after the letter arrives. You pass the one you were already keeping books for.

The question it forces: the inspector's letter is already on the road; for the frontier labs it has arrived. If a regulator asked us this afternoon to produce, for one agent that touched customer data last month, the reconstructable log of what it did, the human who owns it, and the evidence it was evaluated and monitored — could we lay it on the table before the day is out? Or would we be the city with spotless intentions and no books? Are we keeping the records the inspection will ask for, or only hoping we are never asked?

3 · Technical — Keeping the Books Before the Inspector Asks

The inspector arrived: EU AI Act GPAI obligations enforceable since 2 Aug 2026; first formal RFIs sent 29 Aug to frontier providers (reported: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) on model security, external evaluations and post-market monitoring; fines up to 3% of global revenue; legacy models compliant by 2 Aug 2027. The RFI targets the model providers first, but Article 12 reconstructable logging and Article 50 transparency flow downstream to every deployer. The books are emitted, not written: compliance is a byproduct — every agent action writes an immutable, reconstructable log tied to a named owner and a scoped permission, and every customer-facing surface discloses and labels by default. If your evidence is a spreadsheet a human updates, you have a promise, not compliance. The pressure is economic: Gartner — 2026 AI spend ~$2.59T (+47%); McKinsey — only ~6% see significant earnings impact, ~37% any EBIT, "about the same" as 2025. No margin to absorb an avoidable fine.

On legacy: the temptation is to bolt compliance on at the end — ship the agents, write the policy later, reconstruct evidence if anyone asks. Enforcement made that the expensive path. Emit, don't reconstruct: wire Article-12-grade logging, ownership metadata and scoped permissions into every agent as it runs. Evidence: keep independent evaluation and live post-market monitoring on file for every consequential model. Disclose: make Article-50 disclosure and labeling the default on customer-facing surfaces. The census is the roll; the inspector is who asks you to produce it and prove every entry on it.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Emit the evidence. Wire Article-12-grade reconstructable logging, named-owner metadata and scoped permissions into every production agent so the record exists as it runs — and confirm you could produce, for one agent that touched regulated data, the log-owner-evaluation-monitoring bundle on demand. If that takes more than an afternoon, the gap is the finding. Report one number: the share of production agents that emit audit-grade evidence automatically.

2. Manage to the stricter rule. The EU is enforcing while the US goes voluntary; a multinational is exposed to Brussels regardless. Map every model to the strictest regime its footprint touches, hold independent evaluation and live post-market monitoring on file for every consequential one, and make Article-50 disclosure and labeling the default on customer-facing surfaces. The runway closes 2 Aug 2027 for legacy models — put a dated remediation plan against each.

3. Price the fine into the ROI case. With record spend and ~6% seeing real earnings impact, an avoidable penalty at a share of global revenue is pure margin destruction. Measure compliance readiness per agent the way you measure its return, and report it beside ROI. Report two numbers next quarter: the share of consequential models with evaluation-and-monitoring evidence on file, and the share of customer-facing agents disclosing and labeling as required.
