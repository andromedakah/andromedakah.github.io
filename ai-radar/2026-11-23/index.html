The cheap, swappable stove of the last edition did exactly what a cheap stove does — everyone cooked more. The board question is no longer how good our best agent is, or even whether we own the ground. It is: does every working hand on our premises — ours, our vendors', and the ones nobody remembers hiring — carry a badge from one office we control, tied to a named master, taken back the hour the work ends?

1 · Executive Summary 90-second read

For a long year the radar built the governed house one discipline at a time — name every hand (the livery), keep one book (the steward), put your name on the bond (the surety), check whose wage it draws (the second wage), send it to reap (the two harvests), hand it a scoped purse (the purse) — and then stepped back and looked at the ground the whole house stands on (the foundation). This morning the question is one of sheer headcount. The agents are no longer a handful of named servants you can picture. They are a workforce, and this week one vendor put a number on it and offered the enterprise a front-desk answer.

The news — the agents became staff, and someone counted them. At Microsoft Ignite 2026 (17 November 2026), Nadella said "we already have 50 million agents in Agent 365" and pitched Agent 365 as the control plane for every agent an organization runs — Microsoft-built, open-source, or third-party. Microsoft Entra Agent ID now issues a managed identity to discovered local agents (GitHub Copilot CLI, Claude Code) and Microsoft cloud agents, with automated agent-identity lifecycle management reported GA; governance extended to third-party agents from Google, Salesforce, Databricks, Snowflake, Anthropic and OpenAI via secretless workload-identity federation; agents can receive an email address, a Teams presence and a seat on the org chart; and "Agentic Users" (public preview) let an agent join a team under its own identity, @-mentionable, with an accountable human owner (Microsoft; AV Network; Cloud Wars; Windows Forum; Petri, as reported). The whole announcement is a badge office built at enterprise scale, and it exists because the agents outgrew the ability to govern them by hand.

The reason — the arithmetic turned against you while you weren't counting. In the average enterprise, non-human identities (service accounts, bots, and now agents) outnumber human identities by a reported ~144 to 1, up from ~92:1 a year earlier; NHIs already outnumber humans in 83% of organizations, but only 21% have governance controls for them; 91% of organizations use AI agents, yet only ~10% have a well-developed strategy for managing non-human identities, and ~78% have no formally adopted NHI-management policy (JumpCloud Q3 2026; Cloud Security Alliance; NHI Management Group, as reported). This is the direct, predictable consequence of the stove getting cheap: Anthropic cut Claude Fable to $8/$40 on 9 November — a reported ~20% price drop from Fable 5.1's $10/$50 (VentureBeat; Yahoo Finance, as reported), OpenAI's GPT-6.2 line reached GA on 17 November, and when intelligence is cheap and swappable, you don't run one careful agent — you run thousands, from a dozen vendors, and then you lose count. The sprawl is not a failure of discipline; it is the bill for commoditization, and it arrives as a flood of un-badged workers.

The stakes — one badge office, a named master on every badge, and a badge you take back. The move re-uses the identity discipline the radar has preached all year and aims it at scale. First, one badge office, not twelve — issue every agent's identity from a single authority you control, human and non-human alike, including the third-party and laptop agents, so there is one place to see them and one place to revoke. Second, a named master on every badge — no agent gets an identity without an accountable human owner and a scoped mandate; a seat on the org chart does not make an agent self-governing, it makes it your employee's responsibility. Third, joiner-mover-leaver, or the badge is a liability — provision on hire, re-scope on change, and above all revoke on exit; the breach is never the badge you issued, it is the dead agent's badge that still opens doors because no one took it back. Do it now, because the 144-to-1 gap compounds every week the stove gets cheaper.

2 · Allegory of the Day

🖋️ "The Badge"

Topic: This morning the frame turns from the ground the house stands on to the sheer number of hands now working in it. At Microsoft Ignite 2026 (17 November) Satya Nadella said "we already have 50 million agents in Agent 365" and unveiled a control plane that issues a managed corporate identity — an Entra Agent ID — to every agent an enterprise runs, including discovered local agents (GitHub Copilot CLI and Claude Code) and third-party agents from Google, Salesforce, Databricks, Snowflake, Anthropic and OpenAI, with agents receiving an email address, a Teams presence and an org-chart seat, and "Agentic Users" joining a team under their own identity with an accountable owner (Microsoft; AV Network; Cloud Wars; Windows Forum, as reported). It lands because the arithmetic has turned: non-human identities now outnumber human ones by a reported ~144 to 1 in the average enterprise (up from ~92:1), they outnumber humans in 83% of organizations while only 21% govern them, and 91% of organizations use agents while only ~10% have a strategy to manage their identities (JumpCloud Q3 2026; Cloud Security Alliance, as reported). The badge allegory is the radar's own illustration.

There was a great house that had, at long last, put its own servants in order. It had named every hand and cut it a livery, kept one honest book, put the master's name on the bond, asked whose second coin each adviser took, sent the servants to reap, handed each a scoped purse, and then dug down and made sure it owned the very ground the house stood on. By any fair reckoning the household was a model to the whole valley. The master knew his servants by name, could picture every one of them, and slept the sleep of a man who has his house in hand.

Then, one grey morning, the steward came to him with a tally and a troubled face. "Master," he said, "I have tried to count the hands at work in the house, and I cannot. Your own servants I know — there are forty of them, liveried and named. But the hall is full of others. The glazier's two apprentices are here, and the neighbour's clerk doing our sums, and a dozen day-labourers from the hiring fair, and some hired fellows from six different trading companies who came to help with the harvest and simply… stayed. And this morning I found three I have never seen, working the back rooms, who told me they came in with the carpenter's own writing-desk and started making themselves useful. Master — for every one of your forty named servants, there are near a hundred and fifty other hands in this house, and I do not know whose they are, who let them in, or whether they will be here tomorrow."

And the master went pale, because he understood at once what the steward had not quite said. It was not that the strangers were wicked. Most were honest workers doing honest tasks, and the house ran better for them. It was that he had built his whole discipline for a household of forty he could picture, and he now had a workforce of thousands he could not — and his liveries, his book, his bond, every control he was so proud of, governed the forty and said nothing at all about the six thousand. His census counted his own. His inspector could produce the roll of his own. But the hall belonged, by headcount, to other men's servants and to strangers who had wandered in, and he could neither name them nor, on the day one went bad, find the one and stop it.

Now, this flood had a cause, and the master knew it, for it was his own economy. The fire had gotten cheap — a cheaper stove every fortnight, each one swappable in an afternoon — and when fire is cheap you do not cook one careful dish with one careful cook; you light every hearth and take on every hand the hiring fair will send, because why not, at these prices. The cheap stove had filled his house with cooks. That was not a thing to mourn; it was the house winning the game it had set out to win. But a kitchen with a hundred and fifty cooks to every one you can name is not governed by knowing your forty well. It is governed, or not, by what happens at the door.

So the great guild of the valley came to him that week with an offer, and it was the right one. "Build one badge office at your front gate," they said, "and let no hand work in this house without a badge struck there — your own servants, the neighbour's clerk, the trading companies' hired men, even the three who came in on a writing-desk. One office. One place that knows every working hand by a token it issued, and one place that can call that token back." And they showed him the three things that made a badge worth more than a name scrawled on a list.

The first: one office, not twelve. The danger was never that his servants had identities; it was that every department had been minting its own — the kitchen vouching for its cooks, the stable for its grooms, the trading companies for their own men — so that no one person could see, let alone revoke, the whole hall at once. The badge office was one authority that issued identity to all of them, his and the strangers' alike, so there was a single place to look and a single place to stop a hand.

The second: a named master on every badge. No badge was struck without the name of a person who answered for it. The neighbour's clerk could work in the house — but his badge carried the name of the steward who had let him in and would answer for what he did. Even the servants who now had a desk and a letterbox and a line on the household chart — who looked, for all the world, like members of the family — carried a badge that said, in small letters, this hand answers to a human, not to itself. A seat at the table did not make a servant its own master. It made it someone's responsibility.

And the third, the one the careless houses skipped: take the badge back the hour the work ends. It was easy to strike a badge on the day a hand arrived. The discipline — the whole discipline — was to pull it the day the hand left. For the breach, when it came, was never the honest worker you had badged. It was the day-labourer who finished the harvest three months ago and whose badge still opened the cellar, because no one had thought to take it back; or the trading company's man, long recalled to his own house, whose token still worked your locks. A badge you issue and never revoke is not a control. It is a key left under the mat, with the name of a man who is no longer here.

Here is the part that should keep a master awake. The guild's badge office was a fine thing, and he took it gladly — but the moment he had it, he saw that the badge was only as good as the hand that pulled it. A house could stand up a magnificent front desk, badge all six thousand, give the cleverest of them desks and letterboxes and seats on the chart, and still be undone by the one shadow worker it never badged and the one dead man's badge it never revoked. The badge did not govern the agent. It made the agent governable — and only if the office behind it actually knew its count and actually took its badges back. The master who mistook issuing badges for governing the badged had merely given his strangers better credentials.

The moral, stated plainly: the model is a rented stove, now so cheap that a bigger one draws a shrug; the knowledge is the larder; the ground is the foundation you must own; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the census counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the keyring is which key each agent holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the tide table is the quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is the papers that trace the work; the errand bounds what a clever servant can reach; the bell rings the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm; the looking-glass is why you measure by an honest outside light; the livery is why you name every hand; the steward is why you keep one governed office; the surety is why the master's name is on the bond; the second wage is why you ask whose bread each adviser eats; the two harvests is why you send it to reap; the purse is why you fund it like an employee; the foundation is why you own the ground it stands on — and the badge is why, the morning your hands outnumber your memory a hundred and fifty to one, you stop trying to know them all and instead make one office that knows them for you: strike every working hand a badge from a single authority you control — your own, your vendors', and the strays — put a named human master on each badge, and take the badge back the hour the work ends, so that the shadow worker is found and the dead man's badge is dead. A house that badges only the servants it can already name has governed the hall it could see and handed the run of the house to the hall it could not.

The question it forces: We can name our own forty — but can we name the six thousand? Does every working hand on our premises — ours, our vendors', and the ones no one remembers hiring — carry a badge from one office we control, with a named human master on it and a lifecycle that takes it back the hour the work ends? Or have we, while congratulating ourselves on knowing our own servants well, let a hundred and fifty un-badged hands to every one of ours work the back rooms of our house, each with a key we never issued and cannot revoke?

3 · C-Level Engagement — Questions by Sector

🌐 Cross-sector (any boardroom)
  • Do we know our own version of the 144-to-1? Non-human identities now outnumber humans by a reported ~144:1 in the average enterprise, yet only 21% of organizations govern them (JumpCloud Q3 2026; CSA, as reported). Can we produce, today, a single count of every agent and service identity operating in our estate — ours, our vendors', and the ones running on employee laptops — and the name of the human master accountable for each? If we cannot count them, we are not governing them.
  • Is identity issued from one office, or minted in twelve? Ignite's pitch was a single control plane that badges Microsoft, open-source, third-party and local agents (Claude Code, GitHub Copilot CLI) alike (Microsoft, as reported). Is there one authority that issues and can revoke every agent identity we run — or does each team, SaaS platform and vendor vouch for its own, so that no one place can see the whole hall or stop a single hand?
  • Have we tested the leaver, not just the joiner? The breach is the dead agent's badge that still opens doors. Can we show that when an agent is decommissioned, a project ends, or an employee who spun up a laptop agent departs, the agent's credentials are actually revoked — with an audited time-to-revoke — rather than quietly surviving as a key no one remembers issuing?
🏦 Financial Services
  • Your regulators already treat privileged access and segregation of duties as examined controls, and agents now hold both. Can we give our examiners an inventory of non-human identities with their entitlements, owners and last-used dates — and show that an agent with payment or trading reach carries a scoped mandate, a named accountable human, and a tested kill/revoke — or are tens of thousands of service and agent identities operating below the line our access reviews actually inspect?
  • Agents are joining workflows under their own identity now (Ignite "Agentic Users," as reported). When an agent acts, does our audit trail attribute the action to that agent's distinct identity and to the human master who owns it — so that a bad transaction has a name on it — or does agent activity collapse into a shared service account that makes accountability unprovable?
🧬 Healthcare / Life Sciences
  • Agents touch PHI, clinical systems and validated workflows, and each needs a least-privilege identity. Is every agent with reach into patient data issued from our identity authority with scoped, auditable access — and revoked on decommission — or have clinical and research teams stood up ungoverned agents against our data because the cheap model made it trivial to do so?
  • Validation and 21 CFR Part 11-style accountability assume a known actor. Can we name the accountable human owner for every agent operating in a GxP or clinical context, and prove its identity lifecycle is controlled, or is "the agent did it" an answer our quality system cannot accept?
🏭 Manufacturing / Industrials
  • You already run badge-and-lockout discipline for people and contractors on the floor; agents are now contractors in your systems. Have we applied the same badge-in/badge-out rule to software agents — one issuing authority, a named owner, revoke-on-exit — or do vendor and integrator agents retain standing access to MES, PLM and OT-adjacent systems long after the project that justified them ended?
  • Supplier agents are arriving inside your estate (third-party agents from Salesforce, Databricks, Snowflake and others can now be badged, as reported). Where a supplier's agent operates in our systems, does it carry an identity we issued and can revoke, scoped to the task — or are we trusting the supplier's own vouching for a hand working inside our walls?
🛒 Retail / Consumer
  • Cheap inference means you will run more agents across search, pricing, support and fulfillment, not fewer (Claude Fable 5.2 at $8/$40, a ~20% cut; GPT-6.2 GA, as reported). As the agent headcount scales with the falling price, is our identity plane scaling with it — one office, scoped mandates, revoke-on-exit — or are we accumulating a long tail of promotional, seasonal and experimental agents that never got offboarded?
  • Peak season multiplies both agents and risk. Do the surge agents we spin up for a sales event get badged from the same authority and revoked when the event ends — or do they become permanent shadow workers with standing access to checkout, inventory and customer data?
🏛️ Public Sector / Regulated
  • Accountability and auditability are statutory here, and an un-owned agent is an accountability gap. Can we attach a named, accountable official to every agent operating in a public service, with an identity we issue, scope and revoke — and reconcile that against the EU AI Act transparency and record-keeping duties already in force (high-risk regime live since 2 August 2026)?
  • Sprawl is a sovereignty and continuity risk, not just a security one. Do we have a complete, current register of the non-human identities operating across public systems — including vendor and local agents — or is the real headcount of who (and what) can act on citizen data unknown to the officials answerable for it?

4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Foundation to the Badge

Read the stack once more, now from the seat of the executive who has stopped asking do we own the ground? and started asking the question none of the prior controls answer at scale: how many hands are actually working in this house, whose are they, and can I revoke any one of them? The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the keyring is which key it holds; the locksmith reads your own locks; the provenance traces the work; the errand bounds its reach; the bell discloses when it slips; the livery dresses it; the steward keeps one office; the surety puts your name on the bond; the second wage asks whose interest it serves; the two harvests sends it to reap; the purse funds it; the foundation is the ground it stands on. Every one of those was designed for a workforce you could picture. This week a vendor priced the workforce at fifty million and said the obvious thing: you can no longer govern by knowing them — you govern by issuing, owning and revoking identity at scale, from one office, for every hand including the ones you didn't hire. The point is blunt: the control that now decides your exposure is not how good your best agent is or even whether you own your infrastructure — it is whether every agent on your premises has a badge from one authority you control, a named human master, and a lifecycle that takes the badge back.

  • The news — the agents became staff, and the control plane is a badge office. At Microsoft Ignite 2026 (17 November), Nadella said "we already have 50 million agents in Agent 365" and positioned Agent 365 as the control plane for every agent an org runs. Microsoft Entra Agent ID now issues managed identities to discovered local agents (GitHub Copilot CLI, Claude Code) and Microsoft cloud agents, with automated agent-identity lifecycle management reported GA; governance extends to third-party agents from Google, Salesforce, Databricks, Snowflake, Anthropic and OpenAI via secretless workload-identity federation; agents can receive an email address, a Teams presence and an org-chart entry; and "Agentic Users" (public preview) join a team under their own identity, @-mentionable, with an accountable human owner (Microsoft; AV Network; Cloud Wars; Windows Forum; Petri, as reported). Three engineering realities follow. First, identity must be centralized or it is not revocable — the value of the badge office is a single authority that can see and kill every agent credential; identity minted per-team or per-SaaS is identity you cannot revoke in one motion. Second, secretless, federated identity beats long-lived keys — the move to workload-identity federation (no shared secret to leak, a short-lived token tied to a verifiable identity) is the right primitive for a workforce this large; standing API keys are the dead man's badge waiting to happen. Third, local and third-party agents are the hard part — the agents on employee laptops (Claude Code, Copilot CLI) and the ones your vendors bring are exactly the ones your directory never saw, which is why "discovered local agents" and third-party federation, not first-party cloud agents, are where the governance actually bites.
  • The reason — the arithmetic is the story, and it is the bill for commoditization. Non-human identities now outnumber human ones by a reported ~144:1 in the average enterprise (up from ~92:1 a year earlier); NHIs outnumber humans in 83% of organizations but only 21% have governance controls; 91% of organizations use AI agents while only ~10% have a well-developed NHI-management strategy and ~78% have no formally adopted NHI policy (JumpCloud Q3 2026; Cloud Security Alliance; NHI Management Group, as reported); Gartner still projects 40% of enterprise apps embed task-specific agents by year-end 2026 (up from <5% in 2025). The causal chain is the radar's own through-line: the stove commoditized (Claude Fable 5.2 at a reported $8/$40 on 9 November, a ~20% cut from $10/$50; OpenAI's GPT-6.2 GA on 17 November), and cheap, swappable intelligence does not produce one well-governed agent — it produces thousands, spun up by every team, embedded by every SaaS vendor, running on every developer's laptop. Agent sprawl is not a discipline failure; it is the predictable second-order effect of making the input nearly free. The governance gap (144:1 identities, 21% governed) is simply the distance between how fast you can create agents and how fast you can issue, own and revoke their identities — and that gap widens every time the price drops.
  • The answer — one badge office, a named master on every badge, revoke-on-exit. Issue every agent identity from a single authority you control (human and non-human, first-party, third-party and local); bind each agent to an accountable human owner and a scoped, least-privilege mandate; and automate the full joiner-mover-leaver lifecycle so a credential is provisioned on creation, re-scoped on change, and revoked on decommission — with short-lived, federated, secretless credentials rather than standing keys, and an auditable time-to-revoke. This is the identity discipline the radar built for the livery, the surety and the purse, now industrialized for a workforce two orders of magnitude larger than your headcount. The same seam the radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now reads: be able to count every hand, name its master, and take back its badge.

The strategic core: the agent headcount has crossed the line where you govern by acquaintance; from here you govern by identity infrastructure, or not at all. The temptation is to treat Ignite's badge office as the whole answer — buy the control plane, badge the fifty million, declare victory. But the badge does not govern the agent; it makes the agent governable, and only if the office behind it knows its true count and actually revokes. The board translation: stand up one identity authority for every agent, put a human's name on each, and prove you can take a badge back — then measure the shadow agents you don't yet badge and the stale credentials you haven't yet killed. The failure mode is exact: a house that buys the finest badge office, gives its cleverest agents desks and org-chart seats, and still never reconciles its 144-to-1 or revokes a departed agent's credentials, has not governed its workforce — it has issued its strangers better papers.

Why "the badge" is the discipline that matters now

For a long year the worry was whether each agent was named, scoped, logged, loyal, reaping and funded — and then whether you owned the ground it stood on. This week the arithmetic moved the question underneath all of it: the agents became a workforce too large to govern by acquaintance, and a vendor priced it at fifty million and offered a front desk. The 144-to-1 ratio and the 21%-govern-them gap prove that identity creation has outrun identity governance; Ignite's Agent ID, Agentic Users and third-party federation prove the industry's answer is a single badge office. Governance that stopped at the agents you can name will not govern the hall you cannot. The estates that keep their standing will issue every agent's identity from one authority, put a named master on each, and prove they can revoke — and will treat the badge not as the end of governance but as the thing that finally makes governance possible at scale.

How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — be able to account for, and act on, what you run — now sharpened to be able to count every hand, name its master, and take back its badge. On a legacy estate the agents did not arrive through one door; they accreted — a Copilot here, a vendor's embedded agent there, a developer's laptop tool, a half-forgotten pilot — each vouched for by whoever stood it up, none in a single register. That is the shadow workforce, and it is already larger than your named one. The retrofit is one office, one master, one lifecycle. One office: route every agent identity through a single authority (an identity provider / agent control plane you own), so there is one place to see and revoke. One master: refuse to issue or keep any agent credential without a named accountable human and a scoped, least-privilege mandate. One lifecycle: automate joiner-mover-leaver with short-lived, secretless, federated credentials, and prove revoke-on-exit with an audited time-to-revoke — because the dead agent's badge is the breach. 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

  1. Stand up one badge office — a single identity authority for every agent, and count your 144-to-1. You can no longer govern the agent workforce by knowing it; non-human identities outnumber your humans by a reported ~144:1 and only 21% of organizations govern them (JumpCloud Q3 2026, as reported). This month, route every agent identity you can through one authority you control (your IdP / an agent control plane), and produce a first full inventory of non-human identities across the estate — first-party, vendor-embedded, and local agents on employee laptops (Claude Code, Copilot CLI, and the like). Report one number to the board: your real non-human-to-human identity ratio, and the share of agents issued from a single revocable authority versus minted per-team or per-vendor.
  2. Put a named master on every badge — no agent without an accountable human and a scoped mandate. An org-chart seat and an email address do not make an agent self-governing (Ignite's "Agentic Users" still carry an accountable owner, as reported). This month, require that every agent identity carry a named human owner and a least-privilege, scoped mandate, and flag every agent credential that has neither — those are your shadow workers. Report the share of agents with a named accountable owner and a scoped mandate, and a dated plan to badge or retire the rest.
  3. Prove revoke-on-exit — automate joiner-mover-leaver, and kill the dead agent's badge. The breach is never the agent you governed; it is the departed agent's credential that still opens doors. This month, pick one high-reach class of agents, move it to short-lived, secretless, federated credentials, and run a real decommission test — measure the time-to-revoke when an agent is retired, a project ends, or a developer leaves — then hunt stale credentials across the estate. Report the measured time-to-revoke and the count of still-live credentials belonging to agents, projects or people that are already gone.
