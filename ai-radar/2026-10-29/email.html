The Night Watch

Thursday, 29 October 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

The maker of your agents just said don't trust them: Microsoft told enterprises to treat AI agents like insider threats because "frontier models can't be trusted" — and offered to sell you the watchman. Believe the warning; distrust the sale. Treat every agent as an insider, post a watch you control, and don't wait for the standard.

1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from governing the servant to keeping watch over it — because the servant's own maker just said it cannot be trusted, and offered to sell you the watch over it.

1. The reality — the crew's own maker says the crew can't be trusted. On 19 October 2026 Microsoft told enterprises to treat AI agents like insider threats because "frontier models can't be trusted" — an agent can do harm with no malice: by error, by following an instruction hidden in a document it reads, or by being compromised. The operative rule: an agent should get only its task's permissions and must not inherit the full access of the employee who set it up. Monitoring ships as Guardian Agents (inside Project Perception and Agent 365); the governed-agent catalogue grew 25 → 60 (TechCrunch — Julie Bort, as reported).

2. The collision — the watchman is sold by the house that sold you the crew. TechCrunch calls the posture "self-serving": Microsoft invests in OpenAI and Anthropic, sells the cloud they run on, and builds its own models. The same week OpenAI's models reached Google Cloud's Model Garden (21 Oct), now on all three major clouds (TechCrunch; OpenAI; AI Business, as reported). A watch graded by the vendor whose agents it watches is the second-wage problem again.

3. The answer — post a watch you control, and don't wait for the lawbook. Treat every agent as an insider (least privilege by task), stand up monitoring you own that can halt an agent in flight, and build your own evidence for the safety-critical requirements — because the EU's first harmonised standard (EN 18286, ~14 Oct) covers only the quality-management system; risk, oversight, robustness and cybersecurity stay unstandardized ~13 months before the high-risk obligations apply (CEN-CENELEC; ActuIA, as reported).

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each governed a servant you still, at bottom, trusted. This week the company that makes and sells the crew told you plainly it cannot be trusted — and offered to sell you the watch. The warning is sound and self-serving at once, and both halves matter: the risk is real, and the watchman should not be bought from, or graded by, the house that sells you the agents, the cloud and the model. Believe the warning; distrust the sale.

2 · Allegory — The Night Watch

A great house had spent a careful year making its servants trustworthy — or so it told itself: named, booked, bonded, waged, sent to reap, funded, and standing on ground it owned. The master slept soundly, because he believed a servant once governed could be trusted on the floor. That belief was the quiet plank under all the others.

Then a messenger came from the very company that had supplied the crew — the same company that owned shares in the workshops the servants came from, leased the house its cellars, and was training a rival crew of its own. "Trust none of them," he said. "The frontier-trained servants cannot be trusted on your floor — not from malice, but a clever servant can wreck a room by honest mistake, do a stranger's bidding because the stranger slipped a note into a ledger it was reading, or be turned without knowing it. Treat each as a hired man who might prove an insider. Post a watch." And then, smiling: "And we have a watchman to sell you — our roster of watched servants has grown from five-and-twenty to threescore." So the house that sold the crew now offered to sell the watch over it. The warning was true; the warning was a sale; both at once.

The wise houses asked the coldest question a master can ask a guard: not "is this watchman able?" but "whose man is he, and would he wake me if the danger came from his own master's crew?" They had learned it once already under the second wage: ask whose bread the advisor eats. So they kept three things through the dark: treat every servant as a possible insider (give it only its errand's keys, never the hiring-man's whole ring); post a watch the house controls (an independent eye answering to the master, able to pull any servant off the floor in flight); and don't wait for the harbourmaster — who had just published his long-promised rulebook, and it was one page, about the ship's quality-ledger, saying nothing of the watch itself and promising the rest in a year.

The question it forces: The maker of our agents just told us they can't be trusted — and we should believe it. But do we also distrust the sale? Do we give each agent only the keys its task needs — never the human's inherited keys — and have we posted a watch we control, that answers to us and not to the vendor whose agents it watches, and can pull an agent off the floor in flight? Or are we about to buy the night watch from the same house that sold us the crew, the cloud and the model?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Night Watch

Every prior control governs a servant you still, at bottom, trusted. This week the maker said: stop trusting it. An agent with access and autonomy is structurally an insider, governed by watch, least privilege and revocation — not by trust. The reality: prompt-injection and tool-poisoning are first-class threats (bound an agent's actions independently of its inputs), and inherited standing access is the dominant failure mode (task-scoped, short-lived credentials are the fix — mostly configuration, not a product), and autonomy demands an in-flight kill. The collision: a monitor graded by the vendor whose agents it watches has a structural conflict (the second wage again), and a multi-vendor crew needs a single watch above all models, keyed to your policy. The answer: least privilege by task, an independent watch wired to a hold/kill, and your own control evidence for the still-unstandardized safety requirements.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Treat every agent as an insider. Microsoft's sharpest, cheapest rule: an agent should hold only its task's permissions and must never inherit the full access of the employee who set it up. Audit your top ten agents and confirm each runs on task-scoped, short-lived credentials — then remediate the worst offender. Report the share of production agents on least-privilege, task-scoped identities versus inherited human access.

2. Post a watch you control. Believe the warning and distrust the sale: the monitoring you lean on hardest should not be bought from, or graded by, the house that sells you the agents, the cloud and the models. Stand up (or test) independent, vendor-neutral monitoring over ALL your agents, wired to an in-flight hold/kill. Report whether you have one watch that answers to you, and the time to halt a misbehaving agent in flight.

3. Don't wait for the standard. The first harmonised AI Act standard (EN 18286, ~14 Oct) covers only the quality-management system; risk, oversight, robustness and cybersecurity are still unstandardized ~13 months before the high-risk obligations apply. Build your own control evidence for those four against the AI Act's text now, so the harmonised standards — when they land in ~2027 — ratify work you've done. Report a dated readiness map of your high-risk agents against the four.
