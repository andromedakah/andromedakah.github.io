The Sea Trial

Monday, 2 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

The first value-weighted trial just put the whole fleet to sea on the realm's richest runs — and the best ship finished an eighth of the voyage, and foundered completely in the two richest harbours (GDPx: top model 12.0%; finance & legal 0%). The quay dazzles; the sea tells the truth. Run your own sea trial on your own richest runs before you load the cargo.

1 · Executive Summary

The axis moves from fitting out the ship to putting it to sea on the runs that pay — because the first value-weighted trial just scored the fleet, and the same vendors have never sold "the agent already does the work" harder.

1. The reality — the best ship made an eighth of the voyage. On 2 November 2026 SemiAnalysis launched GDPx, the first agentic benchmark weighted to economically valuable work — 206 tasks across 11 domains (chip design, cybersecurity, RSI, finance, law and more), 14 data partners. The top model, Claude Opus 5.5, scored 12.0%, and finance and legal returned 0% across all 13 models under pass/fail grading (SemiAnalysis; Techmeme; unite.ai, as reported). The demo at the quay is not the voyage.

2. The collision — adoption at full volume. Microsoft's FQ1'27 (reported 28 Oct) put Microsoft Cloud up 30% to $63.9B, with Nadella claiming daily Copilot users up ~50% QoQ and automated tasks nearly doubled since July; OpenAI and ServiceNow (28 Oct) expanded their pact to run agents through ServiceNow's 80B+ workflows a year; SAP promises 400 agents in its Autonomous Suite by year-end (Microsoft; ServiceNow/OpenAI; SAP, as reported). Sold by the demo, bought by the quarter.

3. The answer — run your own sea trial before you load the cargo. Put every agent through a staged trial on your own real, hard, high-value tasks; build the trial from the domains where the money and liability live; and keep a portable trial-ground and logbook you re-run on every model change — because the honest score is the one you measure, not the one on the box.

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each fit out the ship — the sea trial is the one test that asks whether it can actually sail the runs that pay. The first value-weighted trial answered: the proudest ship finished one part in eight, and foundered in the counting-house and the court. Buy ships — the fleet is real and improving — but never load the cargo on a vessel you have only watched from the quay.

2 · Allegory — The Sea Trial

A great estate had spent a careful year building the finest ship in the valley — crew named, one log kept, the master's name on the bond, the pilot's second wage checked, sent to trade and weighed, provisioned from a scoped purse, the dry-dock itself owned. At the quay she was a wonder: brass shining, sails cracking, and every shipwright swearing she could run any cargo to any port. And the whole valley was loud with the same promise — one yard boasting its ships on half again as many berths doing near twice the work, another pouring its vessels into tens of thousands of trading runs, a third promising four hundred new ships by Christmas.

Then the admiralty did something new. It did not test on the calm coastal hops where every hull looks able; it built an honest trial out of the realm's richest runs — the bullion packet to the counting-house, the sealed-brief to the court, eleven routes that actually paid the realm's bills — drew its charts from fourteen real trading houses, and sent the proudest ships into real weather. The log went quiet in the reading: the finest vessel in the world finished barely one part in eight of the voyage, and on the two richest runs not one ship came home at all — some turned back refusing the weather, the rest foundered with the cargo still in the hold. "She is a beautiful ship, and she cannot yet sail the runs that pay. A wise house does not mistake the first for the second."

So the wise houses learned three things: run the sea trial before you load the cargo (no ship carries anything that matters until it is driven hard on your water, against your real work, with the completion written down); trial her on the richest runs (prove her on the bullion and the sealed-brief, where the money and liability live, because that is where the fleet founders unseen); and keep your own trial-ground and logbook (your own staged run on your own cargo, re-sailed every time you take a new ship). The louder the valley cried the ships already did the work, the more a careful house walked past the brass and out to the trial-ground — for a fleet that is real and improving is exactly the one you are most tempted to trust untested.

The question it forces: The fleet has never looked better, and the whole valley is loading cargo on the strength of the brass. But have we run our own sea trial — a storm we staged, on our own richest run, with our own cargo in the hold — or are we trusting the bullion to a ship we have only ever admired at the quay, in the very week the first honest trial put the best ship in the world at an eighth of the voyage and at the bottom of the counting-house and the court?

3 · Technical — From the Foundation to the Sea Trial

Every prior control makes the ship seaworthy on paper; the sea trial is the only one that puts it to sea. A benchmark that saturates (coding, chat) flatters the fleet; a value-weighted, unsaturated trial on whole, real tasks tells you what you actually bought. The reality: GDPx grades whether the voyage finished, and the best model finished 12% (finance & legal 0% across 13 models) — a model that produces confident, well-formatted work while completing an eighth of the task is the most dangerous kind to deploy unwatched, and some zeros are safety-classifier refusals, not pure incapacity, so record failure mode, not just a number. The collision: adoption is not competence (daily users, tasks touched, workflows wired measure activity) and integration is not capability (pouring an agent into 80B workflows makes it reachable, not able) — the deeper agents are wired in on the demo, the more a value-weighted trial is the only thing standing between "deployed everywhere" and "trusted with nothing it can finish." The answer: a held-out trial on your own hardest, highest-value whole tasks, graded on completion and failure mode, re-run on every version, kept portable and owned.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Run the sea trial before you load the cargo. The first value-weighted trial put the best model at 12% and at 0% in finance and legal (GDPx), while the fleet sells "the agent already does the work." Put every agent on a high-value workflow through a staged trial on a held-out set of your own real, hard, whole tasks — grading completion and failure mode, not plausible output — and write the completion rate in a log the board sees. Report the share of high-value agent workflows that have passed a documented trial on your own tasks versus a vendor demo.

2. Trial her on the richest runs. GDPx's shock was that the 0% hid in the richest domains while the ships looked magnificent. Build your trial set from your highest-value, highest-liability, hardest whole tasks (the deal that closes, the contract that binds, the control that must fail safe), not the calm demos, and record per-domain how much your agents actually finish. Report the measured completion rate on your three hardest, most valuable task types — so you know your own 0% before it finds you.

3. Keep your own trial-ground and logbook. The admiralty's hallmark is the admiralty's; your cargo rides on your verdict, and the ship changes often. Stand up a portable, domain-specific evaluation you control, versioned and re-run whenever a model or agent updates, that distinguishes incapacity from refusal — so adoption numbers are never mistaken for competence. Report the existence and coverage of your owned trial, and a dated plan to gate every new agent through it.
