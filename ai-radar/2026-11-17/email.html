The Overseer

Tuesday, 17 November 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

The vendors stopped selling agents and started selling the governance of agents — IBM and Microsoft's "Imagine Enterprise" will build, run and govern your agents, fully managed if you like. The gap it fills is real. But governance is the deepest lock-in of all: the knowledge of your own house. Hire the instruments, keep the command; keep your book portable; exit-test governance itself.

1 · Executive Summary

A year of work taught the house to govern its own servants — name, count, bound, fund, ground. This morning the market offers to take the levers back, as a service.

1. The event — the vendors now sell governance, not just agents. On 10 November 2026 IBM and Microsoft launched "Imagine Enterprise" — a platform to build, run and govern AI agents for industry-specific work: IBM Consulting's reported 6,000+ prebuilt agents on Microsoft's Entra, Purview and Defender, self-managed, co-developed, or fully managed by IBM with forward-deployed engineers; no named production customer or independent governance test published yet (IBM; Microsoft; CIO Dive; Petri, as reported). It is a pattern — "the agent governance stack forming, four products in two weeks" — with Cisco shipping Frontier AI red-teaming and agent observability.

2. The reason it sells — the governance gap is real. Only ~1 in 5 (≈21%) enterprises have mature agent governance; ~79% lack policies spanning their full agent footprint and >$375M flowed into governance startups in a month; ~50% of agents are invisible to one another, and audits find 20–40% more agents than leadership expected (Deloitte; Salesforce; iEnable, as reported). A house drowning in ungoverned servants says yes to any overseer — meanwhile the model keeps commoditizing (Google Gemini 4 Hydrogen GA 12 Nov).

3. The answer — keep the roll in your own hand. Hire the instruments, keep the command (own the policy, identity authority and audit record, exportable); keep your own book portable so it leaves with you; and run the exit test on governance itself — if you dismissed the vendor Friday, could you still name, watch and stop every agent Monday?

Bottom line: the livery, steward, surety, second wage, two harvests, purse and foundation each put a lever in your hand — the overseer is the market's offer to hold them all for you. Governance is not a stove you swap or a floor you re-pour; it is the knowledge of your own house, kept or lost. Buy help counting your servants. Never let the man who sells you the servants become the only one who knows what they are.

2 · Allegory — The Overseer

A great house had spent a careful year learning to govern itself — every hand named and scoped, one honest book, the master's name on the bond, each advising servant's loyalty checked, the servants sent to reap, each handed a scoped purse, and the stove-seller no longer pouring its foundation. But the house had grown crowded: hundreds of servants where there had been a dozen, and the master had lost the count. His bailiff told him the truth — one house in five could even name all its servants; four in five had no rule covering the whole staff; half the servants no longer knew each other's faces; and a lantern-walk found a third again as many hands as the master thought he employed.

So when the great trading company's finest man came up the drive, the timing was perfect. He came not to sell a stove but with a single wagon carrying six thousand trained servants — and an overseer to run them all. "Do not trouble yourself with governing. Our overseer will name, count, watch and ring the bell, and keep the whole record for you — we will even run the ledger-room ourselves." The careful houses asked the colder question: not "is the overseer good?" but "whose book does he write in, and the day I send him away, what walks out with him?" And the answer chilled them: the book was the company's, the locks were the company's, and the overseer would leave carrying the only roll of who their servants were and what they'd done. They would have paid, for years, to rent back the one thing they'd learned to hold.

So the wise houses learned three things: hire the instruments, keep the command (let him carry the lantern and ring the bell, but the roll, the rules and the record stay in your strongbox); keep your own book, portable (a record you can only read inside the company's ledger-room is the company's, not yours); and an overseer you cannot dismiss is a master, not a servant — if you sent him away Friday, could your own people govern by Monday? A stove you swap, a floor you re-pour, but the knowledge of your own house is kept patiently or it is gone.

The question it forces: We spent a year learning to govern our own house. Now the company that sells us the servants offers to govern them for us, from a ledger-room it builds and locks inside our walls. Do we still own our governance — the roll, the rules, the record — in a form we could carry out the door tomorrow? Or have we, grateful and tired, let the overseer who alone knows our house become the master we must pay to keep?

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Walk your own lantern first. The gap is real (~21% mature, ~79% without full-footprint policy, ~50% invisible, audits finding 20–40% more agents than expected) — so don't let a vendor's inventory be the first authoritative count of your estate. Run an independent enumeration of every production agent — who deployed it, what it may touch, what it did — before any platform tells you theirs. Report: agents you can independently enumerate today versus agents found only when you looked.

2. Hire the instruments, keep the command. Buy the governance stack as instrumentation, not as a replacement for owning governance. For any platform you run or evaluate, put three things in a store you own and can export on an hour's notice: the policy, the identity authority, and the audit record — and confirm in writing the complete record leaves with you if you cancel. Report the share of your agent governance that is portable by design versus resident only in a vendor's layer.

3. Exit-test governance itself. The deepest lock-in is the knowledge of your own house; an overseer you cannot dismiss is a master. Pick your most agent-dense workflow and prove your own people could name, watch and stop every agent in it with the vendor removed — measure what breaks and how long it takes. Report the result and a dated plan to extend it to the next two workflows.
