# 🗓️ AI Tech Radar — The Looking-Glass

**Monday, 14 September 2026 · Cross-sector edition · Audience: C-level + Engineering**

> Yesterday the story was the locusts — you cannot outrun a swarm, so you close the gaps *before* it comes and field your own swarm to meet it. This morning the harder question is the one that decides whether any of that is real: **how do you *know* you are whole?** The uncomfortable answer arriving this week is that most houses know it the worst possible way — by asking themselves and liking the reply. **AvePoint's third annual "State of AI 2026" report (Osterman Research; 750 global IT leaders across financial services, healthcare and government) finds that 82.7% of leaders are confident they can prevent unauthorised data access — and that nearly nine in ten of those confident organisations were breached anyway; 88.4% suffered at least one AI-agent-related security breach in the past twelve months (most often data leakage, 50.1%, and manipulation by malicious or untrusted inputs, 49.6%), and GenAI breaches rose to 89.5% from 75.1% a year earlier (AvePoint / Osterman Research, as reported).** The finding a board must sit with is the *direction* of the correlation: **the more confident the organisation, the more likely it had already been breached** — 62% to 72% of even the high-confidence firms logged AI-related unauthorised-access incidents (PRNewswire, as reported). Confidence, in other words, is not a reading of your safety; this week it is an *inverse* one. The gap has a name — visibility. **The share of organisations that cannot even tell whether staff are using unsanctioned AI has nearly tripled, from 6.3% (2025) to 17.6% (2026), and 21.1% cannot account for unsanctioned *agent* activity at all, even as 46.9% of employees now lean on agents daily or weekly (AvePoint, as reported).** The board question is no longer "are we confident we're secure?" It is: **do we know we are whole because we *measured* it against an honest, outside light — telemetry, red-teaming, breach evidence — or because we looked in a glass that only ever flatters?**

## 1 · Executive Summary (90-second read)

Yesterday the radar told you that you cannot outrun the swarm, so you must be whole before it arrives and field your own agents to meet it. This morning the sharper, prior question lands: **how would you actually know whether you are whole — and the week's data says most organisations answer it by consulting their own confidence, which turns out to be the least reliable instrument in the house.**

**The event — the flattering glass.** **AvePoint's "State of AI 2026" (its third annual report, run with Osterman Research across 750 global IT leaders in financial services, healthcare and government) finds 88.4% of organisations suffered at least one AI-agent-related security breach in the past twelve months, and 89.5% a GenAI breach — up sharply from 75.1% in 2025 — with data leakage (50.1%) and manipulation by malicious or untrusted inputs (49.6%) the most common incidents (AvePoint / Osterman Research, as reported).** The paradox is the story: **82.7% of leaders say they are confident they can prevent unauthorised data access, yet nearly nine in ten of those confident firms were breached; the *more* confident the organisation, the *more* likely it had already been breached (62–72% of even high-confidence firms logged AI-related unauthorised-access incidents) (PRNewswire, as reported).** The mechanism is a blind spot that is widening, not closing: **organisations that cannot tell whether staff use unsanctioned AI nearly tripled (6.3% → 17.6%); 21.1% cannot account for unsanctioned agent activity; and 46.9% of employees now use agents daily or weekly (AvePoint, as reported).** AvePoint's Dana Simberkoff put the gap plainly: *"the biggest gaps are visibility, control, and trust — many organisations still don't know which agents are operating, what permissions they have, or how they're making decisions."*

**The reckoning — confidence is not a measurement.** The old assurance ritual — a leader asks "are we secure?", the team says "yes," the board records comfort — was quietly workable only while systems were legible and change was slow. Both conditions are gone. **You cannot assure what you cannot see, and the seeing is getting worse:** a fresh report this week counted **17,800 public AI add-ons across 6.7 million installations drawing instructions from unverified external sources — including "skills" impersonating Anthropic and OpenAI that can run arbitrary code (as reported)** — the concrete shape of the visibility gap the survey measures. The frontier keeps moving underneath it all: **Anthropic's Fable 5.1 topped the Real-SWE enterprise coding benchmark this week at 38.8% (ahead of OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra at 33.8% and Google's Gemini 3.8 Flash at 31.2%), and Anthropic told investors it expects a second straight quarter of positive adjusted operating income (~$559M on $11.5B revenue) (as reported)** — capability and adoption both climbing while the average estate's ability to *see* its own agents falls behind. A house that measures its safety by its confidence is, structurally, measuring the one thing the data says is anti-correlated with safety.

**The stakes — throw out the flattering glass; measure by an honest light.** For a board the lesson is exact. **The comfort you feel is not evidence; only an honest, outside measure is.** Two moves follow. First, **replace self-assessed confidence with instrumented truth** — continuous visibility into which agents are running (sanctioned and shadow), what they can reach, and what they actually do — because the survey's central finding is that confidence rises even as visibility falls. The tooling to do it is arriving fast: this week **CrowdStrike launched Falcon Guardian to discover known and shadow AI agents across Windows and macOS, trace prompts through tool calls to downstream system actions, and block agents that are not explicitly approved (as reported)** — the "honest light" made product. Second, **make outside evidence the standard of proof**: independent red-teaming, breach-and-near-miss data, and third-party audit, governed at one control point (MCP's 2026-07-28 Enterprise-Managed Authorization, now stable and adopted by Anthropic, Microsoft and Okta — the place to see and revoke). And govern the mirror itself: **Anthropic, OpenAI and Google DeepMind have been meeting since July to stand up an industry-led AI standards body (Dario Amodei driving, Sam Altman backing) (as reported)** — a bid to set the honest measure before regulators or incidents set it for them.

1. **The event — confidence is inversely correlated with safety.** **88.4% of organisations had an AI-agent breach and 89.5% a GenAI breach in the past year (up from 75.1%); yet 82.7% feel confident, and the *more* confident, the *more* likely already breached (62–72% of high-confidence firms hit) (AvePoint / Osterman Research; PRNewswire, as reported).** The glass flatters.
2. **The reckoning — the blind spot is widening.** **Organisations that cannot see unsanctioned AI use nearly tripled (6.3% → 17.6%); 21.1% cannot account for unsanctioned agent activity; 46.9% of staff use agents weekly; 17,800 unverified AI add-ons span 6.7M installs, some impersonating vendors and able to run code (AvePoint; add-on report, as reported).** You cannot assure what you cannot see.

Tools are shipping (CrowdStrike Falcon Guardian). Confidence is not evidence.

**Bottom line:** the locusts were about the swarm at your walls; the looking-glass is about the instrument you use to tell yourself the walls are whole. **The danger is not that you are breached — the data says almost everyone is — it is that you feel safe *because* you have stopped looking honestly, and this week's numbers show that feeling runs backwards: the surer the house, the likelier the moth is already in the wool.** The households that keep their harvest will do two unglamorous things. First, **stop grading yourselves and start measuring** — replace "are we confident?" with instrumented visibility into which agents run, what they can touch, and what they do, because confidence has just been shown to be an *inverse* indicator of safety. Second, **make the outside light the standard** — independent red-teaming, breach-and-near-miss telemetry, third-party audit, all governed and revocable at one point — and hold the mirror itself to account through the standards now being written. You learned yesterday that you cannot outrun the swarm; today, learn that you cannot flatter your way to being whole. Throw out the glass that only ever tells you you're safe, and buy the honest light that tells you where you're not.

## 2 · Allegory of the Day — "The Looking-Glass"

*Topic: This morning the frame turns from the threat outside the wall to the instrument you use to judge yourself inside it. AvePoint's "State of AI 2026" (Osterman Research; 750 global IT leaders) finds 88.4% of organisations suffered an AI-agent breach and 89.5% a GenAI breach in the past year (up from 75.1%), yet 82.7% feel confident they can prevent unauthorised access — and the more confident the firm, the more likely it had already been breached (PRNewswire). The blind spot is widening: organisations unable to see unsanctioned AI nearly tripled (6.3% → 17.6%), 21.1% cannot account for agent activity, 46.9% of staff use agents weekly, and a fresh report counts 17,800 unverified AI add-ons across 6.7M installs, some impersonating vendors and able to run code. The answer is to measure by an honest outside light — visibility, red-teaming, breach evidence, governed at one point (MCP EMA); tools are shipping (CrowdStrike Falcon Guardian). The looking-glass allegory is the radar's own illustration.*

There was once a great house famed above all its neighbours for how well it kept itself — or so the house believed, for in the hall by the door hung a tall and beautiful looking-glass, and every morning the master and his stewards would dress before it and ask the old question of the house: *"Are we sound? Are we safe? Is all in order?"* And the glass, being a fine and flattering glass, always answered the same: *you are the soundest, safest, best-kept house in the whole of the valley.* And the household would go out into its day warmed and certain, and would tell the neighbours, and would write in the great ledger of the house: *all is well, and we are confident it is so.* The confidence was not a lie they told; it was a thing they truly felt, every morning, looking into the glass. That was the trouble with it.

For the glass showed only the front of them, in a flattering light, at the one hour they chose to look. It did not show the back stairs where the door had been left off the latch, nor the cellar where the damp had got in, nor the wardrobe where — for a season now — the moth had been quietly at work in the good wool. It could not show these things, because a looking-glass shows you only what you already bring to stand before it, and only from the side you turn to it. And so a strange and terrible pattern set in, one the house never noticed because the glass could not report it: **the mornings the household felt *surest* were exactly the mornings it had stopped checking the latches and the linings at all** — why stoop to the cellar when the glass has just told you the house is the finest in the valley? The prouder the reflection, the longer since anyone had actually walked the back stairs with a candle. When at last the reckoning came — the ruined coats, the spoiled stores, the open door a stranger had strolled through in the night — the house was genuinely astonished, and said, as such houses always do, *"but we were so careful; we were so sure."* They had been sure. That was the whole of the problem. They had mistaken the feeling the glass gave them for a fact about the house.

The wise houses of the valley learned three hard things from watching that one, and paid attention where their neighbour had paid only compliments. **The first: confidence is not a measurement — and in a fast, cluttered house it may be worse than none.** A household that grades itself will always, gently, mark itself kind; and the kinder the mark, the less it looks. So the wise threw out the flattering glass, or at least stopped believing it, and hung in its place something colder and more useful: **an honest, outside light** — a lantern carried by someone who did not live in the house, into the cellar and up the back stairs and through the wardrobes, at hours the household did not choose, reporting not how fine the front looked but where the damp and the moth and the unlatched door actually were. **The second: you cannot keep what you cannot see, and the house had been going *blind* while feeling *fine.*** New rooms had been added faster than anyone could map them — a hundred little cupboards and hired hands nobody had a list of, some of them wearing the livery of trusted names but taking their orders from strangers over the wall — and the household's ability to *see* its own house had been falling for years while its confidence rose. So the wise made a true census of every room and every hand, sanctioned and unsanctioned alike, and lit the ones no one had thought to look in. **And the third: the only proof worth writing in the ledger is proof from outside the house.** Not *"we asked ourselves and we are confident,"* but *"a stranger with a lantern walked every stair last night, and here is what he found, and here is what we mended by morning."*

So the wise houses rebuilt the whole ritual of assurance around the honest light. **They stopped dressing before the glass and started walking the house with a candle** — continuous, unflattering, at hours they did not pick. They stopped believing that the front hall in morning light was the measure of the house, and learned that the measure was the cellar at midnight and the wardrobe no one had opened. They kept a true roll of every room and every hired hand — especially the ones that had crept in without being counted, and doubly the ones that wore a trusted name — and they held the maker of the glass to account, insisting that the mirrors sold in the valley be made to show the back stairs too, before the whole valley learned the same lesson the hard way. And they swallowed the truth their proud neighbour had choked on: that the warm certainty of a fine reflection is not evidence of anything except that you have chosen a flattering angle, and that the safest house in the valley is not the one most sure of itself but the one most willing to be shown, by a light it does not control, exactly where it is not yet whole.

**The moral, stated plainly:** the model is a rented stove, now nearly free; your knowledge is the larder; the gate is the one door you guard; the passport names each agent; the lingua franca is the tongue it speaks; the conductor keeps the ensemble in time; the ear hears whether it plays well; the repertoire is the music only your house can play; the census is the roll that counts them; the inspector asks you to produce the roll; the break-in is what the missing entries cost; the two mills are whether the motion grinds flour; the miller's toll is what the cheapest grinding charges; the apprentice's apprentice is what happens when the tools improve the tools; the herald's proclamation is that a declaration of arrival is not arrival; the keyring is knowing which key each agent holds; the locksmith is the tool that opens any lock and mends it; the tide table is building one quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is keeping the papers that trace the work; the errand is bounding what a clever servant can reach; the bell is ringing the alarm the moment your own servant slips; the locusts are why you close the gaps before the swarm and meet machine speed with machine speed — and **the looking-glass is the instrument you use to tell yourself the house is whole: a house that dresses before a flattering glass and asks itself "are we safe?" will always be told yes, and will grow surest exactly when it has stopped looking, so you throw out the glass, carry an honest outside light into the cellar and the back stairs at hours you do not choose, keep a true census of every room and hired hand you cannot yet see, and write in the ledger only what a stranger's lantern actually found — because confidence is not a measurement, and this season the proudest reflection sits in the house the moth has already reached.** A house that mistakes the warmth of its own reflection for the soundness of its walls has not been careful; it has been flattered.

**The question it forces:** *We learned yesterday that we cannot outrun the swarm, so we must be whole before it comes. This morning the harder question is the one under that one: how do we actually know whether we are whole — and do we know it because someone carried an honest light through our cellar last night, or because we dressed before the glass this morning and liked what it said? When we tell the board "we are confident we can prevent unauthorised access," are we reporting a measurement, or a feeling — and do we know that this season the feeling runs backwards, that the surest houses are the likeliest to have already been breached? Can we see, tonight, every agent running in our house — including the ones that crept in wearing a trusted name — or has our ability to see fallen while our confidence climbed? And the hardest one: are we willing to be shown, by a light we do not control, exactly where we are not yet whole — or would we rather keep the flattering glass, and be astonished, like every proud house before us, on the morning we find the moth was in the wool all along?*

## 4 · Technical Deep-Dive — From the Locusts to the Looking-Glass

Read the whole stack once more, but now from the seat of the executive who has to answer, this week, not "are we confident we're secure?" but "what is the *instrument* by which we know?" The gate admits the agent; the passport names it; the lingua franca lets it speak; the conductor coordinates it; the ear hears its errors; the repertoire is the data it performs; the census counts it; the inspector asks for the count; the break-in is what the missing entries cost; the two mills are whether the motion grinds flour; the miller's toll is what the cheapest grinding charges; the apprentice's apprentice is what happens when the tools improve the tools; the herald's proclamation is a claim about the age; the keyring is knowing which key each agent holds; the locksmith is the tool that opens any lock and mends it; the tide table is building one quay any ship can dock at; the provenance is keeping the papers that trace the work; the errand is bounding what an agent can reach; the bell is disclosing when your own agent slips; the locusts are why you be whole before the swarm and meet it at machine speed. **The looking-glass sits at the end of that chain as the discipline for the moment you have to *verify* all of it — and it turns on one engineering fact: self-assessed confidence is not a signal about your security posture, and this week's data shows it is an *inverse* one, so assurance migrates out of "we believe we're secure" and into "we can measure, from outside, that we are."** The point is blunt: **against an estate you cannot fully see, a confidence report is not weak evidence — it is anti-evidence.**

- **The event — the confidence–breach inversion.** **AvePoint's "State of AI 2026" (Osterman Research; 750 global IT leaders across financial services, healthcare, government) found 88.4% of organisations suffered at least one AI-agent-related breach and 89.5% a GenAI breach in the past twelve months (up from 75.1% in 2025), most commonly data leakage (50.1%) and manipulation by malicious or untrusted inputs (49.6%); 82.7% of leaders are confident they can prevent unauthorised access, yet nearly nine in ten of those confident firms were breached, and the more confident the firm the more likely it had already been breached — 62–72% of even high-confidence organisations logged AI-related unauthorised-access incidents (AvePoint / Osterman Research; PRNewswire, as reported).** Read architecturally, three things matter. First, the correlation runs the wrong way: confidence is not merely uninformative about safety, it tracks *inversely*, which means any assurance process that terminates in a confidence statement is optimising the wrong variable. Second, the mechanism is visibility, and it is degrading: **organisations unable to detect unsanctioned AI use nearly tripled (6.3% → 17.6%), 21.1% cannot account for unsanctioned agent activity, and 46.9% of employees use agents daily or weekly (AvePoint, as reported)** — adoption is outrunning observability. Third, the response is mostly reflexive rather than instrumented: **95.5% took at least one mitigating action, most commonly adding human-in-the-loop controls (AvePoint, as reported)** — a control that does nothing for the agents you cannot see.
- **The reckoning — you cannot assure what you cannot see.** **A fresh report this week counted 17,800 public AI add-ons across 6.7 million installations drawing instructions from unverified external sources, including "skills" impersonating Anthropic and OpenAI that can execute arbitrary code (as reported)** — the visibility gap the survey measures, made concrete and adversarial. Meanwhile capability and adoption climb: **Anthropic's Fable 5.1 topped the Real-SWE enterprise coding benchmark at 38.8% (vs GPT-6 Astra 33.8%, Gemini 3.8 Flash 31.2%), and Anthropic projected a second straight quarter of positive adjusted operating income (~$559M on $11.5B revenue) (as reported).** The engineering implication is decisive: **an assurance model built on self-report was tolerable only while estates were small and legible; at today's agent density, with shadow add-ons impersonating your own vendors, a confidence statement is a measurement of the observer's comfort, not the system's state.** The failure mode is precise — the surer the house *feels*, the less it is *looking*.
- **The answer — instrument the honest light.** **Make external, continuous measurement a first-class property. See everything: discover every agent (sanctioned and shadow), map its reach, and trace its actions end-to-end — this week CrowdStrike launched Falcon Guardian to discover known and shadow AI agents across Windows and macOS, trace prompts through tool calls to downstream system actions, and block agents not explicitly approved (as reported); pair it with cyber-AI defenders on the offence/defence loop (e.g. CrowdStrike SafeMind). Prove it from outside: make independent red-teaming, breach-and-near-miss telemetry and third-party audit the standard of proof, not internal confidence. Govern it at one point: MCP's 2026-07-28 specification made Enterprise-Managed Authorization stable — now adopted by Anthropic, Microsoft and Okta, with Client ID Metadata Documents — the single control surface at which every agent can be seen, authorised and revoked (Model Context Protocol / InfoQ, as reported). Hold the mirror-makers to account: Anthropic, OpenAI and Google DeepMind have been meeting since July to stand up an industry-led AI standards body (Dario Amodei driving, Sam Altman backing) — the bid to define an honest external measure before regulators or incidents define it (as reported).** This is the same seam the radar keeps returning to — *be able to account for what you run* — now aimed at the *instrument of assurance* itself.

The strategic core: **when confidence is inversely correlated with safety, competitive and regulatory survival migrates out of "how sure are we" and into "how do we measure, from outside, what we cannot feel."** The AvePoint numbers make the point concrete: the breached firms were not careless in the old sense — they were *confident*, and confidence was precisely the tell. The board translation: **your assurance is only as good as the light you are willing to be shown under; a report that ends in "we are confident" ends in the one variable the data says runs backwards.** The failure mode is exact: **an estate that grades its own security, cannot see its own agents, and mitigates by adding humans to loops it cannot observe will feel safest in the season it is most exposed — and will learn the extent of it, as 88.4% of these organisations did, from a breach rather than a measurement.**

### The flattering glass vs the honest light — the same estate, two very different readings

| Dressing before the glass | Measuring by an honest light |
|---|---|
| "Are we secure?" → the team says yes | "Show me": telemetry, red-team results, breach data |
| Confidence is the headline metric | Confidence is treated as anti-evidence until measured |
| We see the agents we deployed | We discover every agent — sanctioned *and* shadow |
| Assurance is internal self-attestation | Proof is external: independent audit and red-teaming |
| Mitigation = add a human to the loop | Mitigation = see the loop first, then bound and revoke it |
| Blind spot ignored (feels fine) | Blind spot measured, lit, and closing |

### Why "the looking-glass" is the discipline that matters now

For years the enterprise assurance ritual was *ask whether we're secure and record the answer.* **This week retires that ritual: when 82.7% feel confident and 88.4% are breached — and the more confident are the more breached — the confidence report is not a weak signal, it is a misleading one.** When self-assessment inverts, the discipline that separates a firm that keeps its estate from one that is quietly stripped is no longer the strength of its convictions but the *honesty of its instruments* — external, continuous, unflattering measurement. A firm that grades itself is defending a feeling; a firm that instruments its agents and invites an outside lantern is defending a fact.

### How it lands on legacy estates

Same seam this radar keeps returning to — **be able to account for what you run** — now aimed at the *instrument of assurance*. On a legacy estate the instinct is to ask the owning team whether their system is secure and trust the yes; in an agent world that instinct is the flattering glass, because the team can only report the rooms it can see, and the survey shows those rooms are a shrinking share of the house. ## 7 · So What — Three Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. **Stop grading yourselves — instrument the truth.** The breached firms were the confident ones; confidence was the tell. **This month, replace every "are we confident we're secure?" assurance step for AI with an instrumented reading: continuous discovery of every agent running (sanctioned and shadow), a map of what each can reach, and a trace of what each actually does — deploy agent-visibility tooling (e.g. CrowdStrike Falcon Guardian) where you have none.** Report one number to the board: the share of AI agents in your estate you can positively see and account for — versus your best estimate of the true total.
2. **Make outside evidence the standard of proof — and light the blind spot first.** The blind spot nearly tripled and is widest where usage is highest. **Stand up (or commission) independent red-teaming and third-party audit of your highest-risk AI systems, and start reporting breach-and-near-miss telemetry, not self-attestation; prioritise the shadow estate — unsanctioned tools and third-party "skills," especially any impersonating your own vendors.** Report the count of unsanctioned agents and add-ons you have discovered and the trend, and treat rising confidence with falling visibility as a board-level red flag.
3. **Govern the light at one point — and help write the honest measure.** Seeing the agent is half; authorising and revoking it is the other half. **Route every agent through one control point where you can see, authorise and revoke it (MCP Enterprise-Managed Authorization, now stable and adopted by Anthropic, Microsoft, Okta), and close the containment floor (60% cannot terminate a misbehaving agent).
