The Lingua Franca

Thursday, 27 August 2026 · Cross-sector edition · C-level + Engineering

Yesterday the radar issued the papers — a real identity for every agent, the passport that names the traveler at the door. But a named traveler who cannot make himself understood is no more useful than an anonymous one. Today it asks the question the passport leaves open: once you can name the agent, in what language does it speak? This week the market moved the answer out of any one vendor's vault. Google's Agent2Agent (A2A) protocol formally joined the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation, placing it under vendor-neutral governance alongside Anthropic's MCP. The two tongues an agent must speak — agent-to-agent and agent-to-tool — now live in one neutral home. And Gartner projects 150,000+ agents per Fortune 500 enterprise by 2028, with only 13% confident in their governance.

1 · Executive Summary

For a week this radar walked the value up the stack; today it names the layer that makes all of them interoperate: the common tongue. The shift is no longer "can an agent act?" or "who is the agent?" but "in what language does the agent speak, who controls that language, and can any vendor take it away from us?" The market's answer this week: agent interoperability as an open, vendor-neutral standard, placed beyond any single vendor's control.

1. The settlement — one neutral home for both tongues. Google's Agent2Agent (A2A) protocol formally joined the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation (AAIF), placing agent-to-agent standards under the same vendor-neutral governance as Anthropic's Model Context Protocol (MCP), which handles agent-to-tool/data. A2A reached v1.0 in April 2026, is used by 150+ organizations and is in enterprise production; AAIF has grown from <40 members at its December 2025 launch to more than 250. Jim Zemlin, Linux Foundation: "By joining the Linux Foundation, A2A is ensuring the long-term neutrality, collaboration and governance that will unlock the next era of agent-to-agent powered productivity."

2. The scale demands a shared language. Gartner: by 2028 the average Global Fortune 500 enterprise will run more than 150,000 AI agents (up from fewer than 15 in 2025); only 13% believe they have the right governance. SAP LeanIX: 98% of firms use or plan agents, but fewer than half can inventory them. N agents × M tools of bespoke integration does not survive those numbers — the open protocol is the only wiring that scales.

3. Identity moves into the standard. The MCP roadmap (22 Aug 2026) names agent identity and enterprise security a top priority — DPoP and Workload Identity Federation — folding yesterday's passport into the wire protocol itself. The EU AI Act (Article 50 transparency + Article 12 reconstructability logging, live since 2 Aug, fines up to €15M/3%) is easier to meet when agents speak an auditable, standardized language.

Bottom line: the passport named the traveler; the lingua franca is how that named traveler actually works — with your tools, your data, and every other agent. When an enterprise will soon run six figures of agents that must interoperate, the control point is not any one model or vendor but the standard they speak — and the decisive fact this week is that the two core standards now live under neutral governance, so building on them is no longer a bet on one company. Stop hand-wiring agents to private protocols: adopt A2A for agent-to-agent and MCP for agent-to-tool, insist your vendors speak them, and keep your architecture portable. When the model is rented, the knowledge is the moat, and the agent is named, the language they all speak is the one thing you want owned by everyone and controlled by no one.

2 · Allegory — The Lingua Franca

A great trading crossroads grew rich once each house began sending out tireless couriers — to carry messages between houses, to fetch from warehouses, to strike bargains at counters across the city. But every house raised its couriers to speak only its own house dialect, so at every doorway sat a hired interpreter, translating one dialect to another, one painstaking pairing at a time. When a house kept ten couriers and dealt with ten others it paid for a hundred interpreters — and the city was heading toward a day when a single great house would field not ten couriers but a hundred thousand, at which point the interpreters would outnumber the goods and no one could say which courier had spoken to whom, or in whose words.

For a while each house tried to win by making its dialect the one everyone learned. A merchant-prince would publish his tongue for free, coax the others into teaching it — then, once enough of the city depended on it, quietly change a word, charge for the dictionary, or bind the best phrases to his own warehouses. The crossroads that kept its footing did something wiser: it settled on two open tongues — one for couriers to speak to one another, one for couriers to speak to the warehouses and counters — and placed both dictionaries not in any prince's vault but in a neutral public library no single house could rule. The tongues stayed two and distinct, but shared one honest, unlockable home. But some houses kept a private fork and drifted out of understanding; some spoke the open tongue at the gate but wired the inner rooms with old glue; some left no record of who said what. A common tongue is only worth the neutrality that keeps it open, the discipline that keeps you speaking it everywhere, and the ledger that records what was said.

The question it forces: our systems are filling with agents that must talk to each other and to every tool we own — soon a hundred thousand. Are we raising them to speak the open, neutrally-governed lingua franca (A2A for agent-to-agent, MCP for agent-to-tool), so any vendor's agent can understand ours and no one can change the words against us — or are we hand-wiring each agent to a proprietary dialect and a bespoke interpreter, betting the whole estate on one prince's dictionary he can rewrite, ransom, or retire whenever it suits him?

3 · Technical — The Two Tongues, the Neutral Library, and the Register

The two tongues: A2A standardizes how independent agents discover, task and collaborate with one another across vendors; MCP standardizes how an agent connects to tools, data and applications. This week A2A formally joined the Linux Foundation's AAIF, the same neutral home as MCP — the specs are not merged or interchangeable, but share governance no single lab controls (A2A: v1.0 Apr 2026, 150+ orgs, major clouds, enterprise production; IBM's ACP merged into A2A in 2025). The neutral library: AAIF grew from <40 to 250+ members (Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Anthropic, OpenAI, Bloomberg, Block, Shopify); the value is a dictionary no vendor can rewrite, ransom or retire. The register: Gartner — 150,000+ agents / F500 by 2028, only 13% governed; SAP LeanIX — 98% use/plan agents, <50% can inventory them; the MCP roadmap (22 Aug) folds agent identity (DPoP, Workload Identity Federation) into the wire. A named agent that cannot speak a shared, open language is stranded; a shared language owned by one vendor is a lock-in you have not priced yet.

On legacy: integration debt is the estate's oldest tax (every system pair its own connector), and agents threaten to multiply it a hundred-thousand-fold. Standardize, neutralize, register: adopt A2A for agent-to-agent and MCP for agent-to-tool, and make "speaks the open protocols" a procurement requirement; prefer the neutrally-governed spec over a vendor's fork; wire standardized identity and Article-12 logging into every exchange. Keep the engine swappable behind it — the model is the commodity, the knowledge is the moat, the passport names the agent, and the open language they all speak is the freedom you keep.

5 · So What — 3 Moves for the Next 30 Days

1. Standardize — make "speaks A2A and MCP" a hard requirement. Bespoke, per-vendor integration is the tax that compounds with every new agent, and you're heading for six figures. Adopt A2A for agent-to-agent and MCP for agent-to-tool as your default wiring, and write "must speak the open agent protocols" into every agent procurement and build. Prove it on one cross-vendor workflow.

2. Neutralize — build on the neutrally-governed spec, not a vendor's fork. The story is governance, not the protocol: an "open" standard one vendor controls can be enclosed later. Prefer the Linux Foundation / AAIF-governed specifications, and treat vendor-neutral governance as a checklist item. Ask every vendor: if you changed your terms tomorrow, what would we rewire? If "a lot," you're on a leash, not a language.

3. Keep the register — wire identity and logging into every exchange. Interoperability without attribution is a control gap. Adopt standardized agent identity (DPoP / Workload Identity Federation) and Article-12-grade logging on every agent-to-agent and agent-to-tool exchange. Report two numbers to the board: the share of agents on open, neutrally-governed protocols, and the share of exchanges that leave a standardized, auditable record.
